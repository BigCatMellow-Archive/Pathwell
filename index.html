Pathwell

An urban fantasy novel about choice, determinism, and what it costs to matter.

Length~37,000 words (target)
GenreUrban fantasy
AudienceOlder young adults, accessible to adults
POVThird-person limited (Elizabeth)
VoiceConversational, irreverent — dry, precise, slightly absurdist. Treats the extraordinary as unremarkable.
ManuscriptsBCMWriter.txt (discovery draft) · Pathwell_Working.txt (active revision)

The Elevator Pitch

A passive woman who has stopped making choices gets dragged into a magical world by a charming ancient man who treats his own future like loose change. She discovers that the magic system runs on other people's emotions — but she's the only person who can use her own. The story asks whether agency comes from power or from choosing to act, and answers it when Elizabeth runs through a burning archive with nothing but her legs and her will.

The Thesis

Control is not competence. When you treat "future possibility" as leverage, you eventually destroy your ability to choose — and other people pay for your convenience.

Elizabeth matters without any power source. She proves this by running through the archive fire with no magic, no weapon, no leverage. If Elizabeth uses magic — including her own emotions as magic — the thesis collapses into empowerment fantasy.

Personal Origin

The author's experience of not feeling like he mattered until becoming a parent. The fear of living a life devoid of choices — not the wrong choices, but no choices at all. The realization that purpose comes from mattering to someone, including yourself.

How Themes Land

Through dramatic action, not speeches or realizations. Characters demonstrate the theme by what they do under pressure. No preaching. No moments of understanding. Behavior, consequence, change.

Thesis & Controlling Ideas

The argument the story makes, and how each character carries it.

The Controlling Idea

Control is not competence. When you treat "future possibility" as leverage, you eventually destroy your ability to choose — and other people pay for your convenience.

The Argument (as a sentence)

Freedom is preserved when a person chooses to act despite uncertainty, because the act of choosing — not the outcome — is what makes you human.

Every scene argues for or against this. Elizabeth's arc argues for it. Shade's existence argues against it. Pathwell sits in the middle, proving both — he chose freely once, and now he's losing the ability to choose. The climax settles the argument through action, not words.

The Novel's Thesis

Elizabeth matters without any power source. She proves this by running through the archive fire with no magic, no weapon, no leverage.

Critical Constraint
If Elizabeth uses magic — including her own emotions as magic — the thesis collapses into empowerment fantasy. The diary is a tether to her past self, not a weapon. It burns in the archive fire and she runs past it.

The Test

If a character says the theme out loud, you've failed. Theme emerges from the pattern of choices and consequences across the whole story. The reader discovers the theme. The writer doesn't announce it.

Starting Condition

She didn't care enough to be phased by a man breaking into her apartment. She had a routine and didn't notice the world around her. She isn't frozen by fear of choosing wrong — she's frozen by the belief that her choices don't change anything. She doesn't think she matters. She's suppressing the need to be significant.

Elizabeth and Magic

In a world where everyone uses OTHER people's emotions as power, Elizabeth is the only person who uses her own. Her diary — her own anger, fear, hurt — becomes her weapon. Everyone else borrows. She doesn't.

Thesis Constraint
Elizabeth matters without any power source. The diary is a tether to her past self, not a weapon. It burns in the archive fire and she runs past it.

Elizabeth CANNOT

Skip rungs on the agency ladder. Jump from passive to heroic. Have magic systems or fortune tellers reveal her true desires. Learn lessons through realization instead of action. Go back to comfort without losing her arc.

Relationships

Pathwell dragged her into this. Took her cookbook. Brought danger to her door. Every cost she pays traces back to his choices.

Shade cracks her faith in Pathwell with the diner accusation. She stays with him because at least he's honest about what she is to him.

Stansbury tries to keep her safe by limiting her involvement. She pushes back — she's tired of being managed.

Pathwell

Free → Constrained. The charming man who treats his future like loose change.

Core flawHubris/control. Believes he's smarter than the system.
Core wantTo stay in control and remain the competent protector/provider.
Lie"I can always trade a little more."
Truth"Some things, once given, don't come back."
ArcNegative/cautionary, unless he changes. Sharpened, not softened.

What He Is

Charming but hollow. Centuries of pragmatic choices have hollowed him out. He optimizes for survival and getting through, not for living fully. He IS the controlling idea: control is not competence. His need to manage everything destroys the things he's trying to protect.

What He's Been Doing

Running. For centuries. Selling his futures via Pruning for survival, getting more deterministic with each transaction, and refusing to face what that's cost him and the people around him.

What Breaks Him

Pruning cancels when he doubts his own control. He cannot tolerate the loss of leverage.

Voice

Irreverent, quick, charming. Uses humor as deflection. Occasionally drops into seriousness that reveals how ancient and tired he is. Mentions things from centuries ago casually. "I love this part." Snaps his fingers. Uses nicknames ("Lizzy"). Perceptive without being sentimental — the sigh and "Oh fine, you can have a dumpling" instead of "are you okay?"

Thematic Function

Every cost Elizabeth pays in this story traces back to Pathwell's choices. The archive fire that burns her grandmother's cookbook is HIS fault — his forced prune caused it. Other people pay for his convenience.

Arc Pacing Rule

His reckoning must arrive as accumulated consequence, not a lightbulb moment. The pacing is: denial → quiet retry → relief → admission.

Relationship to Shade

Shade is what Pathwell is becoming — his pruned futures given form, the lives he could have lived but sold away.

Shade

The endpoint. No arc — static, because he has no choices left.

What he isA manifested duplicate from a rare failed Pruning. Pathwell's pruned futures given form.
Dominant emotionContempt
Core wantTo expose Pathwell to himself. To force "It was my fault."

The Draw

A deterministic compulsion he can delay but can't stop forever. It manifests as a physical "tell" — an autopilot action or phrase — that eventually exposes him at Camp Cunnan.

Arc Endpoint

Eye contact with Pathwell. The draw stops. Relief. Happiness. He NEVER gets the confession. Most people who need an apology never get the words. Sometimes presence is enough. He dies/is absorbed without ideological victory.

The Diner Accusation

Must hinge on ONE sharp point, not a list:

"He saved you from a mess he brought to your door."

This lands because it confirms Elizabeth's deepest fear — she was never chosen, never special, just convenient.

Why Elizabeth Stays with Shade

At least Shade is being honest about what she is to him — a means to an end. She'd rather be honestly used than dishonestly valued.

Why Shade Reveals Himself

Pathwell pruning casually, almost for fun — contempt for Shade's existence, not pragmatic necessity. Casual pruning treats his existence as worthless, which forces him into the open.

Open Problems
Shade lacks a blind spot. He's currently too correct, which reduces his narrative function. He needs to be wrong about something.

Shade's emotional development is insufficient for his death scene to land. Needs work before the climax.

Stansbury

The pragmatist. Grounded competence with an overprotective instinct that masquerades as care.

FunctionAlternative model of protector. Foil to Pathwell.
Core flawProtects people by keeping them small.
AbilityCan imbue objects with emotional charge (Nerf weapons).
ArcOvercomes flaw in crisis — leads by trusting, not shielding.

The Flaw

The teacher who won't let students take risks because he's seen what happens when they do. His version of "protection" is control too — gentler and more socially acceptable than Pathwell's, but still control. Two flavors of the same flaw: recklessness vs. over-caution.

The Nerf Weapons

He wants to arm people but gives them toys instead of real weapons. That contradiction IS his character.

Key Beat

Notices the archive fire first. Snaps Pathwell out of his spiral. Takes over leadership — not by controlling, but by trusting people to act. His flaw, overcome in crisis.

Open Problems
Underdeveloped interiority and motivation beyond functional role. Needs "keeps people small" flaw established before the fire moment, or it isn't earned. Leadership arc must feel earned, not stumbled into.

Supporting Characters

Mama Baga

Elder of Camp Cunnan. Pathwell's adopted mother. The person Elizabeth heals with the cookbook. Must be established as a character the reader cares about BEFORE she needs healing — otherwise the sacrifice is abstract.

Open Problem
Mama Baga's relationship to Pathwell is an unresolved structural gap.

The Shopkeeper

Runs The Space Between. Practiced, dry, transactional. His introduction speech is a legal disclaimer he resents having to give. Cares about Pathwell underneath the professional distance.

Key line: "You don't have much left to give, Pathwell Shade."

Ending function: Offers the exchange that sets up the final scene. "Do this for me and you can have the book." Elizabeth offers the adventure. Pathwell isn't ready. "Perfect."

Boots

The cat. Blood god. Paid in mice.

Draws blood as entry price to the bookstore. Nobody explains what a blood god is or why a cat qualifies. Pure sequel energy. An orange tabby. Twelve pounds of paws and claws. The diminutive lord of slaughter.

Boots is the litmus test for whether the story is doing "write it like the sequel" correctly. If anyone explains Boots, something has gone wrong.

Magic System

Emotional-text magic — handwritten documents imbued with human feeling that wizards draw power from.

Core Rules

1Written text carries emotional "charge" from whoever wrote it.
2Using text transfers the emotion into the user.
3The transferred emotion can be shaped into magical effects.
4Using a page consumes it (user retains content briefly).
5Handwritten only. This rule is sacred.

Key Established Moments

Grandmother's cookbook → golden protective light, taste of sugar cookies, grandmother's voice. This is the benchmark scene for all worldbuilding.

Love note → wound closes, lingering feeling of a boy from middle school.

Diary (anger/fear/hurt) → destructive potential.

The Cardinal Rule

The strongest moments are sensory, not explanatory. Sugar cookies > classification charts.

The body knows first — goosebumps in the fear section, warmth near love, a flinch when magic costs something.

Imbuing

Stansbury can transfer emotional charge into objects (Nerf weapons). See his character page for what that contradiction means.

Organization

The Space Between organizes texts on a modified Dewey Decimal system by emotional spectrum and intensity.

Critical Revision Rule
The Dewey Decimal classification must NOT become a lecture. Elizabeth's body tells the reader what section she's in. This is the biggest violation of "write it like the sequel" in the discovery draft.

Pruning

The currency of the magical world. Characters sell "what may be's" — potential futures — for goods and services.

How It Works

Pruning is blind — the seller doesn't choose which futures get pruned. Each transaction narrows variance, reduces future agency, makes the seller more deterministic. It's the mechanism by which Pathwell has been running for centuries — and the mechanism that created Shade.

The Consent Rule (Non-Negotiable)

Pruning requires full internal consent/confidence. Doubt cancels the transaction. You cannot lie to yourself into consent.

This creates automatic drama: if it works, he meant it. If it cancels, he's lying to himself.

Interdimensional "cleanup" predators.

Locations

Locations are characters. If a conversation could happen anywhere, the location isn't working.

The Space Between

A bookstore that exists between spaces. Accessible through magic doors. Contains the emotional text archive organized by modified Dewey Decimal. The Shopkeeper runs it. Boots guards it. Terrible coffee. The air is cooler, denser, heavy with accumulated emotion.

Revision Rule
The threshold crossing is Elizabeth's first step into the magical world. It must be sensory, overwhelming, physical. NOT a tour. NOT a lecture.

Camp Cunnan

Refuge with emotionally saturated written archive — letters, journals, histories. A community. Mama Baga as elder. Becomes the site where the archive burns.

Elizabeth's danger here: It's comfortable. It could become her new routine. Comfort is her trap.

Elizabeth's Apartment

Opening location. Destroyed by blob attack in Chapter 1. Removes her safety net.

The Dumpling Stand

Chapter 2's deceleration location. Plastic tables. Site of Elizabeth's first choice (the lo mein). The quiet after the storm.

The Diner

Shade's accusation. Greasy spoon. Neon sign: "Dinner 24/7." Where Elizabeth's faith in Pathwell cracks.

The Museum

Elizabeth chooses who she goes with, and why. She rejects BOTH framings of her. The dramatic question of Act III resolves here.

Plot Structure

Therefore/but chain. Every scene causes the next.

Act I — Hook & Rules

Chapter 1

Pathwell breaks into Elizabeth's apartment BECAUSE he needs her cookbook. THEREFORE he uses it to fight the blob (sugar cookie protective spell). BUT the apartment is now marked and destroyed. She's stuck with this stranger.

Ends: "Ready?" / "No." / "Perfect."

Chapter 2

Elizabeth at the dumpling stand — not because she chose to follow, because she was dragged. No agency. Laundry ticket blows away (last piece of normality — RESPECT THIS). Deceleration. THEREFORE she makes her first choice: the lo mein. She stays.

Ends: "He still has it."

Chapter 3

Elizabeth follows BECAUSE he still has her cookbook and diary. Character reason, not logistics. Threshold crossing to The Space Between. Fainting. Boots. Love note healing. Awful coffee. Pathwell prunes to pay. She sees her books on the counter. She could take them and go home. Instead, she puts them on Pathwell's stack and carries them through the door. Chooses uncomfortable over comfortable.

Act II — Camp, Recruitment, Early Pressure

Camp Cunnan

Pathwell delivers books as contingency. Elizabeth meets Mama Baga. It starts feeling comfortable — dangerous.

Cookbook Sacrifice

Mama Baga needs healing. Elizabeth gives up the cookbook knowing it will be consumed. Permanent cost.

Stansbury Recruitment

Tense history. "Chance to kill me" joke. Stansbury comes but tries to keep Elizabeth small. She pushes back.

Elizabeth's Agency Proof

Bar scene. Blob. Absorbed. Cuts herself out with Nerf dagger. Proves she's not furniture. BUT draws blob attention to her.

Midpoint — Crash + Shade Separation

Pathwell and Stansbury drunk and arguing. Elizabeth sober and furious — she's furniture again. She grabs the wheel to prove she exists in the car. Agency with unintended consequence.

Shade casts protection at impact, takes Elizabeth. He's drawn to her BECAUSE she chose.

Act III — Shade's Framing + Museum

Diner Scene

"He saved you from a mess he brought to your door." One sharp point. Elizabeth stays with Shade — at least he's honest about what she is to him.

Museum Scene

Elizabeth chooses to go back to Pathwell — NOT because she forgives him. Because Shade's honesty is still manipulation. Truth as weapon, charm as shield. She rejects both framings. Pathwell overreaches. Elizabeth gets hurt as collateral.

Shade portals to Camp Cunnan. The draw compels him.

Act IV — Camp Pressure Cooker + Climax

Everyone at Camp. Shade heals Elizabeth (competent, unsettling). Camp trusts him. He's exposed by his draw tell.

Blobs arrive. Pathwell tries to prune — cancels. He forces it — tears. Archive ignites.

Elizabeth runs past her past to save children. Stansbury takes over leadership by trusting. Shade gets eye contact, the draw stops, relief without confession.

Final Stretch

Pathwell denies fault → private retry at Space Between fails quietly → relief → offhand admission ("It was my fault," thrown away) → Shopkeeper's exchange → "Are you ready?" / "No." / "Perfect." She leads. He follows.

Agency Ladder

Elizabeth's visible climb from passive to active. Each rung costs more than the last. No skipping.

Lo Mein (Chapter 2)
Chooses to stay with Pathwell. Costs nothing material. First choice she's made.
Books on the Stack, Through the Door (Chapter 3)
She came to get her cookbook back. Instead, she puts her books on Pathwell's stack and carries them into his world. Chooses uncomfortable over comfortable. Costs her meeting, her job, her routine. The dry cleaning ticket is already gone (blew away in Ch2 — respect that moment).
Cookbook to Heal Mama Baga (Camp Cunnan)
An elder she can look in the eye needs healing. The cookbook is full of love — the strongest healing magic. She gives it up knowing it will be consumed. Costs her grandmother's love, the sugar cookies, the golden light. Permanent.
Uses Her Own Diary as a Weapon
Instead of handing the diary to Pathwell, she uses it herself. Feels every moment she ever felt invisible — every swallowed anger, every fear. Her OWN emotions become power. The only character who doesn't borrow someone else's feelings.
Runs Past the Burning Cookbook (Archive Fire)
Pathwell's forced prune ignites the archive. Whatever remained is burning. Elizabeth runs past her past to save children. Not a realization — an action. No magic, no weapon, no leverage. This is where the thesis lives.
"Are You Ready?" (Ending)
She initiates the next adventure. She's ready before Pathwell is. The roles have flipped. The agency ladder is complete.

Rules for the Ladder

Every rung must be a choice, not a reaction. Responding well to pressure is growth, but initiating action is real agency.

Each step must cost slightly more than the last.

There's a ceiling on sacrifice. At some point, giving things away stops being a higher rung. The next level is INITIATING rather than RESPONDING.

Feelings are fuel, not engines. Excitement, curiosity, and comfort are not choices. Agency requires action.

If the protagonist is being dragged, the option is wrong.

Writing Principles

The 20 guiding principles. Read before you write. Read after you write.

01
Write the Sequel
The sequel assumes you were there. Characters don't explain gravity. The reader catches up by watching. If someone who lives here wouldn't say it — cut it.
02
Therefore, But — Never And Then
Every scene must cause the next. "Therefore" = Scene A forces B. "But" = A is complicated by a new problem. If the connection is "and then," fix it.
03
Every Scene Turns a Value
Something is at stake. By the bottom, that value must have changed charge. If the end matches the beginning, nothing happened.
04
Show the Iceberg's Tip
Build everything. Show one-eighth. A locked door they never open. A name dropped and never explained. The world feels deep because you chose to show almost none of it.
05
The Body Knows First
Before intellectual understanding, the body reacts. Goosebumps. Warmth. A flinch. Sensation before explanation. Always.
06
Characters Want Things
Every character in every scene wants something. When two wants conflict, you have tension. If a character is just present — give them a want or get them off the page.
07
Agency Is a Ladder
The climb must be visible. Small choice → bigger → consequences → changes the story. No skipping rungs.
08
The Lie and the Truth
Every major character believes something false at the start. The story tests that belief. The climax breaks it or confirms it. Every scene should pressure someone's Lie.
09
Don't Explain the Metaphor Before Performing It
The demonstration IS the explanation. Trust the image. Trust the reader. Cut every pre-lecture that precedes a dramatic demonstration.
10
Incomplete Answers Build Worlds
The default answer should be partial, evasive, contradictory, or silence. Complete answers close doors. "You shouldn't have been able to do that" > a full explanation.
11
Breathe Between Punches
Fast scenes need slow scenes after them. If every chapter runs at the same speed, no chapter feels fast. This is where they fall in love with your characters.
12
Locations Are Characters
A place that could be swapped for any other isn't earning its page space. Make the magic shape the environment. Make the place speak.
13
Subtext Over Text
What characters don't say matters more. Give every character a secret agenda. The gap between said and meant is where tension lives.
14
Promise, Progress, Payoff
Opening promises an experience. Middle must feel like progress toward it. Ending pays it off. If the middle sags, the reader lost track of the promise.
15
The Antagonist Is a Mirror
The best villain is a dark reflection. Shade is Pathwell if Pathwell doesn't stop. The antagonist represents the protagonist's worst possible future.
16
Theme Through Action, Not Speeches
If a character says the theme out loud, you've failed. Let characters disagree through behavior. The reader discovers the theme.
17
Rules Are Sacred
Once the magic system establishes a rule, never break it. Every violation trains the reader to stop trusting you.
18
One Unexplained Thing Per Chapter
A name. A place. A scar. A locked room. One sentence each, and they imply centuries.
19
Earn the Big Moments
Big moments are the payoff of small investments. If a moment isn't landing, the problem is almost never the moment — it's the setup.
20
Read It Aloud
If it sounds wrong in your mouth, it reads wrong on the page. Short sentences punch. Long sentences flow. Your ear knows before your brain does.

Quick Diagnostic

Run these five questions on every chapter before moving to the next one.

1. What does Elizabeth want in this scene?

If the answer is "nothing" or "to understand what's happening," she's passive. Give her a want.

2. What word connects this chapter to the next — "and then," "therefore," or "but"?

If it's "and then," fix the transition.

3. What value changed from the top to the bottom?

If nothing changed, this is a non-event. Find the turn or cut the scene.

4. Did anyone explain something the story already showed (or is about to show)?

If yes, cut the explanation.

5. Is there at least one thing in this chapter that never gets explained?

If everything is accounted for, add a mystery. One sentence. One unexplained detail. That's depth.

Structural Risks

Known failure modes to monitor throughout revision.

1. Too Many Magic "Explosions"

Camp fire must be unique. Museum is internal overload, not outward blast.

2. Shade Turning into a Lecture Machine

Diner scene hinges on ONE accusation. Everything else implied.

3. Elizabeth Losing Agency in Act III

She must make at least one meaningful choice while with Shade. She cannot just be transported.

4. Pruning Rules Getting Fuzzy

Consent must be consistent. If consent was already fuzzy, the climactic cancel/force drama evaporates.

5. Camp Loss Feeling Abstract

Name specific artifacts early; burn them later.

6. Pathwell's Shift Too Fast

Pacing: denial → quiet retry → relief → admission. No lightbulb moment.

7. Stansbury Being Too Functional

"Keeps people small" flaw must be established before the fire moment.

8. Dewey Decimal Lectures

Elizabeth's body tells the reader. Not Pathwell pointing at shelves.

9. Comfort as Elizabeth's Trap

Every time she settles in somewhere: is she becoming a passenger again?

10. Diner Articulating Theme Too Cleanly

Characters speaking the theme directly. Flagged for future attention.

11. Architectural Symmetry Feeling Designed

At full-draft stage, monitor that the story feels lived rather than schematic.

Key Discoveries

Insights earned through revision work. Things the story taught us.

About Craft

Scene connections come from character wants, not plot logistics. Test every option: who's choosing and who's being dragged? Important objects leave hands through pressure, not transactions. Agency is a ladder — can't skip rungs. There's a ceiling on sacrifice: at some point, the next level is INITIATING rather than RESPONDING. Feelings are fuel, not engines. Comedy requires breathing room. Don't build plot devices to do character's emotional work. Respect what's on the page.

About Elizabeth

She uses her OWN emotions as magic — the only character who does. The diary's magical cost (reliving anger, fear, hurt) IS the character moment. She can't go back to comfort without losing her arc. Her choices must escalate from reactions to initiations.

About Pathwell

His arc is about the loss of leverage, not the gain of wisdom. He doesn't learn to be good — he loses the ability to cheat. The private retry at Space Between is the most important quiet moment. He tries to prune, it doesn't work, and he's relieved. That's the crack. His admission must be OFFHAND.

About the Magic System

The strongest moments are sensory, not explanatory. Sugar cookies > classification charts. The consent rule for pruning creates automatic drama. Elizabeth using her own diary breaks the system's pattern — and that violation IS the thematic statement.

About Tone

A dry, precise, slightly absurdist voice — treating the extraordinary as unremarkable. This is consistent with what already works in the manuscript. It's about recognizing what the story already is, not changing it.

Open Questions

Unresolved structural problems and gaps that need solving.

Active Structural Gaps

The Diary's Placement

The diary's role in the narrative remains floating. It functions as a tether to Elizabeth's past self (not a weapon), and it burns in the archive fire — she runs past it. But its exact placement and how it moves through the story needs consistent execution.

Shade's Blind Spot

Shade currently lacks a blind spot, making him too correct and reducing his narrative function. He needs to be wrong about something.

Act III Blank (Diner → Museum)

Structural blank between the diner and museum. Elizabeth must make at least one meaningful choice while with Shade.
