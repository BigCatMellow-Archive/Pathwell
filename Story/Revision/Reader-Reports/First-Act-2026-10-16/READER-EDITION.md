# Pathwell — first-act reader edition

This is a reader-only edition of **Chapters 1–3**. Read in order, without looking up the author's notes, later chapters or any editorial reports. You do not have to enjoy the story: confusion, boredom, doubts and stopping points are useful feedback. If you already know the story or have read earlier versions, please say so.

After **each chapter**, before continuing, write a few sentences privately about: (a) what happened, (b) what stood out, (c) what confused you or required rereading, (d) what you now want to know, and (e) whether you'd voluntarily continue reading. **Do not revise an earlier chapter's notes after reading a later one.**

After Chapter 3, use the separate [neutral response questions](READER-PACKET.md), or simply send the three untouched chapter notes plus your overall first-act impressions to the person who invited you. Please don't read other people's opinions or discussion threads before submitting yours. No spoiler guide or answer key is included here.

## Chapter One

It was the crash that woke her.

Stepping out into the living room she found a man going through her boxes. One stack had gone over, and her winter sweaters were all over the rug.

The party was still out here. Paper cups on every flat surface, a tray of cheese cubes going shiny, and half a sheet cake that now said only ELIZ, because her coworkers had started at the wrong end. A WELCOME ELIZABETH banner hung by one corner over the kitchen doorway. It had been a good party. Better than she'd expected for someone so new to the building that she still couldn't find the light switch in the hall. The guests had left hours ago, and she'd gone straight to bed. She had a meeting at nine, her first big one since she'd started.

She knew him, a little. He'd been at the party, talking to everybody. She'd assumed someone from work had brought him. She'd seen him peering into an open box of old notebooks once, before somebody asked for more cake. He'd laughed at her joke about the landlord, which was more than anyone from work had managed. She'd never caught his name. She'd thought he'd left with the rest.

He sat on the floor with a box open beside him, reading her diary like a magazine in a waiting room.

She watched for a moment before interrupting. "Do you mind?"

"Not at all," he said, and turned a page.

A thud hit the front door. Wet. Heavy.

He looked up at the door. Then at her.

"Friend of yours?"

"What?"

She went to the door. Someone might have fallen in the hall. She turned the lock and opened it against the chain.

Something wet and dark sagged into the gap. It shifted against the jamb, leaving a smear that ran toward the floor.

The man came across the room with her diary under his arm. He slammed the door shut hard enough to rattle the chain and threw the bolt.

He lifted his head and sniffed. Under the cheese and the cake there was something else, faint and wrong. Low tide, a long way from any sea.

His face changed.

The door bowed in its frame and settled back.

He went straight back to the boxes, the diary still under his arm.

"Where's your cookbook?" he said without looking up. He shoved a box aside. "The old one."

"Excuse me?"

"We need it."

Another thud, harder.

"Now."

"They're… in a box in the kitchen?"

He blew past her, and she heard tape rip in the kitchen before he came back with the book in hand.

Nana's cookbook, three rubber bands around the spine and the title in Nana's blue marker.

"Don't think. What's your favorite recipe?"

"I—"

"Don't think."

The door creaked and strained.

"My Nana always made the best sugar cookies?"

"Perfect."

Flipping through worn and stained pages, he found a tagged recipe. 'Nana Jones Sugar Cookies'.

He placed his hand flat on the page.

Gold ran along the ink in little starts and stops, even through the crooked places where Nana's pen had hesitated.

Elizabeth leaned close enough to see the ink still there beneath it.

The door began to crack and splinter. Ooze searched through the gaps, and the stench of low tide turned her stomach.

The glow from the letters now consumed the page, a pool of light forming under his hand.

Elizabeth grabbed the cookbook by the spine and pulled it from his hands. The sugar-cookie page stayed under his palm.

"What are you doing!?"

Everything went white, and the scent hit her; buttery and sweet. Then a taste… and something else. A voice that couldn't be here.

She was twelve again, baking cookies with her grandmother. Sun shining through an open window above the sink, the fresh smell of rain filled her lungs. Her grandmother smiled at her, and Elizabeth felt something she hadn't felt in years.

Her body moved back before she did, sending her stumbling into the wall. The cookbook fell out of her hands.

The light under his palm had gone wrong. It spat and flickered, and loose threads of gold dropped onto the carpet.

Another wet thud and the door crumbled beneath the behemoth's weight. Now she could see the whole thing: some sort of blob, pushing its way through the door frame, taking pieces with it.

The blob rolled over the fallen threads of gold. Their light went out.

It darted for the light in the man's hand. He raised the glowing page between them. The blob struck it, and white light burst out.

The impact sang.

Recoiling, the blob rippled, the stench of wet-rot permeating the room.

Elizabeth ran for the bathroom, her stomach fighting to keep its contents to itself.

Lunging again, the blob knocked the man from his feet and into a wall.

Elizabeth could only see it looming over him as she slammed the door shut. Sounds of struggles, pounding, and kicking. A loud wet smack, and a thud against the wall.

Then nothing.

Elizabeth pressed her ear against the door.

There was only her racing heart and stillness, a stillness interrupted by a dull rumbling. A rumbling that grew, shaking the walls around her.

Light streaked in from under the door, and then a pop.

She waited. Hushed, pressed against the door.

"You can come out now. It's… mostly gone."

She cracked the door. The living room had exploded, covered in bits of rotting gelatinous ooze. The banner had come down into it.

The cake, somehow, was fine.

There in the middle of it all stood her last guest, holding Nana's cookbook and her diary.

"What. What just happened?" she asked from behind the door.

He glanced around. "That? That was nothing. Just cleaning up a mess."

Then he looked at her more carefully.

"You really don't know?"

"Know what?"

"Huh."

She opened the door slowly, peeking out.

"Right, so…" He shifted the diary under his arm. "We should probably get going."

He was already moving towards the empty doorway, stepping over and around debris. Sticking his head out, he checked up and down the hall. Then he went still.

When he turned back, he was sure of himself again.

He nodded at the ooze drying on her walls.

"Your house has now been marked, magically speaking. So we should probably be anywhere but here."

"I'm not going anywhere until you tell me what is going on."

"That confusion is quite normal. Do you have shoes?"

"Yes, but—"

"Good. Now here's the thing. We really do need to get going. So please. Grab your things."

She looked at the cookbook in his hand. A ragged edge stuck out where the page had been.

"Why could I hear her voice?"

"What?"

"Why could I hear my Nana?"

He stopped. For a second he wasn't in a hurry at all.

"Because it was hers," he said. Then, "Again, understandable reaction. Reasonable, even. Unfortunately, we do really need to be going."

"I already told you I'm not—"

"Yes, I did mention the marked thing, didn't I?"

She stepped out of the bathroom. Her stomach dropped as the wave of rot hit her again.

His attention shifted down the hall. "Shoes. Now. I did say please."

A wet thud hit from down the hall.

Her heart skipped and without thinking, she jumped into her shoes, and she threw on a coat.

With a smile he grabbed her by the hand.

"Ready?"

"No."

"Perfect."

## Chapter Two

"Will you slow down!?" Elizabeth struggled to keep up with him as he charged down the stairs. "What about all my stuff?"

"Replaceable."

They ran through the lobby, bursting through the front doors.

The street air was cold, shocking her breath.

It took her a moment to find it again, the breaths coming shallow and rapid.

Looking up she could see her apartment from the street, the light still on. She'd been there for just three weeks. Three weeks. Twenty-one days of unpacked boxes and takeout trying to figure out what came next. Her feet almost turned back. The only thing she'd bothered to unpack, her grandmother's mixing spoon, was still sitting on the kitchen counter.

And now this. Whatever this was.

"You okay?"

"No," she snapped.

"Fair."

She stopped walking. "Where are you taking me?"

"There's a lovely Chinese spot just around the corner here."

"I have a meeting at nine tomorrow."

"I swear it's really just around the corner."

"My landlord is going to kill me. How am I going to explain the door? I have to get my dry cleaning before the meeting. Where am I going to get a new door? I don't have my wallet. Do you think someone's going to steal my stuff? Should I call the cops?"

He nodded politely, waiting for her to finish, his eyes on the streetlight above them.

"This is all a dream. Tell me it's all a dream."

He pinched her arm.

She slapped his hand away. "Why would you do that?"

"It's not a dream. Just a really weird Tuesday for you."

"It's Thursday."

"Which makes it all the weirder." He kept walking, leaving her standing there rubbing her arm.

She looked back one more time at her apartment window. A shadow filled the space.

She hurried to catch him at the corner.

She turned left, toward the coffee shop she passed every morning. Except there was no coffee shop.

Just a shuttered laundromat she'd never seen before.

Beyond that, a park that shouldn't have been there.

Three weeks in this neighborhood and she didn't recognize a single thing.

"So then what will it be? You more of a lo mein girl? I'm all about the dumplings, the dumplings are perfect here."

"You just destroyed my home, and now you're hungry?"

"I wouldn't say hungry, I'm more peckish. And for the record, I saved you. Most people are thankful when someone does that."

"Thankful!?"

The shop was open to the street, a neon red dragon flickering above it, a beacon to all late night cravings.

"Two orders of fried dumplings, please."

"I'm not hungry."

"Oh, these aren't for you… and I don't share."

"Then why am I here?"

"Company?"

Elizabeth took one of the plastic tables set out at the curb while he waited at the counter. She let her weight hit the chair and slouched forward on the table. The cold forced her hands into her pockets, her fingers toying with the coins in them. A piece of paper brushed up against her hand.

The ticket.

Her dry-cleaning ticket was in her pocket. She could still have a clean outfit tomorrow.

She could still make it.

She dumped the contents of her pockets on the table, the bright yellow ticket landing on top. As she reached for it, a truck roared past close enough to rattle the table. The ticket skipped off the edge, caught the gutter, and was gone.

"Okay, I can just… I can…"

Her breath caught. Her stomach turned, and the smell hit her again. Wet. Rot. Clinging to the inside of her coat. She pressed her hands flat on the table and her mouth opened one more time, but nothing came out.

She wasn't sure when the tears started, only that she couldn't stop them. Three weeks of bare walls and boxes she couldn't bring herself to open. A life she'd packed into garbage bags and moved across the city because staying felt worse than leaving, but leaving didn't feel like anything at all.

She put her face in her hands and just let it happen.

He sat down brimming with anticipation and dropped her books on the table beside the tray before noticing Elizabeth. He took one look at her and sighed. "Oh, fine, you can have a dumpling."

Elizabeth stared at the table as he placed a dumpling in front of her.

The neon dragon flickered against the night, as Elizabeth sat staring down at a single dumpling, wiping traces of tears. The sounds of the night took over: a distant siren, a taxi splashing through a puddle, a group talking and laughing as they passed. The city was in motion even now.

He inhaled his second one, and nudged her slightly. "You gonna eat that?"

"No."

"I'm tellin' ya, Lizzy, you don't know what you're missing here," he said, snatching up the rogue dumpling with his fork.

"I've had dumplings before."

"Ah, but not these dumplings."

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand.

"Who the hell are you?"

He looked up.

"Pathwell."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"First name?"

"Pathwell."

"Last name?"

"Don't need one. Saves everybody time." He speared another dumpling. "So, where to next?"

"Excuse me?"

"The night is young, Lizzy! Where do you want to go?"

"Home."

Pathwell chuckled softly.

Her eyes snapped to him. "You wrecked my house. There's pieces of some… thing, all over the place. You won't let me go home. I have a meeting that I cannot miss in the morning. Nothing to wear to it. And all you can think to do is get some crappy food and ask me 'what's next'?"

"First off, I wouldn't go back there tonight. But that's just me." He popped another dumpling into his mouth. "Secondly," he said through a half-chewed mouthful, "these things are perfect. Perfectly crappy. They aren't trying to be anything they're not. You have no idea how hard it is to find that kind of quality."

He stood up, brushing his hands off and gathering up his things. "That being said, it's up to you what you want to do next. The choice is yours, Lizzy."

He left the last dumpling on her side of the tray.

She watched him walk off, leaving her in the red neon glow.

She sat there for a moment, staring at the empty night in front of her where he had been.

She heard her again.

Lizzy.

Then a warmth, the taste of butter and sugar, the heat of an oven she hadn't stood in front of since she was twelve.

Her eyes swept the table, slowly at first. Then darting. She jumped to her feet.

He still has it.

## Chapter Three

She found him staring up at a streetlight, Nana's cookbook tucked under one arm and her diary under the other.

"So then," he said, without looking down from the light, "where to?"

"I'm not going anywhere without getting some questions answered."

"Sounds good, Lizzy."

"Don't call me that."

"Isn't that what your grandmother called you?"

"That's… that's not an answer. And don't think for a second I forgot you still have her cookbook."

"You'll get them back at the counter."

"What counter?"

"Lizzy, I can answer all your questions, even the ones you'll have next. We just need to swing by a bookstore first."

"What kind of bookstore is going to be open this late?"

Pathwell turned. "The right kind."

He shifted the diary and cookbook under one arm, pulled a folded piece of paper from his pocket with the other, and pressed it flat against the concrete wall behind her.

Light bled through the creases, spiderwebbing along the wall. A soft square, no bigger than the paper itself, glowing against the grey. He peeled back a layer and the square doubled. Another layer, and it doubled again. The light stretched past the edges of the paper until a doorway stood where the wall had been, warm and humming faintly.

"What is that?"

"Like I said, a bookstore. We really do need to work on those listening ears, Lizzy. Now, don't be shy. They have the most amazing coffee."

"How do I know this isn't some kind of trap?"

"You don't."

Pathwell stepped through and turned left.

She stood on the sidewalk, staring at a door that shouldn't exist. At rows of books stacked floor to ceiling. From somewhere inside, a loose page drifted out, spinning once in the air before settling at her feet. The handwriting was faded, but the ink smelled like lavender.

Then the smell of the place reached her. Old paper and dried leather, binding glue gone brittle, and beneath it all, dust that had been settling longer than she'd been alive.

He still had her books.

She stepped through.

The street noise cut out like someone had closed a window. No cars, no sirens, no wind. Just the soft creak of shelves bearing more weight than they were built for, and a silence that felt like it had been here practicing for years.

Books. Floor to ceiling, wall to wall, stacked and leaning and wedged into every surface the room could offer. Not books — lives. Journals with cracked spines, letters bundled in ribbon, recipes on index cards spotted with oil. Thousands of them, maybe more, pressed together so tightly the shelves bowed under the effort.

"Lizzy, would you mind closing the door?"

She turned. Pathwell was already halfway down an aisle, moving like he knew the place by feel. She pulled the door shut and the frame dissolved into the wall behind her. Just shelves now. Shelves in every direction.

"Pathwell?"

He was gone.

"Pathwell!"

A shush from somewhere deep between the rows.

She moved forward because standing still felt worse. The first aisle was fine — dusty, cramped, unremarkable. But as she turned a corner, her chest tightened. Not fear exactly. Something heavier, pressing against the inside of her ribs like a fist she couldn't unclench. Her eyes burned. She blinked hard and kept walking.

It passed. Two steps later her skin flushed warm and her pulse dropped and she felt, for no reason at all, like she was about to cry over something she couldn't name. She stopped and reached toward the nearest shelf. The spine of a journal was warm to the touch.

She pulled her hand back. None of that had been hers, and she held on to that.

Then something was. The next turn hit her like a door slamming. Her heart punched against the base of her chest, her mind catching on a single thought and looping it — where am I, where am I, where am I — her fingers going numb at the tips, her lungs reaching for a breath that wouldn't come. She braced herself against a shelf and the books there seemed to lean toward her, pressing closer, the aisle narrowing with each exhale she couldn't quite finish.

Grey crept into the edges of her vision. The last thing she registered was the carpet against her knees.

When her eyes opened, they were heavy. She was in a chair, her feet up on an ottoman. A thin orange cat sat in front of her, green-eyed and waiting.

A cup of coffee swung into view, with Pathwell behind it. "Just in time." A plain white mug on a plain white saucer.

She took it by instinct and sipped. It was awful. Reused filters, dirty pots, dollar-store beans. It tasted like late nights spent studying. Like the past.

"That's terrible."

"I know."

"Why does it help?"

"Familiar things are useful."

The room came into focus. A large oaken counter, a round man in round-rimmed glasses behind it, and dim lights that weren't trying hard enough. Nana's cookbook and her diary sat on the counter beside a ledger.

The cat rubbed its head against her shins. She reached down and the skin on the back of her hand split open. Fast, surgical, a single claw dragged through the soft part between her thumb and wrist. She yanked back. Blood welled up in a thin line.

The cat's eyes gleamed as it licked its chops.

"He wanted his tribute," the man behind the counter said. "I would let him have it, personally."

Her eyes darted back and forth, looking for the punchline.

"Hey, it's okay," Pathwell said. "You're fine."

He snapped a strip of paper from his pocket and laid it across the cut. Gripping it firmly, he whispered a few words. The paper glowed warm and faded into her hand. The wound and the pain went with it, leaving behind a lingering feeling that reminded her of a boy she knew in middle school.

"Love note," he said, and smiled. "Cures most wounds."

He rubbed his thumb over the thin line it had left behind. "Unfortunately, scars are outside my wheelhouse. How about this. You finish that coffee there, and I'll answer any questions. Deal?"

She gulped the coffee down.

"I'll take that as a yes." The chair beside hers huffed as Pathwell flopped into it. "Right, so you're wondering where we are. The truth is, we're in a bookstore, commonly referred to as the Space Between. As for Boots there, he did indeed sample the blood from your cut. He's the full-time guardian and part-time blood god."

Boots had already jumped onto the counter. The shopkeeper rubbed him behind the ears. "We pay him in mice."

"A blood god?"

"Neither here nor there. What's important is where we're going."

"Where are we going?"

Pathwell clapped his hands. "Glad you asked."

He got up and went to the counter. Elizabeth put the mug down and followed him. She reached for Nana's cookbook.

The shopkeeper put one finger on it.

"His transaction first."

She looked at Pathwell. "You said I'd get them back at the counter."

"And look," Pathwell said. "A counter."

"That's not the same thing."

"It's alarmingly close."

The shopkeeper looked at Pathwell over his glasses. "Yours?"

"No."

"Good." He didn't move his finger. "After settlement. He brought outside property into an open transaction, and I dislike paperwork."

He went back to his ledger, making ticks and carrying ones.

"So what do we owe?" Pathwell said.

Without looking up, the shopkeeper sighed. "You already owe quite a bit."

"I'm good for it."

"All bills come due eventually, Mr. Pathwell."

"What do I owe for today?"

The shopkeeper set the order on the counter and looked it over. Journals, bundles of letters, sheets of poetry, a few pages of music, blank archival paper, and a wooden crate tied with twine. Enough of it that the counter complained.

"Some of these are very old, Mr. Pathwell. They won't come cheap. Did Camp Cunnan ask you for all of this?"

Pathwell became interested in the crate. "They need it."

"That wasn't what I asked."

Pathwell didn't answer.

The register dinged and clacked, numbers jumping. "We could settle today, past bills and all, for… a Rai Stone."

"Oh, come on. Be serious. A Rai Stone?"

"I'm afraid I am." The shopkeeper removed his glasses. "And quite frankly, Mr. Pathwell, you already owe more than I allow most."

"Surely we can barter here."

"Outside of a significant Rai Stone, I could do it for, say, thirty Lydian coins."

"Okay, now you're just messing with me."

The shopkeeper was unmoved.

"Aren't you?"

"Unless you have something else to barter with…"

"I've got potential to sell. Lots."

"You did."

Pathwell chuckled nervously.

Elizabeth looked between them. "What does that mean?"

Neither of them answered her.

"Are you sure?" the shopkeeper said.

"Can we do this before I change my mind?"

The shopkeeper turned to the wall behind him and slid open a panel that had blended into the soft brown paneling. Behind it sat a cast-iron cube. He pulled a short sheet of paper from his sleeve and laid it over the door. His hands moved over it, turning it light blue. His fingers danced. A thunk, a clank, and shifting gears.

A door popped open from nothing.

Inside sat a leather-bound tome, its edges gilded in silver. A dot was branded into the center of the cover, surrounded by concentric rings and lattice arcs. Thick, heavy and dense, with thin pages.

He lifted it with both hands and placed it on the counter with reverence.

"Mr. Pathwell, are you sure this is worth what it will cost?"

"I am."

"Then say the words."

Pathwell stepped to the book and placed his right hand on the cover, his left across his chest. "I understand the cost of my needs."

The silver pages glowed a beautiful blue, like a distant sky.

"I give what may be, to protect what will."

The pages burned bright. Roots of light shot from the book and grasped Pathwell's forearm, wrapping tight, intertwined and wild.

"Perdat quod esse poterat."

They pulsed, turning a violent blue. New roots split off, snaking up his arm and digging their way in. Splintering thinner, then burrowing, twisting, wriggling. Taking hold.

From the center of the book, fibrous tendrils wove upward, gnarled and knotted. The tree grew to Pathwell's chest before splitting and branching out. Some branches grew mangled, withered, dead. The tree bloomed in patches, sick and breathless, and took its full shape, sitting sickly and worn.

The roots pushed deeper into his arm. Whatever the pain was, it had all of him now.

"You don't have much left to give, Mr. Pathwell."

"Take what you will, and spare me the lesson."

Orange flames took hold of the branches, spreading out from around the tree and burning bright against the light blue leaves. The roots burned a frightful orange, searing the arm they wrapped.

Pathwell screamed in a million voices, voices lost to time, burning out to become memories never made. One by one they fell quiet, until all that was left was a harmony of extinguished cries.

The fire burned down, taking all but a few living branches at the core and leaving only scorched fibers reaching out. Pathwell dropped to his knees, his arm still held up by the burrowed roots.

Then the roots let go, and he caught the counter with one hand. Elizabeth was halfway around it before she knew she'd moved.

"Thank you, Mr. Pathwell." The shopkeeper was already writing. "Your bill has been paid in full. Will that be paper or plastic?"

Pathwell looked up. His right arm was shaking, but there wasn't a mark on it.

"Surprise me."

He tucked the arm against his side and pulled himself upright, as though posture had become the problem.

The shopkeeper slid Nana's cookbook and the diary across the counter. "These were never his to spend."

Elizabeth took them. The diary was cool under her hand. The cookbook felt heavier, which was stupid. Missing a page should have made it lighter. Her thumb found the ragged edge where the page had been.

Pathwell saw, and looked away.

"You just sold your potential," she said.

He flexed the shaking hand once. "Some of it."

"Which part?"

"If I knew that, I'd make worse decisions with more confidence."

The shopkeeper pulled a handcart out from behind the counter. It unfolded twice, extended once, and stopped at a size that shouldn't have fit back there in the first place.

Pathwell stared at it. "You had that the whole time?"

"Yes."

"You've watched me carry things out of here by hand."

"Yes."

"For decades."

The shopkeeper set the first bundle on the cart. "Your account is settled now."

Pathwell looked genuinely wounded.

Elizabeth laughed. One short, surprised sound. Both men looked at her.

"Don't make anything of that."
