## 1. Overall verdict

**What kind of book this is.** A warm, dry, character-driven contemporary fantasy about grief, consent and restitution. The magic system turns handwritten records (recipes, letters, grocery lists) into fuel, and each use destroys the original. That one idea carries the whole theme. Everything the book cares about comes down to the question *whose is it to spend?* Pathwell pays with his own future and assumes that makes the method his to choose. Elizabeth learns to choose, and to refuse other people's choices made on her behalf. Shade is a by-product of someone else's certainty and asks only "to stop deciding what I am before I get to." Underneath the comedy it is a book about a woman whose grandmother has died, and it never says so louder than it has to.

**What it does best.**
- **Specific, funny, sad objects.** The cake that reads ELIZ. The thirteen stairs. The note MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH. Shade's fragments ("A woman named Celia who hated pears"). SAM HOME TODAY. LIKED POCKETS. The book trusts small things to hold big feelings, and they do.
- **Structural rhymes that pay off.** "It was the crash that woke her" (Ch1 and Ch8). "That was the choice" (Ch4 and Ch15). "That's terrible." / "I know." (Space Between, then Shade's coffee). The Camp welcome speech (Ch4 and Ch17). "Ready?" / "No." / "Perfect." with the roles reversed at the end. None of these feel mechanical, because each one comes back changed.
- **Restraint at the big moments.** Elizabeth gives up the whole cookbook, steps over her diary and past the cookbook to reach Milo, and stays quiet after the bowl of dumplings. The book doesn't milk any of these.
- **Comic dialogue** with excellent timing, especially Stansbury ("I have not reached the axle emotionally"), the archivist and Mama Baga.
- **Consent as a lived texture, not a lecture.** The healer asks "Can I touch it?", Mama Baga asks Shade "You want to leave?", and Elizabeth chooses not to use the dagger on a binding that "might be him."

**Weaknesses a reader would feel.**
- **Chapters 6–9 sag.** The mystery of whose door the first blob came to gets re-explained four or five times: Mama Baga, Stansbury, the mirror, the parking lot, the car, the diner. The reader is usually a step ahead of the characters by then.
- **The deadpan sounds the same in many mouths (B).** Almost everyone (Stansbury, Mama Baga, the archivist, the healer, Shade, the curator, the shopkeeper) speaks in the same clipped, one-word comeback rhythm ("Accounting." "Cruel." "Occupationally." "Apparently."). It is enjoyable line by line. In long runs, though, such as the brass-pin scene and the tribunal, the speakers blur together.
- **A handful of small continuity and attribution slips.** All are listed below, and all are easy to fix.

**Would I recommend it?** Yes, without hesitation, to readers who like Becky Chambers, T. Kingfisher, or the gentler end of Pratchett: humane, funny, grief-aware fantasy where the stakes are personal and the repairs are paperwork. The middle needs a trim. The opening four chapters and the last six are very good, and Chapter 15 (the fire) is the best chapter in the book.

## 2. The four passages

### Chapter 3: from Elizabeth passing out in the bookstore to waking in the chair (lines 405–441)

This read beautifully. It is the emotional key to the whole book, and putting it here is a good call. There's a hard cut from "The last thing she registered was the carpet against her knees." to "Nana's house had thirteen stairs, and by the end Elizabeth knew which ones complained." The cut is unannounced, so for a sentence or two I didn't know whether this was a dream, the bookstore doing something to her, or the narrator stepping in. That brief uncertainty works because it feels like being unconscious. By "She had moved back in for a few months" I was fully inside it and glad to be.

What lands:
- The caregiving is drawn in exact, unsentimental strokes: the pill organizer, the pharmacist, the tomatoes, "to carry it back down mostly full."
- Nana is fully alive in four lines of dialogue: "The pepper was the best part, Lizzy." / "Then you made them wrong."
- "Lizzy" in Nana's mouth reframes every time Pathwell has used it.
- The party paragraph is quietly devastating. "She topped off her own glass while she was at it, because she was at it" plants something without pointing at it.
- "and she noticed she was listening for someone upstairs. / There was no upstairs." is the best two-line beat in the opening act.

The return, "When her eyes opened, they were heavy," is clean, and the awful coffee ("Like the past") is a good bridge.

Two snags:
1. The party paragraph repeats Ch1 almost word for word ("which was more than anyone from work managed"). It reads as a repeat rather than an echo (B).
2. Ch2 had said "the heat of an oven she hadn't stood in front of since she was twelve." This passage shows her cooking chicken and dumplings from the cookbook for Nana for years, presumably in the same kitchen. A careful reader catches the contradiction (A, see the issues list).

Otherwise: don't touch it.

### Chapter 16: a young woman brings Elizabeth a bowl (lines 6399–6407)

It landed fully, and it's one of the three or four best moments in the book. It is set up well: "Loss didn't care much about paperwork." then "Someone set a bowl down on the dirt beside her."

The identification line ("It took Elizabeth a second to place her without the blankets: the cot in Mama Baga's wagon, the curled fingers, the north grade") is necessary given the gap since Chapter 4. It does that job quickly and doesn't sentimentalise.

Then: "Chicken," the woman said. "There's dumplings in it. They're not as good."
- "They're not as good" quietly tells us she tasted Nana's recipe through the working, without anyone explaining it.
- She leaves before Elizabeth can answer, which is exactly right.
- The last line, "There was more in it than she could possibly eat.", echoes MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH without quoting it, and it trusts the reader to hear Nana.

I noticed nothing to fix. It's the payoff of the book's central idea (a recipe spent is not a recipe lost if someone carries it), and it arrives right after the cookbook has burned, which is the only place it could go. **Do not touch.**

### Chapter 17: a family arrives at Camp (lines 7239–7257)

This landed. It's short, which is a strength. "all of them wearing the look Elizabeth remembered from the inside of her own face" is a good figurative turn that tells us exactly who these people are without saying refugees, newcomers or the lost.

The key beat is Mama Baga "start toward them, stop, and turn around." It's clear and earned: she is giving Pathwell back a job rather than taking one from him. "He looked at her once, to be sure. She waited. / He went." quietly shows the change in him: he checks before he acts. The speech is the Ch4 litany again, now done for someone else's sake. "He remembered the runemasters this time" is a lovely callback, and "with more spaewife in it" is a delight. Asking the grandmother "whether he could take her bag. She let him." rounds off the consent theme lightly. "Is he always like this?" / "Worse," Mama Baga said, and went to see about the goat. is a perfect exit and rhymes with Shade's "Is everyone here like this?" / "Worse."

Only quibble (B): "the way he had swept it the first night" points at a callback the verbatim speech already makes obvious. Optional cut.

Context note: the scene sits between two much quieter procedural passages (Hearts, the museum reopening), so it may be over before some readers realise it's the emotional turn of Ch17. I would still not lengthen it.

### Chapter 18: the confession and what follows (lines 7621–7761)

**The confession** lands hard, and it's planted fairly. Ch15 had shown "Pathwell lay on his side by the collapsed frame… He was close enough to hear Shade calling. His face was turned toward him." Then "I thought that if the creature took him, it would be finished. The draw. The whole business." / "I could have called someone. I didn't." It's a genuine gut-punch: it recasts his Ch16 apology list ("Shade") from negligence into a choice. Elizabeth's answer ("I didn't help him either… I went to Milo.") and his correction ("You went to save Milo." / "That's not what I did.") are exact. Neither of them is allowed to share the other's guilt. "The shopkeeper turned a page. Elizabeth couldn't tell whether he'd heard. She didn't look at him." is very good staging.

**What follows** mostly works, with one reservation.
- Her retreat into the aisles is right: "with one hand flat against a shelf, the way she had the first night, and waited for her breathing to come back to her". So is the browsing paragraph: "chose not to learn why they were sad" mirrors her new boundary.
- The route choice, "a surprisingly responsible version of the question," the geese ("They have learned commerce."), the invitation and the reversed "Ready?" / "No." / "Perfect." all work.
- The final exchange is a superb bookend.

The reservation (B, not A): between "That's not what I did." and "Do you want to come?" nothing in the text registers that she is choosing him *knowing this*. The text only says "She stayed another hour," and then the tone turns to geese. I believe the silence is deliberate, since it matches her Ch16 refusal ("I am not going to make your guilt useful"). Still, on first read the turn from confession to whimsy felt about one beat too quick. A reader could take the ending as the confession being smoothed over rather than carried. If the author wants to address it, the fix is a cut, not an addition: dropping the geese exchange's last two lines would bring the invitation in sooner. My honest recommendation is to leave it. The roles-reversed final line carries the weight, and the confession makes that ending *harder*, which is better.

One small logic itch: "I could have called someone." A bucket-line man was already pulling at Shade, so a reader may ask what calling would have changed. It's in character for guilt to overclaim, so I'd leave it (B).

## 4. Top 10 by effect on a reader

1. **The repeated "whose door" recap in the middle (Ch6 #18, Ch7 #19, Ch9 #23).** This is the main reason Chapters 6–9 drag. The reader solves it in Ch5, and the characters re-solve it three more times. Cutting the Ch9 paragraph (A) and the Ch6 fragments (D) tightens the middle noticeably.
2. **"He lies, you know." / "I don't lie." (Ch5, #16, A).** It makes Pathwell look telepathic, which is a false magic rule at a point where the reader is still learning the rules.
3. **The oven contradiction (Ch2 #7, A).** Ch3's flashback is the book's emotional foundation, and this line quietly contradicts it.
4. **The confession's aftermath (Ch18, B).** The turn from "That's not what I did." to the geese is quick. Probably right, but it's the moment most likely to divide readers about the ending. See section 2.
5. **Elizabeth's return home is skipped (Ch17 #41, A).** A brief "wait, where is she?" right as the book shifts into its restitution coda.
6. **The coffee contradiction in the final chapter (Ch18 #48, A).** Small, but it sits in the last ten pages, where readers are paying close attention.
7. **Inconsistent italics (#24, A).** In print, the unmarked interior lines in Ch2, Ch7 and Ch8 will read as narration or as disembodied voices.
8. **Pathwell's graffiti lecture (Ch11 #29, D).** A character explaining what the scene just showed, right before the book's biggest set piece.
9. **"This one was especially difficult." (Ch16 #38, A).** An attribution stall in an otherwise tight grief chapter.
10. **"He had the queen." (Ch14 #33, A).** An attribution stall at the start of the book's most tense confrontation.
