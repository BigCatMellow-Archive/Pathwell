# Acquisitions report: untitled manuscript (~46,500 words, 18 chapters)

Reader: acquiring editor, adult fantasy, large-publisher genre imprint. Cold read of the whole book, in order, in 400-line chunks. I opened no other file. My running log, unrevised, is at the bottom of this document.

---

## Would I take it to the meeting?

**Yes, as a revise-and-resubmit I'd champion, or as a conditional offer if the author agrees to the revision below.** Confidence: fairly high on the book, moderate on getting it through sales at its current length.

Why yes: the back half of this book does something I rarely see on submission. It puts real grief, real comedy and real moral consequence in the same scene, without the jokes undercutting the grief or the grief deflating the jokes. The archive funeral in Ch 16 ("LIKED POCKETS") and Elizabeth writing the dumplings recipe on an index card in Ch 17 are passages I'd read aloud in the room. The magic system (handwritten things hold what their writers felt, and using them spends them) gives the book one sentence of hook and a whole ethics to go with it. The found documents (the Hess margin note, SAM HOME TODAY, RUTH LIED ABOUT THE LARD) are the author's superpower. Nobody else on my desk writes them this well.

Why not an unconditional yes: the first ~40% is a different and weaker book. It opens as a Doctor Who-style romp, with a charming immortal dragging a bewildered woman from one location to the next, generic blob fights, and two brothers who know the central secret and refuse to say it for 3,400 lines. Elizabeth is mostly a straight man until she crashes the Cadillac in Ch 8. The book becomes itself at the museum (Ch 11) and is excellent from there to the end. A sales rep who reads the first fifty pages will not reach the museum. And at 46,500 words it's a long novella, not a main-list adult fantasy.

## Positioning

**Audience.** Adult readers of comfort-with-teeth fantasy: the Klune and Becky Chambers readership, plus BookTok's books-about-books crowd. Crossover potential to book clubs, through the grandmother's-cookbook grief story, which is genuinely universal. Not for readers who come for action or romance; there is no romance, and that's a feature I'd protect.

**Comps (what it sits next to):**
- *Under the Whispering Door* (Klune): grief handled with whimsy, a found-family waystation, a gently comic otherworld that takes death seriously.
- *A Psalm for the Wild-Built* (Chambers): a short book whose real question is "what counts as a person, and who gets to decide?" That's Shade's whole arc. It also proves the market will pay for a short, quiet SFF book.
- Tonal ancestor, not a sales comp: *Neverwhere* (Gaiman). A hidden world behind ordinary doors, a door-opening rogue, an ordinary person pulled through. The Ch 2 "shuttered laundromat she'd never seen before" and the park "that shouldn't have been there" are very close to London Below. Fine as lineage; the revision should make sure the opening doesn't read as homage.
- Adjacent shelf-mates: *Legends & Lattes* (low stakes, found family) and *The Ten Thousand Doors of January* (doors, written words, books-as-portals). Both show the shelf exists. Both make it crowded.

**Shelf.** Adult SFF, contemporary/cozy fantasy, the "comfort read" table. Not urban fantasy, despite the first chapter's monster.

**Format.** As written: a long novella or very short novel. Two honest options:
1. **Novella-program fit at ~50–55k after revision.** If the house has a novella list, this is close to ready-length, and Chambers proves the model.
2. **Main list at ~70–75k.** This is my preference, but only if the added words come from the lifts below (more Shade, more Elizabeth, the prune client made concrete), not padding. The book's compression is part of its quality. I would not ask for 90k.

**Standalone or series opener.** It works as a standalone and should be sold as one. The ending (geese that "have learned commerce", Pathwell's depleted future, the Space Between's other exits) leaves room for a companion book with the same cast. Don't ask the author to bolt on a series arc.

**Title.** None on the manuscript. Marketing will want one that signals paper, recipes or archives, not monsters.

## The case marketing would make for it

- **The hook in one line:** a grieving woman learns that handwritten things hold the feelings of the people who wrote them, and that every spell spends something someone loved. That's a pitch, and the cookbook gives it an image.
- **Quote-ready moments:** the cake that reads "ELIZ" because her coworkers started at the wrong end (Ch 1), and its "ABETH" sequel (Ch 17). Boots, the "full-time guardian and part-time blood god" (Ch 3). Foam swords powered by children's school art (Ch 5). Grocery lists as GPS ("MILK / ONIONS / ASPIRIN / STAMPS / SHOE POLISH", Ch 10). The archive ledger's "LIKED POCKETS" (Ch 16). These are made for social media.
- **Books-about-books and archives appeal**, with a fresher angle than most: it's about the ordinary documents, not the grand library.
- **The emotional core is universal:** caregiving for a grandmother, then the boxes afterward. Ch 3's thirteen stairs will make people cry in reviews.
- **A cast readers will fight over:** Stansbury (the dry older brother), Mama Baga, Milo and his sock, the archivist, and Shade. Shade is the character fan art gets made of.
- **No romance, a grown-up ending.** Elizabeth refuses to forgive Pathwell on schedule ("I need you not to make me responsible for whether you become a better person", Ch 16). Marketing can sell that as unusual.

## The case sales would make against it

- **Length and price.** 46.5k words at a main-list adult price is a hard sell to accounts. "Is this a novella?" will be the first question.
- **The first fifty pages sell a different book.** A blob monster bursts through an apartment door, followed by a generic fight and a whimsical stranger. It reads as standard urban fantasy, and buyers who sample it will shelve it there. The book's real identity (quiet, document-driven, grief and accountability) doesn't show until Ch 4's soup scene and doesn't dominate until Ch 11.
- **The marketable face is the problem character.** Pathwell is the charm engine, and by the end he has overridden three explicit "no"s, burned an archive, and confessed that he let Shade die because it solved his problem. Cozy readers may not forgive him. That's what makes the book good, and it's a real sales risk.
- **The heroine is passive for a quarter of the book.** In the first seven chapters Elizabeth is mostly led, fed, told to drive, and asking questions nobody answers.
- **The loved character dies at ~75%.** It's earned, and it will still produce one-star "I was promised cozy" reviews if the packaging promises cozy.
- **A crowded shelf.** Books-about-books, found-family camps and magical bookshops are everywhere. The differentiator (paper as spent feeling, accountability over redemption) has to be on the cover copy, not just inside.
- **No series hook to sell into.**

## The revision I'd require before signing

In order of importance. Each describes a direction; the author writes the new material.

1. **Give the first 40% an engine, and stop the withholding loop (Ch 1–10).** The central question (what Pathwell did, and why the blob came to *her* door) is raised in Ch 4 ("It was already at the door before you used the cookbook?") and isn't answered until the van in Ch 10, around 44% in. In between, the answer is deferred by "Not here." / "Later." / "For now." / "I can't yet." / "Drive." The Ch 8 ditch is the clearest example: Elizabeth crashes a car on purpose to get an answer, and the plot hands her an interruption (Shade's arrival) instead. Her biggest act should buy her something. Seed the pull and the double earlier: Pathwell's "pull" first appears at the bar mirror in Ch 6, but it should twitch in Ch 1–3. Move a partial confession earlier, and let the reader hold the right question from the first act. The book knows this is a problem ("every time we get to the part where somebody explains what the hell is happening, I become the driver", Ch 7). Naming a problem isn't fixing it.

2. **Rewrite the loud magic in the book's quiet register (Ch 1, Ch 3, Ch 5).** Every time magic is spectacle, the prose goes generic: "some sort of blob", "the behemoth's weight" (Ch 1); "Pathwell screamed in a million voices, voices lost to time, burning out to become memories never made" (Ch 3); "The fingers of his left hand turned in a circle, twisting and spinning... A few more twists of his palm, a few flicks of his fingers" (Ch 5). Every time magic is tied to a specific document, it's brilliant: the soup page, the love note, the quarantine letter that becomes a wall, the grocery lists, SAM HOME TODAY flooding the room with relief. The rule should be that every working shows what the paper was, what it meant, and what it does. Ch 1 especially, because it's the sample chapter. The torn sugar-cookie page and the "light gone wrong" only make sense in retrospect (Ch 14's "Don't. Think." payoff).

3. **Make Pathwell's third override different in kind, and move the Shade confession (Ch 14, Ch 16, Ch 18).** The prune, the museum letter and the rejoining frame are the same choice at rising prices. The museum relapse is credible. The frame, a few chapters later, risks reading as a man who can't learn rather than a tragedy. Give the frame scene a new element: a motive he hasn't had before, or a moment where he *thinks* he's asking and isn't. Then move "I could have called someone. I didn't." (Ch 18) into the Ch 16 apology, where his list conspicuously says only "Shade." As it stands, the darkest truth in the book arrives 170 lines from the end and is followed within a page by a whimsical portal with geese. The ending can survive that confession, but not with one paragraph of digestion.

4. **Lift: more Shade (Ch 8–15).** He enters at ~31% and dies at ~75%, and he's the best thing the book invents. One more scene would make his death land harder and the "what is a person" question fuller: Shade alone with Elizabeth or Milo, choosing something unprompted, not reacting to Pathwell. The Hearts chapter shows the author knows how to do it.

5. **Lift: Elizabeth before the call, and her first choice on the page (Ch 1–4).** We get Nana superbly (Ch 3), but very little of who Elizabeth was before caregiving: her mother (mentioned once, Ch 6, "Red sky at night"), her job, what she wanted. Her decision to leave the bookstore for Camp happens off-page and is reported in one line at the top of Ch 4. Put it on the page. Make her want something in the present by Ch 2 beyond "go home" and "answers."

6. **Compress the denouement (Ch 16–17).** Roughly 20% of the book follows the fire. The restitution vignettes (drying rack, transcription, Susquehanna, the Henry Vale letters, The Lemon, the crooked label) are the same beat six times: Pathwell wants to improve something, is told no, complies. Keep the transcription ("Copy the smudge") and the Henry Vale refusal; fold the rest into a paragraph. Cut or clarify Ch 17's last line ("Elizabeth smiled."), which reads as a chapter needing to end. There are three endings (Ch 16 night, Ch 17 The Lemon, Ch 18 Space Between). Ch 18 is the real one, and the other two should feel like steps toward it.

7. **Line polish (throughout).** See top-ten item 8.

8. **Settle the loose threads, or own them.** The person at the "kitchen table" who asked Pathwell for help is never identified or explained. Nana's first name and Elizabeth's family name are pointedly withheld ("She gave it. He wrote it down.", Ch 4; "You have the family name.", Ch 12) and never pay off, so it reads as a tic. Same for the girl with the bucket ("There's something there", Ch 14), Pathwell's depleted future ("You don't have much left to give", Ch 3) after he spends more in the frame, and the "big cat / grande gatto" nickname, which felt like an in-joke the first time (Ch 10). Each needs a payoff, a line of grounding, or a cut.

## Ranked top ten: what I most want the author to hear

1. **The first 40% hides the book you actually wrote.** The real book (grief, paper, accountability, found documents) starts at Ch 4's soup scene and takes over at the museum in Ch 11. Rebuild the opening act so it's recognisably the same book. This decides whether it sells.

2. **Stop making characters withhold the central secret.** It's the cheapest suspense there is, and the reader feels it before Elizabeth says so. When she crashes the car in Ch 8 to get an answer, give her one.

3. **Your quiet magic is great and your loud magic is generic.** Rewrite every set piece so the document drives it. Your own best scene proves it works: the museum collapse in Ch 11–12, where each broken record floods the room with its own specific feeling.

4. **Pathwell's third "no" needs to be a new mistake, not the same one at a higher price,** and his confession about Shade needs room. Put it in the Ch 16 apology, not 170 lines from the end.

5. **Shade is your best invention. Give him one more scene.** His death is the right cost; it'll cost more if we've had more of him.

6. **Make Elizabeth act earlier.** From Ch 8 on she's a real protagonist (the ditch, Shade's car, the archive rescue). Before that she mostly follows. Put her choice to go to Camp on the page.

7. **Your found documents are the best thing in the book. Lean on them harder.** The Hess note, the button card, SAM HOME TODAY, the Ellison letters, RUTH LIED ABOUT THE LARD. Nobody else writes these this well. They should appear in the first three chapters too, not only Nana's cookbook.

8. **Watch the tics.** "Not X. Y." constructions ("Not books—lives.", "Not burned. Spent.", "Not flesh. A seam.", "Not hidden. Not displayed. Available." and many more). Pathwell "went still" five or six times. A personification gag in nearly every scene (the sock "refusing employment", the mailbox that "declined responsibility", the fish "withholding judgment", the wall that "had been arrogant all day"). Narrator underlining: Ch 4's "That was the choice. / Everything afterward was consequence." steals from Ch 15's much better echo. Restating the consent theme in scene after scene of Ch 12–16 ("The healer asked first."; "People keep doing that." / "Asking."). Also the one-off second-person intrusion ("You would be forgiven for not stopping in the town", Ch 5) and the "He lies, you know." / "I don't lie." moment in Ch 5, which reads as mind-reading and confused me.

9. **The aftermath is a fifth of the book. Cut it to a tenth.** Keep the funeral, the index card, the curator scene and the Merritt cookbook. Compress the restitution montage.

10. **Decide the length on purpose.** Either a tight ~50–55k novella or a ~70k novel grown through Shade, Elizabeth and the prune client. Don't let it land at 46k by accident. And give it a title that promises paper and grief, not monsters.

## From where it is to a 9

### 1. Score

**Overall: 7/10** against *Under the Whispering Door* (Klune), *A Psalm for the Wild-Built* (Chambers) and *Neverwhere* (Gaiman). The back half alone would score 8, with 9-level passages. The front 40% drags the whole down to a 7.

| Dimension | Score | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Prose | 7 | The quiet register is 8–9 (the Nana flashback, the soup, the museum, the funeral). The loud magic is 5. Recurring tics. |
| Character | 8 | Shade 9, Stansbury 8, Mama Baga, Milo and the archivist 8. Elizabeth 6 early and 8 late. Pathwell 7: superb voice, but a repetitive flaw arc. |
| Plot and structure | 6 | Episodic first half, withholding as suspense, midpoint at ~51%, a 20% denouement, triple ending, dangling client thread. The Ch 4 / Ch 15 cookbook structure is a genuinely smart exception. |
| Humor | 8 | Consistently funny and character-driven ("I tip historically."; "I have not reached the axle emotionally."; "Categories are temporarily flexible."). Slightly over-dense early. |
| Emotional payoff | 8 | The cookbook, Shade, the index card. The Shade confession is under-landed. |
| Originality | 7 | Paper-as-spent-feeling and accountability-over-redemption are fresh. The whimsical-immortal rogue, the hidden bookshop and the found-family camp are familiar. |
| Ending | 7 | The Merritt cookbook and the inverted "Ready?" / "No." / "Perfect." bookend are good. The confession-then-geese whiplash costs it. |

**What the numbers mean for a book like this:**
- **6:** A charming premise and likeable cast; the plot meanders, the voice is pleasant but generic, and you forget it a month later. Competent cozy.
- **7:** A distinctive voice and at least a few scenes that genuinely move you, held back by structural slack, an uneven opening, or a protagonist who takes too long to become one. *Where this book is.*
- **8:** Every act is the same book. The opening sells the real premise. The protagonist drives it. The set pieces obey the magic's logic. You recommend it to friends by describing a specific scene.
- **9:** All of the 8, plus inevitability: the ending reframes the beginning, the central image (here, the handwritten recipe) carries the whole book's argument, and there are no flat stretches. It's the book other submissions get compared to. *Under the Whispering Door* at its best moments; *Psalm* throughout.

### 2. The gap to a 9, ordered by value

1. **Rebuild the opening act (Ch 1–7) around the real book.** Worth about **+0.75**. Seed the pull and the double from Ch 1. Cut the withholding loops. Let the ditch in Ch 8 buy a partial answer. Put Elizabeth's Camp decision on the page. Bring found documents in before Ch 4. *Large. Fix.*
2. **Rewrite the loud-magic set pieces in the document-driven register (Ch 1 blob fight, Ch 3 pruning tree, Ch 5 portal).** Worth about **+0.5**. Each working should show what the paper was and what it meant. Make Ch 1's torn page legible on first read. *Medium. Fix.*
3. **Differentiate Pathwell's frame choice and relocate the Shade confession (Ch 14, 16, 18).** Worth about **+0.5**. The third override needs a new kind of failure. "I could have called someone. I didn't." belongs in the Ch 16 apology, and Ch 18 needs space for Elizabeth to respond before the geese. *Medium. Fix.*
4. **Add a Shade scene (between Ch 9 and Ch 13).** Worth about **+0.25–0.5**. Shade chooses something unprompted, away from Pathwell's pull. *Medium. Lift.*
5. **Give Elizabeth a pre-Nana self and a present want (Ch 1–4, threaded later).** Worth about **+0.25**. Her mother, her job, what moving across the city was supposed to fix. That makes her early passivity a starting point rather than a default. *Medium. Lift.*
6. **Compress the denouement and remove the false endings (Ch 16–17).** Worth about **+0.25**. *Small to medium. Fix.*
7. **Resolve or cut the dangling threads (the kitchen-table client, the withheld names, the bucket girl, Pathwell's remaining future, the "big cat" nickname).** Worth about **+0.15**. The kitchen-table client is the one I'd most want answered, even in a line, because the whole prune turns on it. *Small. Fix.*
8. **Line pass for tics, POV slips, dangling modifiers and theme-restating (throughout).** Worth about **+0.15**. *Small. Fix.*

All of that is roughly +2.5 on paper; in practice, landing items 1–4 well gets it to a solid 8.5. The last half-point to a 9 comes from making the index-card image carry the whole book. That means planting Elizabeth's own handwriting earlier (her diary lists, her failed funeral page) so that writing the recipe herself in Ch 17 reads as the inevitable end of a line that started in Ch 1. *Medium. Lift.*

### 3. The ceiling

**A 9 is within reach of this book as conceived.** It doesn't need a different premise or a different cast. It needs the first 40% to be written by the author who wrote Chapters 11–18. Everything a 9 needs already exists somewhere in the manuscript: the magic logic, the moral argument, the comedy, the image system (handwriting, copies versus originals, terrible coffee, "I know"). The front half just doesn't use it yet.

What a 9 has that a solid 7 doesn't: **unity**. In a 7, the good scenes are islands. In a 9, every scene is visibly part of one argument, so even the jokes carry weight. This book's back half has that unity: the funeral is funny *because* it's about paperwork and grief at once. The front half has charm without unity: the dumplings, the bar and the foam swords are fun but could sit in any whimsical fantasy.

Where it sits on the curve: **past competent, into distinctive-but-uneven.** This isn't a manuscript stalled at competence; the voice and the best scenes are already professional and better than much of what's on shelves. The risk at this stage is different. Authors who reach "distinctive" tend to polish sentences when what's needed is structural surgery on the parts they're fondest of. The work that moves this book past where it is:
- Cutting charming material that repeats (the restitution montage, the sock beats, the third terrible-coffee scene, the brothers' banter loops).
- Asking of every scene, "is this the book's argument, or just its tone?"
- Re-drafting the opening *after* knowing the ending. The opening reads like it was written before the author discovered what the book was about.

# Appendix: running log (written as I read, unrevised)

**Lines 1–400 (Ch 1 – early Ch 3)**
- Ch 1 opening line "It was the crash that woke her." Clean, does its job. The half-eaten cake reading "ELIZ" is the first thing I'd quote in a pitch: specific, funny, sad. She's new, the party was for her, nobody stayed.
- The magic: he lays a hand on Nana's handwritten sugar-cookie recipe and it lights up; she pulls the book, the page tears, she gets a full sensory memory of Nana. Memory-as-fuel, handwriting-as-power. That's a hook I can say in one sentence. Interested.
- The monster is a "blob", "behemoth", "some sort of blob". Generic. The fight in Ch 1 is blurry: I couldn't tell what the torn page did or why the light "had gone wrong". Possibly deliberate, but it reads as murk, not mystery.
- "The cake, somehow, was fine." Good beat. Lands.
- Pathwell = whimsical immortal-ish stranger who eats, deflects, whisks a sad woman off into a hidden world. Doctor Who's Doctor, Neverwhere's door-opening. The shuttered laundromat and the park "that shouldn't have been there" are straight Neverwhere/London Below. Not a dealbreaker; noting the comp pull.
- Ch 2 crying scene over the dry-cleaning ticket is earned and the best thing so far: "staying felt worse than leaving, but leaving didn't feel like anything at all." That's a real sentence.
- Pathwell banter: "Just a really weird Tuesday for you." / "It's Thursday." / "Which makes it all the weirder." Charming once. The "Lizzy" tic and the deflection rhythm could wear thin fast. Watching it.
- Ch 3 bookstore made of lives: "Not books—lives. Journals with cracked spines, letters bundled in ribbon, recipes on index cards spotted with oil." Shelf: Starless Sea / Invisible Library / Midnight Library territory. Crowded shelf.
- Line-level: dangling modifiers ("Flipping through worn and stained pages, he found a tagged recipe"; "Lunging again, the blob knocked the man"), "sat on the floor" (British) in an otherwise American setting (dry cleaning, cops, landlord). Copyedit-level, but a sales rep reading 20 pages notices.
- Stakes so far are small and personal (meeting at nine, dry cleaning). I like that. Question is whether the book knows that's its strength.

**Lines 401–800 (Ch 3 cont. – Ch 4)**
- Aisle of other people's emotions tipping her into a panic attack: smart use of the setting, physical and specific.
- Nana flashback (Ch 3) is the best writing in the book so far and the reason I'd keep reading a submission: "She learned to carry a plate up thirteen stairs without spilling it, and to carry it back down mostly full." / "as if the pages were running out before she was." / "she noticed she was listening for someone upstairs. / There was no upstairs." Short, unsentimental, devastating. This is the book's engine. If the rest is built on this, I have something.
- Boots the "full-time guardian and part-time blood god" cat; "We pay him in mice." Cozy-fantasy signalling. Marketable, also a little familiar.
- Paper magic system clarifying: a love note heals a cut, leaving "a lingering feeling that reminded her of a boy she knew in middle school." Emotionally charged writing as currency/fuel. Coherent and pitchable.
- Pathwell selling his "potential": the tree-of-roots set piece is overwritten. "Pathwell screamed in a million voices, voices lost to time, burning out to become memories never made. One by one they fell quiet, until all that was left was a harmony of extinguished cries." Generic grand-fantasy language, unlike the rest of the book's voice. "Some branches grew mangled, withered, dead... sick and breathless... sitting sickly and worn": stacked adjectives saying the same thing.
- But the comedown is great: "Will that be paper or plastic?" / "Surprise me." and the handcart ("You had that the whole time?" / "Yes." / "For decades."). "If I knew that, I'd make worse decisions with more confidence." Pathwell is good at being funny about pain. That's the character.
- Rai Stone and Lydian coins as fantasy currency: nice real-world in-joke.
- STRUCTURE FLAG: Ch 4 opens with her already having chosen to go to the camp ("after the shopkeeper had offered to send her back to the sidewalk"). The single biggest choice so far, accepting the call, happens off-page and is reported in one sentence. I want that scene.
- Camp Cunnan / Cunning Folk / Mama Baga (Baba Yaga wink). The beetle ("It's practicing." / "Being dead.") lands. But the whimsy density is rising: "someone who believed replacement was a personal failure", "something tea had become after a long argument with bark", "Sit before you fall over politely", the sock that "had surrendered". Pratchett/Kingfisher register. Each is fine; stacked, the narrator starts performing. Watching.
- Pathwell's catalogue speech ("Sanctuary, alcove, holdfast and haunt...") is fun once. Elizabeth is mostly a straight man so far ("Darn it", "Thankful!?"). She's sharper in the flashback than in the present.
- Comps forming: Klune (Under the Whispering Door: grief + whimsy), Gaiman's Neverwhere (hidden world, door-opening rogue), Emily Wilde / Kingfisher for the voice. Cozy-adjacent with real teeth.

**Lines 801–1200 (Ch 4 cont.)**
- The cookbook-for-the-dying-girl scene. This is the book. "She believed soup was an argument you could win." / "If I keep holding the rest, I'm going to spend the next ten minutes finding a reason this page is the wrong one."
