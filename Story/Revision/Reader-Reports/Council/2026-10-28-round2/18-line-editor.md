## Part 2: Style sheet (as kept)

**Names and nouns**
- Elizabeth (Pathwell calls her "Lizzy" off-page; it is referenced at 6563), Nana / Nana Jones / Nana's (capital N), Pathwell, Shade, Stansbury, Mama Baga, Milo (unnamed "boy with the turnips" in Ch 4 and 12), Boots (cat), Thumper.
- Lowercase roles: the archivist, the healer / Camp healer, the shopkeeper.
- Camp Cunnan / Camp is capitalised, but Ch 4 line 702 has "the camp". Cunning Folk. the Space Between. blob (lowercase). prune / pruning. working (noun). charged (record). reserve shelf. intake.

**Notes written inside the text**
- All caps on their own line (MORE IF THEY'RE SICK…; the card at 4498).
- Exception: Ch 1 puts the recipe title in single quotes ('Nana Jones Sugar Cookies'). Make it consistent.

**Punctuation**
- Ellipsis is the "…" character: six in Ch 1, one in Ch 4, none in Ch 9, 12, 16 or 18.
- Unspaced em dash for interruption.
- "!?" appears twice (Ch 1, Ch 4). Choose a policy.

**US vs British usage**
- The setting is American: sheet cake, fish fry, county road, "Darn it".
- Two British forms slip in: "sat on the floor" (Ch 1) and "got the shoe back on" (Ch 4). Elsewhere the book uses "gotten" (Ch 4 1038, Ch 18 7350).

**Spelling and hyphenation**
- "okay", lowercase.
- "wet-rot" should be "wet rot".
- "sugar-cookie page" is hyphenated as a modifier. Consistent.
- "chicken-and-dumplings page" in prose vs "CHICKEN & DUMPLINGS" on the card. Fine.

**Numbers and contractions**
- Times written as "seven-oh-three". Years as numerals (1912, 1948).
- Contractions in dialogue are unsettled for Pathwell (Ch 1/4 vs Ch 16/18) and Mama Baga (inside Ch 4).

## Part 5: Where characters sound alike

1. **One comic engine for everyone: the pedantic correction delivered as a verdict.**
   - Shade: "Observation. Not memory." "Accurate." "Accepted."
   - Shopkeeper: "Accounting." "A month is not a character trait."
   - Mama Baga: "That is not a posture." "That was not the question." "That is not an archive category." "Wrong." "Better."
   - Archivist: "That is different." "Specificity is expensive." "That's different from ownership."
   - Healer: "Attached and working are different questions." "Useful." "Better."
   - Stansbury: "Noted." "Define a while."
   - Elizabeth: "Important distinction" (×2), "Define using.", "Convenient.", "Strong sales pitch."
   - Pathwell: "Profoundly." "Historically." "Unfortunately."
   - Each line is good. Cover the speaker names, though, and I often couldn't tell who said it.
2. **No contractions as house style.** Shade, the shopkeeper, the archivist, the healer, Mama Baga (some of the time) and Ch 18 Pathwell all speak without contractions. By Ch 12 and Ch 16 Elizabeth does too ("I am enjoying the possibility." "It has been a morning." "I am not going to…").
3. **This undercuts the book's own device.** Shade is made from pieces of Pathwell, and the text points out the resemblance ("Pathwell would have smiled after saying it. Shade didn't."). That mirroring should be the one place where two voices overlap. When every voice overlaps, it loses its charge.
4. **The healer and Mama Baga** are interchangeable brisk caregivers in Ch 12 and Ch 16, with the same deadpan threat and concession rhythm.
5. **Elizabeth's dryness** is the narration's dryness. In Ch 1 she is flustered ("They're… they're in a box in the kitchen?"); from Ch 9 on she is the wittiest person in every room, in the narrator's idiom. That could be growth, but I didn't see it shown as change.
6. **Pathwell's voice drifts.** Ch 1 and Ch 4: loose, contracted, "probably", florid showman ("Did I say runemasters?"). Ch 16 and Ch 18: clipped and formal. If it's an arc, it needs one visible hinge. I can't tell from my chapters.
7. **Mama Baga drifts inside Ch 4** between a formal elder ("We did not ask for this") and stock grandmother ("Hush now… I won't hear another word about it"). In Ch 16 she speaks accountability language ("as if saying a large enough number is accountability").
8. **Milo is the most distinct voice in the book**, with child logic ("I could give it to somebody with one foot"). Use him as the benchmark.

## Part 6: Twenty line fixes

1. **A** — Ch 1, 39: "They're… they're in a box in the kitchen?" answers a question about one cookbook. Make it singular.
2. **A** — Ch 1, 9–11: "She knew him, a little, he'd been at the party." is a comma splice. Two consecutive sentences open with "But". Fix the splice and drop one But.
3. **A** — Ch 1, 85: "the stench of wet-rot splattered across the room." A smell can't splatter. Let the ooze splatter and the smell follow. "wet rot" takes no hyphen.
4. **A** — Ch 1, 71: "Sun shining through an open window above the sink, the fresh smell of rain filled her lungs." Dangling clause, plus a possible contradiction. Choose one, or make the sun-shower explicit.
5. **D** — Ch 1, 71: "Elizabeth felt something she hadn't felt in years." Name it through a concrete object or sensation, the way Ch 4's flu memory does ("Steam in her face. Nana pretending not to watch").
6. **D** — Ch 1: five thuds in about 1,200 words. Cut two, and let the sound change as the threat grows (strain, splinter, silence).
7. **A** — Ch 4, 676–680: she "got up with dirt in her teeth", then "lay there", then is pulled onto her knees. Reorder. Cut "another greeting with the ground" (no earlier fall is shown) and one of "frustrated. Annoyed."
8. **B** — Ch 4, 951–953: "That was the choice. / Everything afterward was consequence." The scene has already dramatised this. I'd cut it; some editors would keep it as a hinge.
9. **C** — Ch 4, 1095–1097: Nana's actual name is withheld ("She told him"). Giving it to the reader would change the effect, either privacy or a missed intimacy. Decide on purpose.
10. **D** — Ch 4, 863: "That sounds as bad as she looks." Said over a dying stranger, this reads as callous. Shift it to something Elizabeth thinks, or soften it toward shock.
11. **A** — Ch 9, 2805: "It was for the pull itself." The referent is unclear and the line over-explains. Either attach "No" to the pull in the action beat itself, or cut the explanatory sentence.
12. **A** — Ch 9, 2901: "That's his answer." reads as Shade answering. The intended meaning seems to be "his to answer". Make that unambiguous.
13. **B** — Ch 9, 2999: end on 2997 (Shade's hand opening and closing). The final sentence states a resolve the reader already feels.
14. **C** — Ch 9, 2991: Shade "didn't offer to drive her", so she walks off at night, broke, toward directions three turns long. Changing it alters the point of the scene (her independence, his non-interference), but one line acknowledging the walk would stop the logistics question.
15. **A** — Ch 16, 6464–6466: Milo's "My boot burned too." and "He nodded solemnly. 'It was a good boot.'" sit in separate paragraphs with no reply between them, so it reads as two speakers. Merge them.

## Part 7: Ranked top ten

1. **Chapter 1 is the weakest prose in the book, and it's the first page every reader sees.**
   - It reads like an earlier draft layer: splices, a mixed image, stock heart and stomach reactions, five thuds, six ellipses, "His face changed", "felt something she hadn't felt in years".
   - Chapters 4 onward are clearly a better writer. Bring Ch 1 up to Ch 4's level, especially the Nana memory, which should be as specific as the flu memory.
   - High confidence. This is worth more than anything else here.
2. **Every character uses the same comic engine** (pedantic correction, one-word verdict, no contractions). It flattens the cast and undercuts the Shade/Pathwell mirroring. Give each speaker a distinct rhythm and a different kind of wit. Milo shows the book can do it.
3. **The restraint beat is overused.** "didn't ask / didn't comment / let her / didn't reach" appears 30+ times. Keep it where restraint costs the character something (6824, 6848, "He did not say Lizzy"). Elsewhere, cut it or show it in action.
4. **Gesture saturation.** "looked" 154 times in about 15,400 words, "nodded" 30, "considered" about 14, "waited" about 13, plus 15+ one-line reaction paragraphs. A dedicated pass would cut 40–50% without losing any meaning.
5. **Stop explaining your best images.** The thesis sentences ("That was the choice. Everything afterward was consequence." / "Loss didn't care much about paperwork." / the Ch 9 and Ch 16 last lines) and the counsellor-voiced speeches in Ch 16 tell the reader what superbly built scenes have already delivered. Roughen the speeches into each character's idiom.
6. **Ration the running gags.** Sock ×5, terrible drink in every chapter after Ch 1, and verbatim repeats ("mouth moved at one corner", "Important distinction", "Define ___", "Elizabeth corrected herself"). A callback works when it's rare and slightly changed. Repeated verbatim, it becomes a tic.

8. **The consent motif is the book's ethic and it works, but it repeats its mechanics.** "Can I…?" / an answer that becomes "less automatic" / "corrected herself" happens the same way in Ch 12 and Ch 16. Vary how it appears so it builds instead of repeating.
9. **Style-sheet consistency:**

   - ellipses (only in Ch 1)
   - the contraction policy for Pathwell and Mama Baga, made deliberate
10. **Protect what's working.** Keep the handwritten notes in the margins, the archive ledger as an emotional instrument (UNKNOWN; LIKED POCKETS), Milo, the dry narrative wit, and the motif of paper being used up. Don't sand these down in revision.

### 3. The ceiling

- A 9 is reachable as conceived. The concept (memory spent like currency, an archive that keeps records "people still need", accountability without absolution) can support a 9, and several passages already sit there. This doesn't need a different book.
- It does need a different kind of revision from the one that got it to a 7. The book has cleared the stage where many manuscripts stall: its scenes are built, its structure rhymes, and its emotional set-pieces work.
- Where it's stuck now is texture: one voice spread across eight mouths, a narrator who explains, and gestures repeated until they're wallpaper.
- What a 9 has that a solid 7 lacks is *differentiation and trust*. Every voice is its own, and the author lets the best images stand alone.
- Getting past competent is subtractive and character-specific: read each speaker's dialogue in isolation, cut every sentence that explains an image, and make each recurring device appear less often but change each time.
- Confidence: moderate-high that this reaches an 8 with items 1–4. Moderate that it reaches a 9 without item 2 done thoroughly.
