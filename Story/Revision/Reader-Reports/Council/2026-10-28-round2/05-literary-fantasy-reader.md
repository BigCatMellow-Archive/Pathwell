# Report: literary fantasy reader (cold, whole book)

I read the whole manuscript in order, in chunks of about 400 lines, and opened no other file. The running log I kept while reading is in the appendix at the end. It is unrevised, including two places where I changed my mind as I read (Ch4's "That was the choice," Ch4's welcome speech).

The short version: this book has two voices. One is the voice of the Nana flashback in Ch3, the cookbook gift in Ch4, the fire in Ch15 and the bowl of stew in Ch16. That voice is as good as anything on my shelf. The other is a sitcom voice: three-beat banter that the whole cast and the narrator share. That voice is competent and sometimes very funny, but it takes up most of the page count. The book's best moments come when the first voice wins.

## Where interiority is deep and where the book stays on the surface

### Where it stays on the surface (cut and fix first)

- **Ch 1 action.** I watched from outside. "His face changed." is used here and again in Ch 7 ("Pathwell's face changed. 'Oh,' he said."). Both times the book declines to tell me what I'm looking at. "behemoth" a paragraph after "blob" is a register slip. The memory flash ("Elizabeth felt something she hadn't felt in years") names a gap instead of filling it. Ch 3 then fills that gap brilliantly, which shows the author can do it.
- **Ch 3, the selling of potential.** "beautiful blue, like a distant sky," "violent blue," "frightful orange," "screamed in a million voices, voices lost to time, burning out to become memories never made... a harmony of extinguished cries." The adjectives do the work the stakes should do. I felt more from the aftermath ("as though posture had become the problem") than from the spectacle.
- **Ch 5, portal and school.** A long choreography paragraph (wind, coat, bandana) and then a screwball sequence ("Small child. What is today?", "Mrs. Pathwell... Don't be rude, honey.") in which Elizabeth suddenly becomes an improv comic. That doesn't fit the woman of Chs 2–4. Mama Baga's bandana is singed in half and nobody feels anything about it, in a book whose subject is objects that carry people.
- **Ch 6, the bar,** apart from the silent gestures between the brothers and the mirror section.
- **Ch 10, the brothers walking home.** The slap routine, "big cat," "grande gatto." This is the author enjoying the characters more than I could.
- **Chs 17–18, denouement.** Several scenes repeat one beat: the drying rack is three inches too low, the label is one degree crooked, the wagon is a "diseased lemon," Pathwell accepts each time. I understood the change the first time.
- **Pathwell at the decisive moment.** In Ch 15 we see only "His face was turned toward him." Then in Ch 18 he confesses that he chose to let Shade be taken. The plant is good, but the book stays outside him for the most important choice he makes, and the confession gets one exchange before the geese.
- **Elizabeth outside of grief.** She is deep wherever Nana is concerned and nearly blank everywhere else. We never learn Nana's first name. In Ch 4 the book withholds it at the exact moment Elizabeth struggles to remember it ("She told him"). We never learn Elizabeth's surname, what she does at work, or anything about the mother who taught her "red sky at night." The diary, the one object that is wholly hers, appears once in Ch 5, and then we read nothing from it except one moving-day list. When it burns, I lost a symbol, not a voice I'd come to know.

### Where it is deep

- **Ch 2,** crying at the plastic table. The dry-cleaning ticket going into the gutter is her ordinary life literally blowing away, and "He left the last dumpling on her side of the tray."
- **Ch 3, the Nana flashback and the party.** This is the best sustained writing in the book. "Nana corrected the measurements with her eyes closed." "Afterward there was nobody to carry anything up the stairs for. Elizabeth carried boxes down them instead." I was entirely inside her.
- **Ch 4, giving the cookbook for the girl on the cot.** "Elizabeth almost said sugar cookies. The answer hurt before she reached it." MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH. "If I keep holding the rest, I'm going to spend the next ten minutes finding a reason this page is the wrong one." This is interiority carried in speech, with no wink.
- **Ch 6, Pathwell at the mirror.** "like hearing your own voice on a recording and recognizing every word without recognizing the man who said them." "damage was easier than mystery." This is the only long passage inside him. It's good enough that I wanted three more like it.
- **Ch 9, the back seat and the diner.** "because she had chosen the back seat, and the distinction felt more important the farther they got from the wreck."
- **Ch 12, the diary donation.** "like a radio left on in another room of a house."
- **Ch 15, the fire.** She steps over the diary and runs past the cookbook. "Her body knew the book before her mind finished identifying it." There's a motif here that I'm not sure the author knows is a motif: her body lags behind her will, or runs ahead of it. Ch 1 has "Her body moved back before she did." Ch 8 has "They weren't hurt, just late to the decision." Ch 16 has "didn't remember sitting down." It's good. Use it on purpose.
- **Ch 16, the grief.** "Alive. / The word kept arriving as if somebody had to keep delivering it." The woman from the cot brings stew with dumplings ("They're not as good"). Then LIKED POCKETS, and Mama Baga's "Person is the word I have for that."
- **Ch 17, the apartment.** The heavy dumplings and the pepper put in on purpose, then the recipe written on an index card in her own hand. The book answers the Ch 3 flashback without commenting on it. That's the real ending.

## Does the banter serve or crowd the feeling?

It does both. The problem is how much of it there is and that everyone sounds the same. Any single line is fine.

**Where it crowds (named first):**
- **During danger.** In Ch 7, while Pathwell is being swallowed: "Yes! It slipped my mind! Leo, would you please focus!?" and Elizabeth's "That's useful advice late." The joke reflex fires at the exact moment the feeling should be fear.
- **During grief.** In Ch 16, within two pages of Shade's death and the archive collapsing, the healer gets a full three-beat comic exchange: "I will tie you to the wagon." / "Is that allowed?" / "No." / "That seems less effective." / "It is what we have." People do joke at disasters. But this is the same joke shape as the bar in Ch 6, so it reads as the book's habit, not as grief.
- **The narrator joins in:** "something tea had become after a long argument with bark," "The mailbox declined responsibility," "a burnt fence post... concentrated it for shipping," "detergent in boxes small enough to qualify as insults," "excessive pockets" in the middle of Elizabeth's crying. A narrator who quips in every scene leaves no quiet place for a reader like me to feel anything.
- **Everyone has the same voice.** The shopkeeper, Mama Baga, the archivist, the healer, Stansbury, Milo, Pathwell and, from Ch 5 on, Elizabeth all talk in clipped setup and deflating punchline: "That explains the filing." / "Specificity is expensive." / "Consistency." "Stagnation." "Branding." / "Strong sales pitch." Each line is good. Stacked together, they turn the cast into one witty person. Elizabeth in Chs 1–4 didn't talk like this. By Ch 12 ("Define using.") she does, and some of her distinctness is gone.
- **Specific set pieces to cut:** the school ID scene (Ch 5), the foam "noodle" jokes (Ch 5), the slap routine (Ch 10), the blue-spot parking joke (Ch 11), the purple-foot joke (Ch 13), the Lemon and the crooked label (Ch 17), and "emotional support dumpling" (Ch 18).

**Where it serves:**
- **When the joke is the wound.** "If I knew that, I'd make worse decisions with more confidence." (Ch 3) "Several dignities." (Ch 7) "I have not reached the axle emotionally." (Ch 10). That one made me laugh out loud and also told me he was upset.
- **When a child's literalness carries grief.** "I could give it to somebody with one foot." / "He still owes me a hand." (Ch 16)
- **When the joke is a choice claimed.** "Pathwell stupid?" / "Worse. Mine." (Ch 15)
- **When the joke is how a community holds a loss.** LIKED POCKETS. "Archive burned. Categories are temporarily flexible." (Ch 16)
- **When the book turns on its own habit.** "You are not making that joke today." (Ch 14) "You don't get to use being funny to decide when this becomes easier." (Ch 16) These are the most important lines about humor in the book. They show the author knows Pathwell's wit is a defense. But they only work if Pathwell is the one who does it. As long as the narrator and everyone else do it too, the indictment has no target.

**Verdict:** right now I'd estimate about a third of the banter serves the feeling and two thirds crowds it or is neutral filler (moderate confidence). Keep the jokes that cost the speaker something. Cut the jokes that only show the author is clever.

## What would move this book toward the literary end of the shelf, and what would be lost

**Moves, in order of weight:**
1. **Make the Nana voice the governing voice.** Strip the narrator's quips almost entirely. The narration of the dumplings, the stairs, the sock and the fire is already literary. Let it run the whole book.
2. **Give each character a distinct voice and register.** Pathwell gets the wit and the deflection, and uses it less as the book goes on. Mama Baga gets economy (she already has it: "That is not a posture"). The archivist gets procedure. Milo gets literalness. Shade gets flatness. Stansbury gets dryness, but with tenderness under it. Elizabeth gets the plainness of the Ch 2–4 woman. Her wit should come late and be her own.
3. **Open the diary.** Give us entries, maybe one at the head of some chapters, or as Elizabeth reads it in the field in Ch 5, so that by Ch 15 the reader has lost something too. Name Nana. Give Elizabeth a surname and a past before she became a caretaker.
4. **Let the ethics of spending other people's records weigh on someone.** Shade's "Somebody wrote them." (Ch 11) is the only time anyone feels the cost to the writers of the unsent letters, the grocery lists and the Elsie quarantine letter. In a literary version, Elizabeth would feel that a stranger's six years of longing were burned to find her.
5. **Give the Shade confession its weight.** It belongs either inside Pathwell during Ch 15 or at the center of the last chapter, with nothing light after it.
6. **Stop saying the theme out loud.** Keep one or two of the best statements of it and trust the reader with the rest.
7. **Make the big magic quieter so it matches the small.** The grocery-list navigation (Ch 10) and the cookbook that "began to give off the particular heat of a kitchen in winter" (Ch 4) are the book's true magic. The fire-tree (Ch 3) and the wind-portal (Ch 5) belong to a different, louder book.

**What would be lost:**
- **Pace and warmth, and some readers.** The banter is a real skill. It makes the book easy and companionable, and it gives the opening chapters momentum when Elizabeth is passive. A cozy-fantasy reader (the House in the Cerulean Sea crowd) may like this draft more than the version I'm describing.
- **Some of Pathwell's charm, the hook of the first act.** Strip too much and Chs 1–3 lose their drive.
- **Lightness.** Literary versions of whimsical premises can curdle into preciousness. Piranesi avoids that by being strange, not by being sad. The Kelly Link-adjacent moments here (the beetle "practicing" being dead, the geese that "have learned commerce," the cannon inching toward the gift shop) are worth keeping because they're odd, not because they're funny.
- **My judgment:** the trade is worth it. The material wants to be the quieter book. It's about grief, caretaking and who gets to decide, and its best scenes are already written in that register.

## Ranked top ten: what I most want the author to hear

1. **You have two voices; choose one.** The Ch 3 Nana flashback, the Ch 4 cookbook gift, the Ch 15–16 fire and its aftermath, and the Ch 17 dumplings are at the level of the books I love. The sitcom register (Ch 5's school, Ch 6's bar, Ch 10's slap routine, much of Chs 17–18) is competent genre comedy. Every top-ten sentence comes from the first voice.
2. **The narrator should stop being funny.** Bark tea, the mailbox, the fence-post coffee, the detergent boxes, "excessive pockets" in the middle of grief. Each one takes a little of the reader's room to feel.
3. **Everyone talks like the same witty person.** Differentiate the voices. Your thematic critique of Pathwell's humor ("You don't get to use being funny to decide when this becomes easier") only lands if he is the one who uses it.
4. **The most important fact in the book is hidden in the epilogue and followed by geese.** Pathwell chose to let Shade be taken. That should change the temperature of the ending. Give it room, either inside him in Ch 15 or as the weight of the last chapter.
5. **Elizabeth is deep in grief and shallow everywhere else.** Name Nana. Give Elizabeth a surname, a job, a mother, a want of her own. Let us read the diary before it burns.
6. **Trust the small things.** Your marginalia and objects are your best writing every time: MORE IF THEY'RE SICK, HESS SAYS FOUR, the socks Daniel Vale kept, SAM HOME TODAY, RUTH LIED ABOUT THE LARD, LIKED POCKETS. Your best magic is the most modest (grocery lists). The loud set pieces (Ch 3 tree, Ch 5 portal) are your weakest prose.
7. **Stop diagnosing Pathwell aloud.** Stansbury (Ch 14), Shade (Chs 9 and 14), Mama Baga (Ch 16) and Elizabeth (Chs 10 and 16) all explain his flaw. Keep Shade's "You still think the result gets to decide whether the choice was acceptable" and Elizabeth's "I need you not to make me responsible for whether you become a better person." Let the rest go unsaid.
8. **Cut the sitcom set pieces and compress the middle and the denouement:** the school ID scene, the foam noodle jokes, the brothers' slap routine, the blue-spot parking joke, the repeated corrections in Ch 17 (rack, label, Lemon), and "emotional support dumpling."
9. **Let the cost to strangers land.** The unsent letters (Ch 10), the Elsie quarantine letter (Ch 11) and the grocery lists are burned for the protagonists' convenience, and only Shade notices. That is the book's richest moral vein, and it's barely touched.
10. **The real ending is Ch 17's chicken and dumplings.** The Ch 18 bookend ("Are you ready?" / "No." / "Perfect.") is clever, and her asking first is the real change. But ending on his quip in her mouth returns the book to the register it spent its last third arguing against. Consider ending on her hand around the index card. (Medium confidence. Many readers will love the bookend.)

Line-level, quickly: "His face changed" twice (Chs 1 and 7). "Darn it" (Ch 4). "Mrs. Elizabeth" (Ch 6). In Ch 5, does Pathwell hear her thought "He lies, you know"? I couldn't tell. In Ch 11 the "I don't need to be rescued" attribution is unclear. Ch 11's "two brothers split by land and by ideology" is on the nose. The split foam dagger is set up heavily and then never resolved.

## From where it is to a 9

### 1. Score

**Overall: 7 / 10**, measured against *The Invisible Life of Addie LaRue*, *The Night Circus* and, for the warm-whimsical register this draft often sits in, *The House in the Cerulean Sea*. It's a solid 7: better than most published cozy fantasy at its best moments, and below Addie or Morgenstern in consistency of voice.

| Area | Score | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Prose | 7 | At its best (Chs 3, 4, 15–17) it's a 9. Its median is clean and competent, and it's pulled down by narrator quips and the purple set pieces. |
| Character | 7 | Shade 8.5: the best-made character, and you made me grieve him in one chapter. Pathwell 7. Mama Baga and Milo 7.5. Elizabeth 6.5: deep grief, thin life. |
| Plot and structure | 7 | The causality is excellent: the pocketed letter pays off at the museum, "Don't. Think." pays off in Ch 14, his relapse is enacted, not reported, and the cookbook and diary gifts become two refusals in the fire. The sags are Ch 5, Ch 6, parts of Ch 13, and a denouement with too many endings. |
| Humor | 6.5 | Line for line, highly skilled. In aggregate, overused and undifferentiated, and it often fights the feeling. |
| Emotional payoff | 8 | The stew bowl, LIKED POCKETS, the sock, the chicken and dumplings. I was moved four or five times, which is more than most books manage. |
| Originality | 7 | Fresh: magic as spending what people wrote, selling one's possible futures, and Shade as the pruned self. Familiar: the bookstore-between-worlds with a cat, and the found-family camp. |
| Ending | 6.5 | Ch 17's dumplings is a 9 ending. The Ch 18 epilogue (the confession given too little room, then geese, then the mirror quip) dilutes it. |

**What each score looks like for a book like this:**
- **A 6** has a clever premise, likeable banter and a clear plot, and you forget it a month later. Feeling is announced, not built.
- **A 7** is this book: real moments of feeling and strong setups and payoffs, held back by an inconsistent voice and characters who serve the theme a little too visibly.
- **An 8** has a governing voice that never slips. The humor belongs to specific characters, the middle has no rest stops, and the ending is the one the book has been earning.
- **A 9** has all of that plus a sensibility you'd recognize from a single paragraph. Every scene does two things, the reader is trusted with the theme, and the last image rearranges the first chapter (the way the end of Addie rearranges the deal).

### 2. The gap to a 9, ordered by points

1. **Set one governing voice: de-quip the narrator and differentiate the dialogue.** About +0.75. *Where:* the whole book, heaviest in Chs 5, 6, 10, 12–14, 16–18. *What:* cut nearly all narrator jokes. Cut a third to a half of the three-beat exchanges. Give each speaker one register. Make Pathwell the main quipper, and have him quip less over the book's arc. Keep the jokes that cost the speaker something. *Size:* large. *Type:* fix.
2. **Deepen Elizabeth beyond grief.** About +0.5. *Where:* Chs 2, 4, 5, 9, 12, 15. *What:* name Nana and give Elizabeth a surname. Give her a life before caretaking (work, the mother, friends lost during the years upstairs) and a want of her own beyond "being told" and "choosing." Bring in the diary's voice early so its burning costs the reader. *Size:* medium to large. *Type:* lift.
3. **Give the Shade abandonment its weight.** About +0.5. *Where:* Chs 15, 16, 18. *What:* either a short passage inside Pathwell in Ch 15 as he lies in the dirt hearing Shade call, or move the confession into the Ch 16 apology. There, his long list of apologies that leaves out the worst thing would be devastating irony, and the last chapter can live with what it means. Whichever you choose, nothing light should follow it directly. *Size:* medium. *Type:* fix and lift.
4. **Cut the sitcom set pieces and compress the middle and the denouement.** About +0.4. *Where:* Ch 5 (school ID scene, noodle jokes), Ch 6 (trim the fish and branding runs), Ch 10 (slap routine), Ch 13 (trim; keep Hearts, pockets and coffee), Chs 17–18 (fold the rack, label and Lemon into one beat; trim the geese). *Size:* medium. *Type:* fix.
5. **Let the cost to strangers land.** About +0.25. *Where:* Chs 10, 11, 18. *What:* give Elizabeth one moment of reckoning with the letters and lists burned on her behalf. Shade's "Somebody wrote them" is the seed. *Size:* small to medium. *Type:* lift.
6. **Stop stating the theme.** About +0.25. *Where:* Ch 4 (trim "That was the choice," since the Ch 15 echo is the stronger use), Ch 11 ("two brothers split by... ideology"), Ch 12 and Ch 13 ("Asking." / "Terrible habit."), Ch 14 (Stansbury's speech), Ch 16 (Elizabeth's speech after its first sentence), Ch 17 ("The choice was ridiculous. / It was still hers."). *Size:* small. *Type:* fix.
7. **Quiet the loud magic.** About +0.2. *Where:* Ch 3 (the tree and the "million voices"), Ch 5 (the portal choreography). *What:* write them in the register of the grocery lists and the kitchen-in-winter page. Fewer adjectives, more consequence. *Size:* small. *Type:* fix.
8. **More time inside Pathwell.** About +0.15. *Where:* one or two more passages at the level of the Ch 6 mirror, for example during the Ch 14 frame or the Ch 17 transcription. *Size:* small to medium. *Type:* lift.

Taken together, that's roughly 7 to 9. Items 1–3 alone would make it a strong 8.

### 3. The ceiling

**A 9 is within reach of this book as conceived. It is not within reach of this book as voiced.** The conception is a 9 conception. Magic is the spending of what ordinary people wrote down. Grief is the handing-on of a recipe. A man pays for his rescues with his own future and calls that responsibility. The person his failed payment made becomes more of a person in one morning of losing at cards than he ever was. Those are the bones of a book I'd press on friends. What holds it back is the governing sensibility. If the author decides the house voice is the comic one, the ceiling is a very good 8: a superior cozy fantasy, which is a legitimate book on a different shelf. If the author decides the house voice is the Nana voice, with comedy living inside particular characters, a 9 is reachable without changing the plot.

**What a 9 has that a solid 7 doesn't:** consistency of attention. In a 9, the paragraph about the thirteen stairs and the paragraph about the bar have the same narrator. A 9 trusts the reader with the theme, and it makes every scene do two jobs: Ch 13's Hearts game already does two (Shade becoming a person; Elizabeth catching Pathwell's habit in "That's your plan. Let me find mine."). Ch 5's school scene does one.

**Where this manuscript is on the curve:** past the competent stall point in structure. The setups and payoffs, the causal chain of the climax and the relapse-as-plot are better than most published books in this space. Plotting doesn't usually stall a book like this. The stall happens in voice and subtraction. The work that moves it past competent is cutting lines that are individually good: the clever exchange, the funny aside, the second statement of the theme. It's harder than adding, because the cut lines will be some of the author's favorites. The test for each line: does it cost the speaker something, or does it only show the author is clever?
