# Reader report: the caregiver / grief-memoir reader

Read cold, whole book, in order, in chunks of about 400 lines (lines 1-7779, Chapters One through Eighteen). I opened only the brief and the manuscript. I ran one `wc -l` on the manuscript to find its length. I opened nothing else.

A note on what I expected versus what I got. My lens is grief memoir and the logistics of care. This is not a grief memoir. It is a comic portal fantasy (a charming trickster, a hidden camp of "Cunning Folk", a bookstore that trades in other people's feelings) with a grief story folded inside. I read it for how grief, care and loss are written, as asked. Where the fantasy is only fantasy, I say so rather than fault it for not being something else.

## Running log (first reactions, not revised)

**Ch. 1-2 (lines 1-330).** A cold open that is all farce: a stranger reading her diary, a blob at the door, a gold-light working. I was entertained but at arm's length. The first thing that made me sit up was small: the cake that "now only read ELIZ", and then the dry-cleaning ticket skipping into the gutter, after which she cries and the text says "leaving didn't feel like anything at all." That is the first true note. Pathwell is quippy to the point of wearing me out already.

**Ch. 3 (330-647).** The Space Between is a bit of a fantasy-shop set piece (Rai Stones, Lydian coins, a cat that drinks blood). But the swerve into Nana's house stopped me cold: "Nana's house had thirteen stairs, and by the end Elizabeth knew which ones complained." This is the real book, and it is a page long. The pill organizer, the plate carried down "mostly full", "The pepper was the best part, Lizzy." Then the party paragraph ending "she noticed she was listening for someone upstairs. / There was no upstairs." I would have kept reading a whole novel of that. The tree-and-fire payment ritual afterward left me flat ("Pathwell screamed in a million voices, voices lost to time").

**Ch. 4 (648-1268).** Camp. Twee in places ("tea, or something tea had become after a long argument with bark"), but "repaired by someone who believed replacement was a personal failure" is exact. The cookbook surrender is the best scene so far: "If I keep holding the rest, I'm going to spend the next ten minutes finding a reason this page is the wrong one." I believed her entirely. Then the narrator announces "That was the choice. / Everything afterward was consequence," and I felt a hand on my shoulder. The archivist's "That is generally the opposite of an archive" is the thesis, delivered by a character. "Hess has been dead twenty-two years." / "He'd still argue." is perfect.

**Ch. 5-7 (1269-2233).** The long middle begins and my attention drops. A portal to an elementary school, a walkie-talkie guard named Ken, foam swords, "noodles", Stansbury as a man who "steals children's energy", a bar with a mounted fish, a mirror, a blob in a parking lot. Competent and jokey. I did not care about the swords or the school. The bar has warmth (brothers who move each other's beer six inches from the edge without comment). "He looked fine, which, she had recently learned, was a posture" is a lovely callback. Grief is absent for about 900 lines apart from two details (the ginger ale; "What do you normally have?" / "She didn't have an answer for that either"). Those two are real and tiny.

**Ch. 7-8 (1916-2625).** "'Drive,' Pathwell said. ... Not cruel. Worse. Useful." That is the best sentence in the book about what care does to the carer. It hit hard. Her deliberate ditch crash is a satisfying agency beat, if a bit "empowerment moment". The echo "It was the crash that woke her" worked on me.

**Ch. 9-10 (2626-3600).** Shade, a doppelganger made of pruned future, arrives. The diner is good, and the cheesy grits with "extra pepper" is a quiet callback to Nana. "It was exactly how she'd have made it." But the engine of the plot is now people withholding information ("Not here." "Later." "I don't know yet.") and I started to feel the mechanism. The best thing in the stretch is the grocery lists: MILK / ONIONS / ASPIRIN / STAMPS / SHOE POLISH, "Nothing more romantic than errands." The slaps ("Bank it") I did not care about. Elizabeth's van interrogation works but she starts to speak in therapeutic summary ("And paying for it yourself made you feel like the method was yours to choose").

**Ch. 11 (3626-4190).** The museum is where the book wakes up. "SAM HOME TODAY. THIN AS A FENCE RAIL. ATE THREE EGGS." Margaret Bell's corrected accession card. "Mostly they were worrying about socks." When the case breaks and "Relief hit the room, huge and impossible and pointed nowhere. Someone was home. Someone wasn't dead. Someone needed eggs. / Elizabeth started crying and had no idea why." I was moved. This is the book's own method working: grief and love as ordinary found text.

**Ch. 12 (4192-4630).** The healer says "Can I touch it?" and "Because it's your shoulder." This is the best depiction of care in the book, and the opposite of Pathwell. Then the diary donation; "FIND NANA'S SPOON / The last item was checked twice." and "every sentence sounded like someone else's grief." I believed it. But I noticed the second surrender repeats the beat of the first ("Elizabeth's hand stayed suspended after the diary left it. Empty.").

**Ch. 13 (4632-5140).** A long, sweet Shade-at-camp interlude: coffee, Hearts, onions, "Pockets." I liked Shade here more than any character. It ran longer than its tension, though. "People keep doing that." / "Asking." / "Terrible habit." The motif is now being said aloud for the third time.

**Ch. 14 (5141-5755).** The pin working. Jargon grows ("rejoining frame", "return working", "released payment"). The line "You are confusing care with tyranny." / "Family tradition." / "Inherited badly." is good in rhythm and bad in being a stated theme. "For who?" / "Pathwell had no answer fast enough" is the real hit. The fire begins.

**Ch. 15 (5756-6155).** Fire, the shelf, Milo. "His sock had finally stayed up. Elizabeth hated that detail immediately." Superb. She steps over the diary and past the cookbook. "Her own ordinary handwriting disappeared without being used for anything." Then the text repeats "That was the choice. / Everything else burned afterward." Second time, and now it reads as a tic. Shade is eaten by a blob. I was upset, which surprised me.

**Ch. 16 (6157-6878).** The aftermath is the strongest chapter. "Alive" arriving "as if somebody had to keep delivering it." The young woman brings chicken and dumplings ("They're not as good") and "There was more in it than she could possibly eat." Milo: "He still owes me a hand." The archivist: "SHADE — DIED AT CAMP." then "LIKED POCKETS." I cried here. Elizabeth's refusal speech to Pathwell ("I am not going to make your guilt useful") is right in idea and too composed in delivery.

**Ch. 17 (6879-7305).** Restitution. Well made, but it goes on and the arcs begin to announce themselves. The apartment and dumpling-making paragraphs are quietly strong. I got restless in the drying-rack and yellow-wagon stretch; it is the same joke (he almost improves it; he stops) five times.

**Ch. 18 (7306-7779).** The Merritt family cookbook ("RUTH LIED ABOUT THE LARD." / "SHE DID NOT. YOU USED THE WRONG CUP.") is the funniest and truest page in the book. Pathwell's admission that he let Shade be taken, and Elizabeth's "I went to Milo" / "That's not what I did," is the book's one real moral knot, and it gets about 40 lines before the geese. Ending: "Are you ready?" / "No." / "Perfect." A neat echo of Chapter One. I liked it and also saw it coming.

## 1. Moments that are true to grief and care

These are the passages I would hand to another reader as the reason to read the book.

- **The caregiver's house, Ch. 3.** "Nana's house had thirteen stairs, and by the end Elizabeth knew which ones complained." / "She learned the pill organizer with the days stamped on the lids. She learned which pharmacist didn't make you wait and which neighbor would sit with Nana for an hour if you brought her tomatoes. She learned to carry a plate up thirteen stairs without spilling it, and to carry it back down mostly full." This is precisely the logistics I look for, and the plate going back down "mostly full" tells the whole story.
- **Nana's voice, Ch. 3.** "'You ate the pepper.' / 'The pepper was the best part, Lizzy.'" And "Nana ate four spoonfuls and told her the dumplings were heavy. / 'They're your dumplings.' / 'Then you made them wrong.'" That is a real old woman who is dying and still correcting you.
- **"We'll see" in the voice she used for weather and doctors** (Ch. 3). Exact.
- **Handwriting getting smaller in the margins** "as if the pages were running out before she was" (Ch. 3). Exact and restrained.
- **After a death, the house in the ear.** "She noticed she was listening for someone upstairs. / There was no upstairs." (Ch. 3). Honest and brief.
- **The party self.** "She had forgotten she was good at parties. She refilled the chips before anyone noticed they were low... Whenever a conversation ran out, she picked up three empty cups and went to the kitchen, where there was always something to do." This is the caregiver who cannot sit down.
- **"What do you normally have?" / She didn't have an answer for that either** (Ch. 6). A woman with no preferences left after years of someone else's schedule. Tiny and right. The follow-up (the ginger ale, "I don't want an appletini") is a lovely first act of taste.
- **"'Drive,' Pathwell said. Her hands went still on the wheel. The word had come out automatically. Not cruel. Worse. Useful."** (Ch. 7). The single truest sentence about being the carer: you are a function that other people are glad of.
- **The ticket in the gutter, Ch. 2.** "Three weeks of bare walls and boxes she couldn't bring herself to open. A life she'd packed into garbage bags and moved across the city because staying felt worse than leaving, but leaving didn't feel like anything at all."
- **The reflex of the empty hand.** "The expectation arrived automatically, the way you hold out your hand for change after paying" (Ch. 4); "checking, like a tongue finding the place a tooth used to be" (Ch. 16). The second image is a stock one, but the first is fresh and the pair work together. Also: "The diary hadn't been hers when it burned. That didn't stop her body from expecting its weight."
- **The smoke of Nana's kitchen, Ch. 15.** "For one breath the smoke smelled like Nana's kitchen. Pepper. Flour. Sun on the window over the sink. / Then it was smoke again." No gold light, no voice. It is the right size.
- **"Alive. The word kept arriving as if somebody had to keep delivering it."** (Ch. 16).
- **The bowl.** "'Chicken,' the woman said. 'There's dumplings in it. They're not as good.'" And "There was more in it than she could possibly eat." This inverts Ch. 3: the carer is finally the one being fed too much.
- **Milo's "He still owes me a hand."** and the archive ledger lines: "SHADE — DIED AT CAMP." / "LIKED POCKETS." / "That is not an archive category." / "Archive burned. Categories are temporarily flexible." (Ch. 16). This is how groups actually mourn: with a form, badly, and it is enough.
- **"Loss didn't care much about paperwork."** (Ch. 16). A shade on-the-nose, but it earns it.
- **Cooking from memory, Ch. 17.** "She put in too much pepper, on purpose." The pepper pays off from Ch. 3 and Ch. 9, and the index card in her own hand is a better ending to the cookbook thread than the fire was.
- **The healer asking.** "Can I touch it?" / "Because it's your shoulder." (Ch. 12). Good care described as consent. This is the book's best statement of its own theme because nobody announces it.
- **The museum.** "SAM HOME TODAY. THIN AS A FENCE RAIL. ATE THREE EGGS." and "For her, perhaps that was the end of the war." (Ch. 11); the museum's later card "ORIGINAL DESTROYED IN COLLECTION INCIDENT" (Ch. 17).
- **The Merritt kitchen book** (Ch. 18): "TOMATO SOUP — FOR SNOW DAYS" / "ALSO GOOD WHEN YOU HAVE BEEN FIRED." / "NOT THAT GOOD." And the shopkeeper's "Nobody had room for the original and everybody wanted somebody else to store it." That last line is the truest sentence about inheritance in the whole manuscript.
- **Funeral diary.** "her attempt to explain Nana's funeral, which she'd given up halfway through because every sentence sounded like someone else's grief" (Ch. 12). Exactly what grief does to a writer.

## 2. Moments that ring false, preachy or too tidy

- **The narrator announcing the turning point, twice.** "That was the choice. / Everything afterward was consequence." (Ch. 4) and "That was the choice. / Everything else burned afterward." (Ch. 15). The first was already a hand on my shoulder; the second turned it into a tic. The scenes are strong enough to carry themselves. Cut both lines.
- **The theme said aloud, repeatedly.** The archivist: "That is generally the opposite of an archive." Pathwell and Stansbury: "You are confusing care with tyranny." / "Family tradition." / "Inherited badly." (Ch. 14). Elizabeth: "And paying for it yourself made you feel like the method was yours to choose." (Ch. 10). Shade: "Asking." / Elizabeth: "Terrible habit." (Ch. 13). The "asking" motif is clear from the healer in Ch. 12; saying it aloud makes it a lesson. Also: "Only then did she realize she was following another man who sounded certain. / Too late to call it an accident." (Ch. 8). The narrator closes the loop for me.
- **The grief is spoken in a tidy therapeutic register by the person who is supposed to be the one holding it.** In Ch. 10 Elizabeth diagnoses Pathwell with insight no stranger in a stopped van at 4 a.m. would have. In Ch. 16: "I am not going to make your guilt useful. I am not going to say losing Shade was worth something because maybe you change after it." The idea is superb. The delivery is too composed for a woman who has just run through a fire and lost her grandmother's book.
- **The book refuses the lesson in dialogue and delivers it in structure.** After Elizabeth says she won't treat Shade's death as productive, Chapters 17-18 are an extended demonstration that it was: Pathwell asks before touching the wagon, loses at Hearts "on purpose", welcomes the family. "I am capable of growth." "I am trying very hard to have been house-trained by consequences." Characters announcing their own arcs is the opposite of Joan Didion. The contradiction undermines the best speech in the book. Either let Pathwell remain only partly changed, or let Elizabeth's speech be a thing the book fails to honor.
- **Grief gets rationed to a paragraph at its single release.** Ch. 16: "Elizabeth cried for Nana. / For Shade's green jacket and excessive pockets. / For a man whose history had only just begun..." Nana gets one line, then shares a sentence with a jacket. The displacement is the book's thesis, but this is where it should break open, and it resolves in four lines.
- **The Ch. 17-18 return to the apartment is too clean.** Landlord replaces the door with "something brown, heavy, and aggressively ordinary", plant survives, cake with "ABETH", work email answered. Each is charming. Together they read as a checklist of reassurances. "She decided it counted." is a nudge.
- **Pathwell's confession, Ch. 18** ("I could have called someone. I didn't.") is the book's biggest moral act and gets about 40 lines and "Say his name" / "Shade." That is a good beat, but then the geese. A man let someone die because it was convenient, and the book moves on in a paragraph with no consequence beyond a mood.
- **The healer in Ch. 4 and Camp in general are suspiciously kind.** Mama Baga gives Elizabeth a chain and a wardrobe within an hour of meeting her; the archivist, healer, Milo, Mama Baga, and the cook all behave like an ideal care team. There is no friction in anyone's help. Real care is clumsy, and someone is always annoyed.
- **One voice for everyone.** Elizabeth, Pathwell, Stansbury, Mama Baga, the shopkeeper, the archivist, Shade and Milo all speak in the same deadpan call-and-response ("Cruel." / "Accounting." / "Important distinction." / "It has required practice." / "Disorienting."). The jokes are often good. But I often could not tell who was speaking without the tag.
- **Nana is nearly absent.** After Ch. 3 she is evoked by magic ("Lizzy" in the ear, taste of butter), not by character. The reader is asked to mourn someone we met for a page and a half.

## 3. Does it let grief be messy, or resolve it?

It resolves, gently, and mostly by displacement. That is the best case for it and the main complaint.

What it does that most such books don't: it refuses forgiveness ("I don't know if I forgive you"), refuses to make Shade's death a lesson (in speech), keeps a scar as a scar ("Let it be, big cat"), and lets the fire destroy the objects rather than saving them in a last-minute rescue. The lines "Both can be true" and "Loss didn't care much about paperwork" are not tidy.

What it does that I'd call too tidy: the grief for Nana is a plot about possessions. The cookbook and diary are handed over, burned, and replaced by a recipe card in Elizabeth's own hand. The sequence is moving and also schematic: she gives away the dead woman's book, a child's life forces her to abandon it, and she ends up as the living version of it. The structure says: you let the things go, and you carry the person. That is a lesson, delivered by plot.

What it avoids entirely: anger at Nana, anger at herself, relief, boredom, the body, money, and the unlived years. Elizabeth "moved back in for a few months" and "The few months became years." We never learn what she gave up. Her mother is mentioned once (the red-sky rhyme in Ch. 6) and never again: who else was there, and why wasn't she? The death itself and the funeral are not on the page.

The ending ("Are you ready?" / "No." / "Perfect.") leaves her ready-ish, which is more honest than closure but still warm. I'd say the book permits grief to be sad, funny and quiet. It does not yet permit it to be ugly.

## 4. One thing the book should know about this experience that it doesn't yet

**Caregiving is where care and tyranny cannot be separated, and Elizabeth lived there.**

The book's moral engine is Pathwell: a man who decides for other people ("Quickest. Cleanest. I can handle it."), pays with his own future, and has to learn to ask first. Elizabeth is the one who sees this and says no. But the pill organizer, the plate sent back up "mostly full", "MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH" in Nana's own margin, "She believed soup was an argument you could win" are all decisions made for another person, daily, for years. A carer overrides someone she loves: what they eat, whether they take the stairs, which pills, who visits. A good carer does it with love and a bad carer does it with contempt and in both cases it feels like both. The guilt in caregiver grief is almost never "I didn't do enough" in the abstract. It is "I decided that for her. I was right to. I'm not sure."

Elizabeth is written as the moral clean hand. She asks permission and refuses paternalism, and the book rewards that. I would like the book to turn the lens: let her catch herself in Pathwell's sentences, not only hear "the work van inside the sentence" in his. A memory of overriding Nana (the thing she decided, perhaps something Nana didn't want), and an honest moment of relief at the end, would make the care/tyranny theme about her, not about a quippy man. That is the version of this book I would put next to the grief memoirs I love.

## 5. Ranked top ten: what I'd most want the author to hear

1. **The real book is the three pages of Nana's house, and it's under-written.** Ch. 3's stairs, pill organizer, plates, "We'll see", and the listening-for-someone-upstairs are as good as anything in the grief memoirs I measure against. Everything else in the book is built to avoid the thing those pages promise. Give Nana and the caregiving years real room: two or three more flashbacks across the book, each as specific as the first.
2. **Turn the care/tyranny lens on Elizabeth.** Pathwell decides for others; Elizabeth has spent years deciding for Nana. The book makes her the clean hand. Let her recognize herself in him (Ch. 11, 14, 16, 18) and carry an honest guilt about something she overrode.
3. **Let grief be unlovely somewhere.** Anger at Nana, relief that it ended, resentment about the lost years, what her mother was doing. At the moment grief is sad, funny, and generous. Those are not the only registers it has.
4. **Stop having the characters and narrator state the theme.** Cut "That was the choice. / Everything afterward was consequence" (both times, Ch. 4 and 15), "You are confusing care with tyranny", "Asking. / Terrible habit", "Too late to call it an accident", and the archivist's "opposite of an archive." The healer's "Can I touch it?" and the ledger's "LIKED POCKETS" already say it.
5. **Resolve the contradiction in Ch. 16 versus Ch. 17-18.** Elizabeth says she won't make Shade's death useful; the next two chapters make it useful. Either let Pathwell's change be partial and unsatisfying, or let the book acknowledge the irony. Cut "I am capable of growth" and "house-trained by consequences."
6. **Tighten the middle by about a third (Ch. 5-10).** The school, Ken, the foam-sword inventory, the bar's banter, the slaps, the "grande gatto" are charming but nothing happens to Elizabeth's inner life for roughly 900 lines. I felt the book was writing a different, jokier novel there. The gems (the ginger ale, "Drive", the ditch, the grocery lists) can survive in a shorter run.
7. **Give the voices their own edges.** The deadpan one-word retort is overused and belongs to everyone ("Cruel." "Accounting." "Important distinction."). Mama Baga, the archivist, Stansbury and Milo should sound different when I cover the tags. Pathwell especially: after Ch. 14 he should lose the quips and I should hear the loss of them.
8. **Give Pathwell's confession its weight.** "I could have called someone. I didn't." (Ch. 18) is the darkest act in the book. It deserves a scene, a consequence, and a hard conversation, not a moment before the geese.
9. **Pay off or cut the dangling mysteries.** What the blobs are, why the first one came to Elizabeth's door, the mirror in the bar's back hall, Prague, the client at the kitchen table, "Stansbury" being "older" and "two hundred years": I got interested, then stopped caring because nothing was resolved. The emotional plot works without them, so pick which to answer and drop the rest.
10. **Keep the found-text device and push it.** SAM HOME TODAY, the corrected accession card, HESS SAYS FOUR, RUTH LIED ABOUT THE LARD, LIKED POCKETS. This is the book's own voice and it's the original thing here. Use more of it where you now use dialogue, and use it for Nana's side of the story (her margins, her corrections, her handwriting shrinking).

## 6. From where it is to a 9

### 6.1 Score

**Overall: 6.5 / 10.**

Measured against: *Neverwhere* and *Good Omens* for the comic-urban-fantasy voice; *The House in the Cerulean Sea* for found-family warmth; and, for grief carried through fantasy, *The Ocean at the End of the Lane* and *A Monster Calls*. By the grief-memoir standard I normally use (*The Year of Magical Thinking*, *H Is for Hawk*, *Crying in H Mart*) it is lighter and gentler, but it contains four or five pages that hold their own beside them.

| Category | Score | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Prose | 7 | Clean, exact on objects, good comic timing. Sentence-fragment rhythm and repeated deadpan tags wear. |
| Character | 6.5 | Elizabeth and Shade are alive; Milo, the archivist and Mama Baga are lovely. Pathwell is overexposed quip. Nana is missing. |
| Plot / structure | 6 | Strong opening, strong third act. A long middle chase with withheld information. Several mysteries never resolved. |
| Humor | 7 | Often genuinely funny (Hess, the cannon, the Merritt book, Milo). Over-used, and sometimes deflects grief. |
| Emotional payoff | 7 | The fire aftermath and the museum get me. The Nana grief itself is under-served. |
| Originality | 7.5 | Magic as the spending of other people's records and one's own future is a real idea, and the archive-as-moral-system is fresh. |
| Ending | 6 | Warm, a good callback, a bit pat; Pathwell's biggest act is left under-weighted. |

**What the scores mean for a book like this:**
- **A 6:** a likable portal fantasy with a good premise and a pleasant voice; the emotional parts are handled competently and could be anyone's.
- **A 7:** the same, but with several set pieces and a few passages of real feeling that readers will quote and recommend. The seams still show.
- **An 8:** the grief and the fantasy are the same story; every set piece carries the emotional argument; the voice is distinct for every speaker; I would hand it to a friend who has just lost someone.
- **A 9:** an 8 that takes a risk on the ugly part of its subject and gets it right, so I would reread it. The structure is economical, and I can't find a chapter to cut.

### 6.2 The gap to a 9, in order of points

1. **Put the caregiving on the page (+1.0). LIFT. Large.**
   Where: Ch. 3 (expand), then 2-3 new short flashbacks threaded into Ch. 7, 11/12, 16 and 18, plus the death and the first hour afterward (currently skipped). What: Nana as a person with moods, vanity, temper, a failing body; the real work (nights, lifting, doctors, money); one scene of the actual death or the days after; one concrete decision Elizabeth made for Nana that she's not sure about. The Camp material can stay; these scenes carry the weight it currently borrows.

2. **Turn the care/tyranny lens on Elizabeth (+0.75). LIFT. Medium.**
   Where: Ch. 10 (van), 11 (museum), 14 (pins), 16 (speech to Pathwell), 18 (confession). What: let her recognize her own "quickest, cleanest, I can handle it" in him; give her a flash of relief or resentment she's ashamed of, so the moral conversation isn't one-sided.

3. **Trim the middle and give each chapter a turn (+0.5). FIX. Medium.**
   Where: Ch. 5-10, with lighter trims in Ch. 13 (Hearts) and Ch. 17 (restitution). What: cut roughly a third; compress the school, bar and drive; keep the mirror, the ditch, the diner, the lists; make the withheld information less mechanical ("Not here." / "Later.").

4. **Remove the announced theme and the announced growth (+0.4). FIX. Small-medium.**
   Where: Ch. 4, 8, 10, 13, 14, 15, 17, 18 (specific lines listed in section 2). What: delete the narrator's "That was the choice" lines; cut the characters' arc summaries; resolve the Ch. 16/17 contradiction.

5. **Differentiate the voices (+0.3). FIX. Medium.**
   Where: throughout. What: reduce one-word retorts; let Mama Baga, the archivist, Milo, Stansbury and Shade have distinct syntax and humor; let Pathwell go quiet after the fire and show it.

6. **Give Pathwell's confession consequence and close or cut the mysteries (+0.3). FIX / LIFT. Medium.**
   Where: Ch. 18, and mentions in Ch. 6, 7, 9. What: one real scene where Elizabeth and Camp respond to "I could have called someone"; decide what the blobs, the mirror and the original client were, or consciously leave them open.

7. **Let the ending stay unfinished (+0.2). FIX. Small.**
   Where: Ch. 17-18. What: trim the reassurance checklist (door, plant, ABETH, work email) to its best two items, and let one thing about Nana remain unsettled.

### 6.3 The ceiling

A 9 is within reach of this book as conceived. It does not need to be a different book. The premise (records as charged objects, a camp whose ethic is to ask before using, a trickster who pays with his own unlived future) already carries a grief argument. The strong parts (the museum, the fire aftermath, Shade, the Merritt book) are the book at its best and they are close to a 9 already.

What a 9 has that a solid 7 doesn't: it commits to the thing it is afraid of. A solid 7 is warm, funny, well crafted, and arranged so the reader is never made uncomfortable. A 9 makes the reader sit in the stairs, the plate, the relief, and the decision she isn't sure about, and lets the fantasy serve that rather than shelter it.

Where it is on the curve: past competent. The book already does a lot of the "polish" work (clear scenes, callbacks, a controlled tone), and from here more polish gives diminishing returns. The next gains are not sentence-level. They come from three kinds of work: risking the ugly part of the subject, cutting hard, and trusting the objects and found text to speak instead of the characters. I would put it at the edge of the plateau where many manuscripts stall: it has everything except the nerve.
