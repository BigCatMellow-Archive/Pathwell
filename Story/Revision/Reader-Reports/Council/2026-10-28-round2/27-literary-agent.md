# Agent read: untitled contemporary fantasy (46,567 words)

## Gate decisions

**Page one (lines 1–39): keep reading.** The ELIZ cake ("half a sheet cake that now only read ELIZ, because her coworkers had started at the wrong end") and "reading her diary like a magazine in a waiting room" earn the next page. The hook ("Where's your cookbook?… The old one, the handwritten one with notes") is a good one. But page one has the roughest prose in the book:
- A comma splice: "She knew him, a little, he'd been at the party."
- A missing comma: "Stepping out into the living room she found…"
- A dialect slip: "sat on the floor", in an otherwise American register.
- Two flat summary sentences mid-scene: "It was a good party, better than she'd expected."
- Blurry blocking: "The man threw himself across the room, and the door slammed". I had to reread to see that he slammed it.
- "His face changed." Changed how?

On a slow Tuesday I'd read on. On a heavy one, page one is where this gets lost.

**Chapter 1: lean request (partial).** The magic is the reason: a handwritten recipe lit like a fuse, Nana's voice coming back, "The impact sang," "The cake, somehow, was fine." Against that:
- The blob action is generic ("some sort of blob," "the behemoth's weight").
- The prose gets labored in places: "Stillness… rumbling that grew into shaking, a shaking that rocked the walls".
- Pathwell is a very familiar figure: the whimsical, ageless man who scoops up a grieving woman. His exit line is "Ready?" "No." "Perfect." That's a Doctor-and-companion setup. I'd request on concept, not prose.

**First 10% (through about line 778, the arrival at Camp in Ch4): request the full.** Chapter 3 changed my mind. "Nana's house had thirteen stairs, and by the end Elizabeth knew which ones complained" through "There was no upstairs" is the best writing in the opening by a mile. The Space Between transaction scene has real texture: a bookstore that takes future potential as payment. "If I knew that, I'd make worse decisions with more confidence." "The cookbook felt heavier, which was stupid. Missing a page should have made it lighter." Mama Baga's "Sit before you fall over politely" / "That is not a posture" told me there's a voice here.

**The full: phone call and a strong revise-and-resubmit. I'd expect to offer if the author is aligned on the revision.** I forgot I was working three times: the cookbook donation (Ch4), the museum (Ch11), and the fire and its aftermath (Ch15–16). That's more than most fulls I request. It doesn't go out at 46.5k words with this climax, though, and the length conversation comes first.

## Two-line pitch (if I take it on)

The night after her housewarming, a woman who spent years caring for her dying grandmother finds a stranger reading her diary and a monster at her door. She learns that handwritten pages hold real power, and that Nana's cookbook can heal if she's willing to spend it.

Following the charming, reckless Pathwell through a bookstore that buys futures and a traveling camp of cunning folk, Elizabeth has to decide what to keep, what to give away, and whether a man who pays with pieces of his own future gets to decide anyone else's.

## Comps and shelf

- **Shelf:** Adult upmarket contemporary fantasy, the "cozy with real grief" end of cozy fantasy.
- **Comps:**
  - TJ Klune's *Under the Whispering Door*: grief, found family, whimsy with a moral spine.
  - Matt Haig's *The Midnight Library*: the unlived-lives conceit, which here becomes a person, Shade.
  - T. Kingfisher (*Thornhedge*, *Nettle & Bone*) for the dry, kind, practical voice.
- **Market reference:** Travis Baldree's *Legends & Lattes* for low-stakes community and coffee.
- **Length reference:** Becky Chambers' *A Psalm for the Wild-Built* for a compassionate short novel.
- **Tonal reference (not a comp):** the Doctor-and-companion dynamic. Editors will see it, so name it before they do.
- **Pitch line:** *Under the Whispering Door* meets *The Midnight Library*, in a T. Kingfisher voice.

## Length

At 46.5k words it's the wrong length for its category. Adult fantasy debuts typically run 90–120k. Cozy and upmarket fantasy usually lands around 75–100k. At 46k it's a short novel, which really only fits a novella-friendly imprint, and that's a narrow and hard door for a debut. I'd aim for about 70–80k. The growth should come from specific places, not padding:
- Elizabeth's life before and after: the job, her mother (mentioned once), what she wants for herself.
- More time with Shade before his death.
- The "old job" and the cleanup/blob logic.

The book's compression is part of its charm, and the expansion has to be earned scene by scene. Confidence: high that 46k is a sales problem; moderate on the exact target.

## What I'd ask for in revision before submission

1. **Rebuild the climax so Elizabeth's choice costs her something real (Ch14–15).** The real dilemma on the page is Shade versus Milo. Right now Shade makes it for her ("Go."), and the book instead frames child-versus-diary as "the choice," which no one would hesitate over. Let her own the Shade/Milo choice, or let her see Pathwell not acting and have to act on that. Either way, cut the "That was the choice" framing unless the choice is genuinely hard.
2. **Move Pathwell's confession about Shade out of the last chapter.** He watched Shade die and didn't call for help. Put it on the page during the fire, from her point of view or his, or in the Ch16 apology. Then let the ending reckon with it. As it stands it undercuts the bookend.
3. **Grow to about 75k from the places named under Length.**
4. **Differentiate the voices and cut about a third of the quip buttons.** Mama Baga, the archivist, the shopkeeper, Stansbury, Pathwell and Shade should not all speak in the same clipped, dry two-liner.
5. **Cut the narration that states the theme**, and most of the tribunal's therapy-speak.
6. **Make the two relapses (museum Ch11, rejoining Ch14) escalate differently**, and trim the stonewalling loop in Ch6–10 by a round or two.
7. **Line-edit Ch1 and Ch5 to the standard of Ch3 and Ch11.**
8. **Compress the Ch17 restitution montage to two scenes.**

## Top ten: what I most want the author to hear, most important first

1. **The climax's central choice isn't a dilemma (Ch15).** Choosing a trapped child over a diary is not a choice readers feel. The real choice was Shade or Milo, and Shade made it for her. The whole book is about choosing, so the climax has to be the hardest choice in it, and it has to be Elizabeth's.
2. **Pathwell letting Shade die is your biggest moral fact, and it's hidden in Ch18.** It's an aside followed by a cheerful exit through a door full of geese. Either bring it into the fire scene and let it reshape the ending, or the ending reads as though the book didn't notice what it said. As it stands I didn't trust the "Perfect."
3. **46.5k words is a hard sell for this shelf.** Grow to about 75k from the places listed above, not by padding. You have the material and the world for it.
4. **Your best writing is about grief and paper. Lead with it.** Nana's thirteen stairs, the cookbook donation, the archive, three eggs, LIKED POCKETS, the dumplings from memory. Ch1 has none of that interior, and it's where agents decide. Give page one and Chapter 1 some of Chapter 3.
5. **Everyone sounds the same.** The dry, deadpan two-line exchange is your signature, and it's in nearly every conversation in the book. By Ch14 I was predicting the buttons. Give each secondary character a distinct register and cut a third of the zingers. The ones left will hit harder.
6. **Trust the scenes and stop stating the theme.** "That was the choice," "It was her decision," "The choice was ridiculous. It was still hers," the third and fourth "asked first," and Mama Baga's accountability speeches all explain what the scenes already showed. Keep the two best and cut the rest.
7. **Fix the Ch1 mechanics.** There are comma splices, a stray "sat," unclear blocking at the door, a generic blob fight, and labored rhythm in places. It's a small job worth more than anything else per hour spent.
8. **Pathwell fails the same way three times** (the prune, the museum, the rejoining) and gets told "no" and does it anyway each time. That's true to character, but the museum and the rejoining are the same beat. Either make each failure reveal something new, or merge them and use the space for Shade.
9. **Your document magic is genuinely original. The set-piece magic isn't.** Grocery lists for direction, unsent letters for longing, copies that keep the information but not the thing: that's fresh and exactly on theme. The portal whirlwind (Ch5) and the burning potential-tree (Ch3) are generic. Make every working as specific as the grocery list.
10. **Compress the denouement but keep its jewels.** Four restitution scenes say the same thing. Keep ABETH, the dumplings and the index card, "Copy the smudge," the spaewives welcome, and LIKED POCKETS in the drawer. Cut the drying rack or the wagon color, not both.

### 3. The ceiling

An 8 is clearly within reach of this book as conceived. A 9 is reachable without writing a different book. The concept already carries 9-level ideas: copies keep the information but not the thing; futures as currency; a person made from someone's discarded possibilities who insists on choosing for himself. What separates a solid 7 from a 9 is structural moral design. In a 9, the climax is the exact place the protagonist's flaw and the book's argument collide, with no exit, and every supporting voice is distinct enough to argue with the others. The manuscript is past competent. It has passages at 9, scenes at 8, and structure at about 6.5.

Books stall at this point when the author keeps polishing sentences. More line-work won't move this one. What will:
- Redesign the climax.
- Decide what the book thinks about Pathwell.
- Let Elizabeth exist before the magic arrives.
- Trust the reader with subtext.
