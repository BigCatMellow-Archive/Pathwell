# Report: the Pratchett devotee's cold read

Reader condition: cold, whole book, in order, in 400-line chunks. I opened only the brief and the manuscript. I kept notes after each chunk and this report is those notes transcribed, not revised afterward. Chapter numbers are from the manuscript. I did not keep a precise tally; roughly 25 lines made me laugh aloud, and a few hundred got a smile or a groan.

A calibration note before the detail. I came in braced for the "Pratchett-esque" blurb problem: quips with nothing under them. That is true of about the first third of this book and not true of the last two thirds. The book gets better as it goes, and the best of it is very good.

## 2. Where the humor has teeth, and where it goes soft

**Teeth.** The jokes bite when they are aimed at the same two targets the book is actually about.

1. Certainty and the person who has decided what is best for you. "I can contain it." "Quickest. Cleanest." "That seemed reasonable at the time." / "So did the ditch." "You still think the result gets to decide whether the choice was acceptable." This is Pratchett's moral register (the Granny Weatherwax and Vimes strain), and it is the strongest thing in the book.
2. What institutions do to the things people leave behind. The archive, the museum, the shopkeeper's ledger, the curator's three stacks, "Paid is not completed", "The information." / "The information.", "Specificity is expensive." This is where the book is most original. The jokes are about how records get kept, argued over and mistreated, and they are true.

**Soft.**
- **Pathwell's patter in the first act.** The wacky-wizard register ("peckish", the Chinese spot, "Home is such a permanent term. I prefer... layover", the roll call of Cunning Folk) is the stock version of the character. The book knows this. In Ch16 Elizabeth says "You don't get to use being funny to decide when this becomes easier", which is the best self-critique in the manuscript. The fix is to let the reader see the patter as armor from the start, not discover it in Ch16.
- **One voice for everyone.** Stansbury, the archivist, the shopkeeper, Mama Baga, the healer, the bartender and Shade all speak in short deadpan declaratives with a dry last line. It is a good voice. It is the same voice. When the archivist says "Specificity is expensive" and Stansbury says "It's the useful version" I could not have told you which speaker I was hearing without the tag.
- **The book repeats its own trick.** The trick is: a short dry exchange, then a single-word capper ("Fair." "Cruel." "Traitor." "Perfect." "Accounting." "Disorienting."). It works, and by Ch17 and Ch18 roughly a third of the laughs are callbacks to the book's own earlier lines ("Important distinction", "Worse", "Cruel", "It has required practice", "Disorienting"). They are well-built and they reward a reader who has stayed, but late in the book the book is mostly laughing with itself, and there is little new comic invention. The geese are the exception. See section 5 on why the geese matter.
- **Tics that wear thin.** Counted by feel, not exhaustively:
  - "laughed once" and its cousins ("Elizabeth laughed once through her nose", "Shade laughed once, without humor", "She laughed once, because the question was offensive") at least ten times.
  - The "Not X. Y." fragment: "Wet. Heavy." "Not cruel. Worse. Useful." "Not hidden. Not displayed. Available." "Not burned. Spent." Each one is good alone. Together they are a rhythm the book leans on too often.
  - Glance choreography: "Pathwell looked at Stansbury. Stansbury looked at Pathwell." and "Mama Baga looked at Elizabeth." It appears dozens of times.
  - The sock. By Ch15 the sock gag has been used about seven times. The Ch15 payoff earns it; several earlier appearances do not.
  - Three healer scenes (Ch4, Ch12, Ch16). The consent-before-touching beat is a good motif, but by the third time it is a routine.

## 3. Three funniest, three flattest

**Funniest**
1. **Ch8, the ditch.** From "Elizabeth chose the ditch" through "So did the ditch." It is the one set piece where the comedy is the plot: a person who has been managed for two chapters takes the wheel and forces the conversation. It is physical, character-driven and true, and it has a thematic rhyme ("Too late to call it an accident").
2. **Ch16, Milo and the loss sheet.** "He cheated at Hearts." / "He was losing." / "That's why he cheated." through LIKED POCKETS and "Categories are temporarily flexible." It is funny because a child is the one doing the grieving, and the archive's absurd bureaucracy turns out to be the only funeral available.
3. **Ch11, the museum.** The three-times-corrected accession card, "History has casualties", "poor boundaries", and then "Someone needed eggs." It runs from comedy to catastrophe without a join.

Honorable mentions: Ch17 "ABETH", Ch18 "RUTH LIED ABOUT THE LARD" and "NOT THAT GOOD", the Ch6 fish sequence, Ch15's sock.

**Flattest**
1. **Ch5, the portal and the school.** The circle-and-wind sequence is a blow-by-blow of vague spectacle ("A few more twists of his palm, a few flicks of his fingers, and he drew a circle"), and then the school scene is stock: the lanyard guard, "I'm the missus", the elbow, a string of "I don't know." The "I don't know" gag does get a self-aware capper ("I'm going to run out of ways to say 'I don't know'"), but it arrives after the joke has already been run into the ground. About 250 lines that could be 80.
2. **Ch10, the slap and "grande gatto."** "The next time I call you an idiot, you smack me" followed by two slaps and a nickname I could not decode. It is the one bit in the second half that is purely sitcom. The preceding peas-and-axle exchange is good; this one is noise.
3. **Ch2–3, Pathwell's first-act patter.** Peckish, the dumpling monologue, "Perfectly crappy. They aren't trying to be anything they're not." I smiled, but this is where a stranger picking up the book decides whether it is the cheap kind of whimsy. Elizabeth's crying at the curb is stronger than anything Pathwell says in these two chapters.

## 4. How this compares to the comic fantasy I know best

I am measuring against Pratchett (especially the Watch and Witches books), *Good Omens*, Adams, and early Moore. I would also put it next to T. Kingfisher's *A Wizard's Guide to Defensive Baking*, which is the closest recent book in temperature.

**What it has that those books have.**
- A moral spine. "Certainty" as the thing wrong with a well-meaning man, "ask first" as the thing right with a community. That is a Pratchett-grade idea, and the book dramatizes it rather than only stating it.
- The grief-through-objects conceit is original and I have not seen it done this well. Handwritten family records as spendable magic, the "copy is not the thing" logic, a cookbook page as a medicine that costs the original. It would be a strong premise for a serious novel and it works in a comic one.
- Secondary characters who steal scenes in the proper Discworld way: Milo, the archivist, the shopkeeper, Mama Baga, the healer. Milo especially. He is the best thing in the book.
- Real tonal range. It can make me laugh and then make it cost something in the same page, which most "Pratchett-esque" books cannot.

**What it lacks.**
- **A narrator.** Pratchett's jokes live in the narration as much as the dialogue: the aside, the footnote voice, the sentence that widens into a cosmic observation. This book has almost one (the Ch5 town paragraph, "Camp Cunnan kept being a place without asking permission" is a miss, the Ch10 "Most everyone knew everyone else, which meant most everyone knew better"). The dialogue carries nearly all the comedy, and dialogue-only comedy runs out of range.
- **A comic worldbuilding engine.** In Discworld, the setting is a joke generator: guilds, Death, the Patrician. Here the setting has great material (the Space Between's economy of potential, the cleanup creatures, the Cunning Folk) and it is mostly played straight. The "cleanup" creatures as bureaucracy of magical waste is a Pratchett-sized idea that is used only as a threat.
- **A comic antagonist.** Good Omens, Discworld and Moore all have villains who are also funny. The blobs are a generic menace ("low tide", "wet, heavy") with no personality. The real antagonist is Pathwell's flaw, which works, but it means the action sequences (Ch1, Ch7, Ch14 to 15) are the least distinctive prose in the book.
- **Adams-style invention.** There is little of the sudden absurd leap. The geese ("They have learned commerce") are the one place the book does it, and it reads like it came from a different, freer book.

So: closer to Kingfisher or Pratchett's quieter late work than to Adams or Moore. The jokes are gentler, character-first, and more consistently kind. Against Pratchett at his best it is a smaller instrument. Against most of what is sold as Pratchett-adjacent, it is better, because the jokes point at something.

## 5. Top ten: what I most want the author to hear

1. **The book is about forty percent better in its second half than its first, and the first half is where a stranger decides whether to keep going.** Ch1 to Ch7 mostly deliver smiles and a stock wizard, and Ch5 in particular sags. The laughs that make this book special start at Ch6 and Ch8. Move that energy forward, cut the school and portal sequence hard, and let the reader meet Elizabeth-with-opinions by Ch3.
2. **Your best comic material is the book's own subject: records, ownership, copies and accountability.** The accession card, DONOR PERMISSION REQUIRED, "Paid is not completed", "Categories are temporarily flexible", the Merritt cookbook. When the jokes come out of that world they land every time. When they come from generic banter (the school, the bar's pool table, the slap) they do not. Build more of the book out of the first kind.
3. **Pathwell's first-act patter is the book's weakest voice and the book's only real exposure.** Everyone else talks like a person; he talks like a wizard in a sitcom. Ch16 shows you know it. Move that knowledge earlier. Let the reader catch him using a joke to avoid a question in Ch2 or Ch3, so the later confrontation reads as a payoff.
4. **Everyone speaks in the same register.** Short, dry, last-word capper. It is a strong voice and it is one voice. Give Stansbury, the archivist, the shopkeeper and Mama Baga at least one verbal habit each that the others do not share (a longer sentence, a digression, a mistake, a different kind of dryness).
5. **By Ch17 and Ch18 the book is laughing at its own earlier lines more than at the world.** The callbacks are well-built and reward attention, but the supply of new comic invention thins out in the last quarter. Make sure each late chapter has at least one thing that is new. The geese show how.
6. **The mechanics of the magic got fuzzy where it mattered.** The tracking-by-grocery-list working (Ch10), the rejoining frame (Ch14), and "malformed pruning / unclaimed possibility" (Ch10) cost me the thread. I could not state the rules back. Pratchett's magic can be silly, but it is always consistent. Reduce it to three rules and stick to them.
7. **The theme is spoken more often than it is shown.** Characters say "choice", "ask", "decide for me", "responsibility" in nearly every serious scene (Ch9 Shade, Ch10 confession, Ch14 argument, Ch16 apology). Several of these are strong lines ("being uncertain no longer mattered to him"). Several are the author telling me what the scene already showed. Cut the explicit statements by a quarter and trust the actions: the ditch, the diary, "Say his name."
8. **The back third has three denouements.** Ch16 (the accounting), Ch17 (restitution montage), Ch18 (the Space Between visit) each end a story. Ch17 especially is a sequence of near-identical beats: Pathwell almost improves something, defers, is approved. It is charming and it is the same beat four times (the rack, the notebook, the Henry Vale letters, the yellow wagon). Trim Ch17 by half and let Ch18 carry the emotional close.
9. **The tics need a pass.** "laughed once", "Not X. Y.", the look-at/look-at choreography, the sock, the three healer scenes. None is a flaw alone. Together they are the reason the prose starts to feel patterned by Ch12. This is the easiest improvement in the book and probably worth half a point.
10. **The ending is a good echo and a soft landing.** "Are you ready?" / "No." / "Perfect." reverses Ch1 nicely. But Shade's fate is left technically open ("DIED AT CAMP") with no door left ajar and no real closure, the blobs vanish without being explained, and the last scene is a coda. The book earns a stronger final beat. See section 6.

## 6. From where it is to a 9

### 6.1 Score

**Overall: 6.5 / 10**, measured against *Good Omens* (a 9), Pratchett's *Small Gods* and *Guards! Guards!* (9s), and *A Wizard's Guide to Defensive Baking* (an 8). Confidence moderate. I could be talked up to 7 by a reader who values the back half and warmth more than I do.

| Dimension | Score | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|
| Prose | 7 | Clean, specific, often lovely (the plate on the thirteen stairs). Patterned by tics; weak in action and magic description. |
| Character | 7.5 | Elizabeth, Milo, Shade, the archivist and Mama Baga are real. Pathwell is the least fresh and the most necessary. |
| Plot and structure | 5.5 | The first half meanders; the antagonist is generic; the climax is strong; the back third repeats itself. |
| Humor | 7 | High hit-rate in Ch6 to 18, thin in Ch1 to 5. Teeth when about records and certainty; callback-heavy late. |
| Emotional payoff | 8 | The strongest dimension. The cookbook, the diary, Shade, the pepper and dumplings all pay off. |
| Originality | 7 | The records-as-magic conceit is genuinely fresh. The blobs, the cleanup system and the chosen-guide structure are familiar. |
| Ending | 6.5 | The Ch18 "Say his name" is strong and the final echo is neat, but the book ends on a coda with its central mystery tied off by silence. |

**What each rung looks like for a book like this.**
- **A 6:** Likable and well written with a strong premise; the jokes are charming but could be anywhere; the middle plateaus; the ending is pleasant.
- **A 7:** A distinct comic voice and at least one memorable set piece; emotional beats land; flaws are structural, not scattered; a stranger would finish it and recommend it with caveats.
- **An 8:** The comic voice is consistent from page one; the world generates jokes and plot; the antagonist matters; the ending lands on a beat only this book could have written; you could hand it to a stranger without caveats.
- **A 9:** All of an 8, and the book's central idea is embodied in its form, so a reader remembers the idea and the jokes as one thing. The best chapters are not exceptions to the rest of the book; they are the book's average.

### 6.2 The gap to a 9

Roughly in order of points. They are not strictly additive.

1. **Rebuild the first act so the book is itself by Ch3.** (about +1.0) *Where:* Ch1 to Ch7, especially Ch2, Ch5 and Ch6's front half. *What:* cut the portal-and-school sequence to a fraction; move the Ch6 "appletini" energy and the Ch8 ditch sensibility into the first act (Elizabeth with opinions early); make the blob a puzzle with a face, not a menace. *Size:* medium to large. *Type:* **fix**.
2. **Add a comic narrator and make the world a joke engine.** (about +0.8) *Where:* throughout, concentrated in Ch3 to Ch5 (the Space Between economy, the Cunning Folk) and Ch9 to Ch10 (the road, the diner). *What:* let the narration widen into an aside a handful of times per chapter; take the cleanup creatures, the shopkeeper's economy and the Camp rules and play them as institutions with absurd internal logic. The Ch5 town paragraph and the geese show you can. *Size:* medium. *Type:* **lift**.
3. **Dramatize Pathwell's flaw earlier, and cut the stated theme.** (about +0.6) *Where:* Ch2 to Ch5 (seed), Ch10, Ch14 and Ch16 (trim). *What:* one early instance where we see him choose for someone else and be insufferably right, so the Ch14 refusal of every "no" reads as a tragedy of character, not a plot lever. Then cut 20 to 30 percent of the speeches that name the theme. *Size:* medium. *Type:* **fix** (cut) and **lift** (seed).
4. **Reshape the back third around one real ending.** (about +0.5) *Where:* Ch16 to Ch18. *What:* compress Ch17 to one or two beats, keep "Say his name" as the emotional climax, and give the last chapter an obstacle rather than a coda. Decide Shade's fate deliberately. Make the geese the last set piece rather than a tag. *Size:* medium. *Type:* **fix**.
5. **Make the magic consistent and say less about it.** (about +0.4) *Where:* Ch10, Ch14 and the mentions in Ch5 and Ch7. *What:* three rules, stated once, used the same way every time. Remove "malformed pruning" and "unclaimed possibility" or define them in a sentence. *Size:* small to medium. *Type:* **fix**.
6. **Differentiate voices and give each secondary character one non-deadpan habit.** (about +0.3) *Where:* every scene with Stansbury, the archivist, the shopkeeper and Mama Baga. *Size:* medium. *Type:* **lift**.
7. **Line-edit the tics.** (about +0.3) *Where:* the whole book. *What:* thin "laughed once", the "Not X. Y." fragments, the look-at choreography, and half the sock gags. *Size:* small. *Type:* **fix**.

### 6.3 The ceiling

A 9 is within reach of this book **as conceived**. It would not take a different book. It would take a more even version of this one. Its best chapters (6, 8, 11, 13, 16) are already 8s and 9s locally. What holds the whole at 6.5 to 7 is the distance between those chapters and the others.

What a 9 has that a solid 7 does not: a voice that is recognizably the book's from the first page; a premise that generates both jokes and plot; a climax that could only happen in this book; and, most of all, consistency, so a reader cannot point to a "weak" stretch. A 7 has a few great chapters. A 9 has no chapter you would skip.

On the improvement curve: this manuscript is past "competent" and sits on the plateau where the problems stop being errors and become choices. The work that moves a book off that plateau is not polishing sentences. It is deciding what the book is for, cutting everything that does not serve it, and concentrating the strongest material. Here that means: the book is about what people owe the things they leave behind and the people they decide for. Everything in the first act that is not that (the portal, the school, the first Pathwell patter) is a candidate for the cut. Everything that is that (the archive, the museum, the diary, the ditch) should be the shape of the book from Ch1.
