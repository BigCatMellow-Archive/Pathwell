# Revision guide: the three highest-value items from "the gap to a 9"

## GUIDE 1. Put the caregiving on the page (+1.0)

### 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

Chapter 3 holds nearly all the caregiving in the book, and it does so in one mode: summary narration. The camera sits above the years and reports them. The grammar is "she learned":

> "The few months became years. Elizabeth learned the pill organizer with the days stamped on the lids. She learned which pharmacist didn't make you wait and which neighbor would sit with Nana for an hour if you brought her tomatoes. She learned to carry a plate up thirteen stairs without spilling it, and to carry it back down mostly full."

Look at what the paragraph is made of. It is a list of competencies, each about one object, each succeeding. Nothing goes wrong, nothing costs anything, no one is in the room, and no day of the week is named. The details are excellent, but they are details of a job done well. Nobody in the paragraph is tired, angry, short of money or touching a body.

The death is the same. It is two sentences of summary:

> "The last week, Nana didn't come downstairs at all. Elizabeth read to her from the cookbook because novels made her impatient, and Nana corrected the measurements with her eyes closed."

Then: "Afterward there was nobody to carry anything up the stairs for." The death happens in the white space between those sentences.

Two things follow. First, the most memorable lines in the flashback are the ones that drop into scene ("You ate the pepper." / "The pepper was the best part, Lizzy." and "Then you made them wrong."). The book already shows you that scene mode is where it comes alive. Second, the withheld material is exactly what a reader who has been through caregiving is waiting for: the body, the money, the other family, the thing she got wrong, and the relief.

That is why this reads as a 7 and not a 9. A 7 gets the true details right. A 9 shows the costs those details were paying for.

### 2. The technique

1. **Build a carer's inventory before drafting.** Make four columns: *Body*, *Money*, *Time*, *Other people*. Write six specifics in each column, ones a stranger would not guess. For example: what Nana does with the pills when she thinks you're not looking; what the pharmacy co-pay was; what time you stopped being able to sleep through the sound of her turning over; which relative calls, and what they ask. Put "I would never put this in a book" things in the list. You can cut them later, but write them first.
2. **Choose three scenes from the inventory, not ten.** Pick one each from *Body*, *Decision*, and *Death or the days after*. Three full scenes will do more than a dozen paragraphs of summary.
3. **Write each as a clock scene.** Fix a day, an hour and a room. Name the object in her hand. The pill-organizer lid already gives you the day ("TUE"). Run the scene for about ten minutes of story time. If you can't say when it is, it is still summary.
4. **Give Nana a want, and let her win one exchange.** Nana wants something specific (the pill not swallowed, the stairs, the window open). Elizabeth wants the opposite. Nana must land one blow ("Then you made them wrong" is the model).
5. **Plant one act Elizabeth wouldn't want read aloud.** A lie, a sharp word, a shortcut, a moment of wanting it to be over. Do not comment on it afterward. This step is what makes Guide 2 possible.
6. **End on an object or an action, not on a feeling.** The reader should leave holding the spoon, the lid, the stair, not a sentence about what it meant.
7. **Enter and exit the way Chapter 3 already does.** Chapter 3's flashback begins when she faints from the bookshelves' pressure and ends with "When her eyes opened, they were heavy." Use the same device: a body sensation in the present plot opens a memory and the present plot pulls her out. Keep each memory to 300 to 600 words.
8. **Choose the entry points from what the present scene already hands you.** The diner grits (Ch. 9); the healer's "Can I touch it?" (Ch. 12); the bowl of chicken and dumplings (Ch. 16); the spoon (Ch. 17); "MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH." (Ch. 18). Each already points at a memory.
9. **Write the death once, in full, and decide afterward where it goes.** The best place I can see is Chapter 16, at the night scene where "Elizabeth cried for Nana. / For Shade's green jacket and excessive pockets." Right now Nana gets one line there. A short death scene a few paragraphs earlier would make that line land.

### 3. A model

- **Roz Chast, *Can't We Talk About Something More Pleasant?*** Chast writes the decline of her parents with the money, the cluttered apartment, the facility, and her own irritation and avoidance all on the page. The mechanism is that comedy and dread sit side by side without cancelling each other, and the narrator is as unflattering to herself as to anyone. A scene can be funny, and the funny does not let her off.
- **Joan Didion, *The Year of Magical Thinking*.** She keeps her husband's shoes because he will need them if he comes back, and she says so flatly. The mechanism is irrational behavior reported at the same temperature as everything else, with no explanation attached.

### 4. Worked example

Current text: the Chapter 3 paragraph quoted above. Here is the same material at a moment of override, in scene mode.

**ILLUSTRATION** (a sketch of the move, not final wording):

> Tuesday, and the lid said TUE, and Nana had the evening pill in her cheek like a child.
> "Swallow it."
> "It tastes like a penny."
> "It's been a penny for two years."
> Nana held the water but not the pill. Elizabeth counted the stairs she had left in her legs. Thirteen up, thirteen down, twice since dinner.
> She did what the pharmacist had said not to do, ground it into the applesauce, and said it was cinnamon.
> Nana ate two spoonfuls. Looked at her. "You're a bad liar, Lizzy."
> "Then eat it."
> She ate it. The spoon scraped. On the landing Elizabeth put her forehead against the cool wall and felt, for one clean second, nothing but relief: it was done, and she had won.

What each choice does:
- **Cut:** the verb "learned" and the list of competences. The sketch trades the general ("she learned the pill organizer") for one night.
- **Added: the clock.** "Tuesday" and "TUE" pin the scene to a day, and the lid from your original detail is doing the work.
- **Added: the body.** The pill in the cheek, and the legs counting stairs, put two bodies in the room.
- **Added: Nana wins.** "You're a bad liar, Lizzy" is her blow, in the register of "Then you made them wrong."
- **Added: the override.** Grinding the pill into applesauce is the unlovely act. It also plants what Guide 2 needs.
- **Added: "she had won."** The ugliest word in the passage is the last one. I did not add a reflective sentence after it, because that would explain it.
- **Kept:** the thirteen stairs, which is your detail and your anchor.

### 5. How to check it worked

- **The date test.** For each caregiving passage, can you say what day and roughly what hour it is? If not, it is still summary.
- **The body count.** Highlight every word about a body (skin, cheek, spoon at a mouth, hands, breath). The flashbacks should have several per page. Chapter 3 currently has almost none.
- **The aloud test.** Read the scene to someone who has cared for a relative. Ask what they remember afterward. If the answer is a physical thing (the spoon, the lid), it works. If it is a feeling you stated ("she felt guilty"), the scene is still reporting.
- **The embarrassment test.** Is there one line you'd rather not read aloud? If not, you haven't gone far enough.

### 6. Traps

- **Sentimentality.** Nana turns into a saint, or the scene ends on a hug. Let her be sharp, vain, wrong and right.
- **Flashbacks as exposition.** If a memory stops the present plot for more than a page, shorten it or move the entry point. Never open a memory without a present trigger.
- **Overwriting.** The more painful the material, the plainer the sentences should be. Chapter 3 is strong because it is spare.
- **Trauma dump.** Three scenes, not a catalog.
- **Spending the small detail.** Don't use up "listening for someone upstairs" in a bigger scene. It works because it is small.

**Protect:** the Chapter 3 paragraph on the stairs and the plate (trim only the "learned" structure if you must; keep the content), "'We'll see' in the voice she used for weather and doctors," the shrinking handwriting, "listening for someone upstairs. / There was no upstairs," the pepper that comes back at the diner and in the dumplings, and "Afterward there was nobody to carry anything up the stairs for."

## GUIDE 2. Turn the care/tyranny lens on Elizabeth (+0.75)

### 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

Every time the theme appears, it points outward. Elizabeth is the detector and Pathwell is the specimen. In the van she analyzes him (Ch. 10):

> "And paying for it yourself made you feel like the method was yours to choose."

At the museum she hears the flaw in his voice (Ch. 11):

> "Elizabeth heard the work van inside the sentence. Quickest. Cleanest. I can handle it."

The camera sits inside Elizabeth, but her interior is almost entirely observation of others. She is never wrong in a way that costs. Her errors are comic (the ditch) or tactical (the dagger), never moral. Whenever she does something paternalistic, the text slides past it. The best example is in Chapter 13:

> "You could stay here," Elizabeth said. "Mama Baga wouldn't mind. Milo needs someone to beat at cards. When Pathwell comes, you don't have to talk to him. I could—"
> "Elizabeth."
> She stopped.
> "That's your plan," Shade said. "Let me find mine."
> She didn't say anything else.

This is the seed of the whole fix. She arranges another person's future out of kindness, he calls it, and the book moves on. Within a chapter or two Shade is dead. The text never lets her notice.

This reads as a 6 or 7 because the moral conflict is external. The reader can side with Elizabeth at no cost, so the theme asserts instead of tests. A 9 makes the heroine the case study.

### 2. The technique

1. **List Pathwell's tells, with line numbers.** For example: "Quickest. Cleanest." (Ch. 10); "I can contain this" (Ch. 11, 14); "You're not going with him" (Ch. 8); answering before the question; "Muscle memory." Make it a one-page list.
2. **For each tell, write Elizabeth's version.** Ask: where did Elizabeth say or do the equivalent, to Nana, to Shade, to anyone? You have already written two: grinding the pill (Guide 1) and "You could stay here... I could—" (Ch. 13). Find two or three more in the existing text, such as the sling she tears off after the healer says "You are not using this arm today" (Ch. 15). That one is defiance, but it is also her deciding for her own body against care.
3. **Choose three echo points and no more.** One plant (Nana, Guide 1), one slip in the present (Ch. 13, already there), one recognition (Ch. 16 or 18). Plant, slip, recognition.
4. **Make her hear herself without saying the parallel.** She should never think "I'm just like him." The recognition is a quoted fragment from earlier ("I could—") surfacing at the moment someone else says something similar. Let the reader do the matching.
5. **Withhold the absolution.** In the recognition scene she does not confess to Pathwell and he does not forgive her. She tells no one, or tells only the reader.
6. **Add one honest cost to her speech in Ch. 16.** After "I am not going to make your guilt useful," give her one line that fails her (she overreaches or is unfair). Right now the speech is flawless.
7. **Rewrite the van diagnosis in Ch. 10 as a question or a half-formed guess.** "And paying for it yourself made you feel like the method was yours to choose" is a therapist's summary. A tired woman in a stopped van would say something rougher and less complete.

### 3. A model

- **Kazuo Ishiguro, *The Remains of the Day*.** Stevens narrates with immaculate dignity while his father is dying upstairs and he keeps serving. The mechanism is that the narrator's formal diction is the evidence against him, the reader sees what he cannot, and the theme is tested on the person telling the story. Nothing is announced.
- **Didion again.** She replays the night he died, tests her own decisions, and does not absolve herself. The self-scrutiny is part of the prose, not a verdict.

### 4. Worked example

Current text: the Chapter 13 exchange quoted above, followed in Chapter 18 by Pathwell saying "Shade" because she asks. Here is the recognition applied there.

**ILLUSTRATION** (Ch. 18, after "Shade," he said; a sketch, not final wording):

> Elizabeth nodded once, the way you do with a receipt. Then she was somewhere else: the card table, her own voice. You could stay here. Milo needs someone to beat at cards. She had arranged his whole future in four sentences and called it kindness. He'd had to say her name to make her stop.
> She had not told Pathwell this. She did not tell him now. It would have let him off, a little, to have company.
> "Say the rest," she said instead. "What you did after."
> He did. She listened to every word without once finishing his sentences, and it cost her, and she made a note of what it cost.

Explanation:
- **Added: the quoted echo.** "You could stay here. Milo needs someone to beat at cards" is her own Chapter 13 line returning. The reader matches it with no help.
- **Added: "called it kindness."** This is the one place the sketch names the problem, and it names her act, not his.
- **Added: refusing to share the guilt.** "It would have let him off, a little, to have company" is the ugly motive. She doesn't absolve him, and she doesn't use her own fault to soften his.
- **Cut:** any line like "I'm just like him." The sketch never makes the comparison.
- **Kept:** the existing "Say his name" beat, so the scene you already wrote still carries its weight.
- **Distrust:** the last line ("she made a note of what it cost") is the one I'd rework. It risks being a lesson. A plainer ending is that she listens and doesn't interrupt.

### 5. How to check it worked

- **The swap test.** Take any sentence where Elizabeth judges Pathwell. Swap the names. If the sentence is still true (she has done the same thing), the theme is tested. If it becomes absurd, she is only the judge.
- **The count.** Count the scenes in which Elizabeth is corrected by someone and does not win. Right now there are almost none (Shade at the card table is the best). You need at least three, and at least one must not be funny.
- **The reader question.** Ask a reader, "Who did Elizabeth hurt?" If the answer is "no one," revise.

### 6. Traps

- **Hypocrite heroine.** If she merely turns out to be as bad as Pathwell, the book turns cheap. Her fault should be smaller, more loving and more forgivable than his, and still real.
- **Wallowing.** One recognition, not a chapter of guilt. A single memory that surfaces and is left alone does more.
- **Over-explaining the parallel.** The moment a character says "that's what you did too," the move is dead.
- **Damaging the fire.** Chapter 15 is heroic and should stay heroic. Don't retroactively taint it.
- **Making the care team suspect.** The healer's "Can I touch it?" is the book's clean example of good care. Don't undermine her to make a point.

**Protect:** "I went to Milo." / "That's not what I did." (Ch. 18); "I don't know if I forgive you." (Ch. 16); "Drive" (Ch. 7), the clearest picture of being used; the ditch (agency); and the healer's "Can I touch it?" / "Because it's your shoulder."

## GUIDE 3. Trim the middle (Chapters 5 to 10) and make every scene turn (+0.5)

### 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

The middle runs on one engine: a question, a deflection, a button joke. The school search is the clearest case (Ch. 5):

> "Which room is he in?"
> "I don't know."
> "What does he teach?"
> "I don't know."
> "Does he even work here?"
> Pathwell sighed. "You know, I'm going to run out of ways to say 'I don't know' if you keep asking the same question."

The bar has the same shape (Ch. 6):

> "Order your own."
> "We're family."
> "That has never entitled you to my fish."

Both are funny. Neither changes anything. Nobody wants something different at the end of the exchange than at the start, and the reader learns nothing new about Elizabeth or the mystery. The paragraphs are stacks of one-line replies, the camera stays outside Elizabeth, and almost every exchange ends on a rhythmic button.

The withholding is also a repeated device. "Not here." "Later." "I don't know yet." become the plot's switch. Yet the real answer, as Chapter 10 shows, is that Pathwell often doesn't know. So the dialogue spends pages pretending to conceal what is really an absence.

That is a 6 because the pacing depends on charm. A 9 would make me unable to find a scene that doesn't change someone.

### 2. The technique

1. **Make an index card for every scene in Ch. 5 to 10.** My count is about two dozen: the portal field (line 1271), the closet and bell (1305), the hallway search (1363), Ken and "the missus" (1383), the maroon car and foam weapons (1426), the town and the drive (1545), the bar order and fish (1606), Stansbury's quiet talk (1725), the mirror (1789), the parking lot blob (1916), the car argument (2132), the ditch (2237), Shade arrives (2331), the radio (2628), the diner (2677), the road walk (3004), the brothers' walk and slaps (3019), Stansbury's house and the letter working (3094), the van (3320 to 3623).
2. **Write one line on each card in a fixed form:** *"At the start X wants A; at the end the reader knows B / X has lost C."* If you can't fill it in, mark the card red.
3. **Cut or merge every red card.** Candidates, in my reading: most of the school search (keep the "missus" bit), the "Bank it" slaps and "grande gatto" (3063 to 3091), the second and third rounds of the bar's brother banter, and the tab-paying exchange (keep one line of "I tip historically").
4. **Ration the withholding.** Allow "Not here" or "Later" twice in the whole middle. Everywhere else, let Pathwell show the not-knowing in action, such as reading door names "like prices."
5. **Do a button pass.** In any exchange, if three lines in a row end on a joke, cut the middle one. Keep the joke that reveals a character.
6. **Add one line of Elizabeth's interior to every scene that survives.** It should be a thing she notices that no one else would (the muddler's thud before the hand came down; the dagger she keeps checking). The interior is what makes the cut scenes feel like they belong to her.
7. **Target roughly a third.** Do it by cards first, then by line.
8. **Keep a gem list and check it after every pass.** (See Protect.)

### 3. A model

- **Susanna Clarke, *Piranesi*.** The withholding is structural: the narrator genuinely doesn't know, so there is no character saying "later." Each short journal entry gives one image or one fact, and the reader assembles the rest. Pace comes from the form, not from deflection.
- **Terry Pratchett and Neil Gaiman, *Good Omens*.** The comic duo scenes almost always end with a fact the plot needs, so the banter is also the delivery mechanism.

### 4. Worked example

Current text: the Chapter 5 search quoted above. Here is the same stretch collapsed.

**ILLUSTRATION** (a sketch, not final wording):

> "Which room is he in?"
> "I don't know," Pathwell said, and kept walking. He was reading the names on the doors the way he'd read her diary: like prices.
> By the third hallway she had stopped asking. It was Friday, she had missed her meeting, and she was following a man through an elementary school in someone else's blazer. She could hear the sentence she'd say to HR. It had no verbs she could defend.
> "Can I help you?" said the man with the lanyard.
> Pathwell's hand went to the back of his coat.
> "There you are, honey," Elizabeth said, already walking.

Explanation:
- **Cut:** three of the four "I don't know"s and the "run out of ways to say it" joke. One "I don't know" is enough, and the joke is a button.
- **Added: "like prices."** This echoes Ch. 3, where she sees him "turning the pages of her diary like he was pricing it." It carries his character and her suspicion in a few words.
- **Added: her missed meeting and the HR sentence.** It is the one piece of her ordinary life in the scene and it keeps her stakes alive.
- **Changed:** the hand in the coat is now the trigger. Elizabeth improvises because Pathwell's solution would be worse. That makes her the one who acts, which is already true in your "missus" scene.
- **Kept untouched:** the whole "I'm the missus" exchange with Ken, which is already a scene that turns (she takes control and he becomes the prop).

### 5. How to check it worked

- **The turn test.** Per scene, finish the sentence "At the start she/he wants ___; at the end ___ has changed." One blank left means cut or merge.
- **The skip test.** Remove a scene and read the next. If the next still makes sense, the scene was optional.
- **The five-sentence summary.** Summarize Ch. 5 to 10 in five sentences. If you need many more, the middle is doing too much. If you can't think of five, it's thin.
- **The cover-the-tags test.** Cover the speaker tags on a banter page. If you can't tell who is speaking, the voices are too alike (and the exchange can probably be shorter).
- **The count.** Count button jokes per page. Over three is a signal.

### 6. Traps

- **Cutting the book's own good bits.** The middle holds many of the best small moments.
- **Cutting setup.** The foam dagger, the museum route, the cleanup creatures and the first sight of Shade all pay off later. Check each cut against what returns in Ch. 11 to 15.
- **Cutting the relief.** The humor is also pacing, and a middle with no levity would be airless. Cut repeats, not jokes.
- **Flattening the brothers.** Their bar scene is where we learn how they love each other. Compress it; don't erase it.
- **Cutting without adding.** If you trim the middle and add nothing, the book becomes a thinner version of itself. That is why the order below matters.

**Protect:** the ginger ale ("I don't want an appletini") and "What do you normally have?"; "He looked fine, which, she had recently learned, was a posture"; the brothers moving each other's beer; "Drive"; the ditch; Shade's diner scene including the extra-pepper grits; the grocery lists (MILK / ONIONS / ASPIRIN / STAMPS / SHOE POLISH); and Stansbury's "It makes the cost mine to approve."

## Revision order

1. **Guide 3 first (trim the middle).** It is the cheapest job and gives you a lean skeleton, so you can see where the new material would go and where the book already breathes. It also tells you the true length of Ch. 5 to 10 before you add anything.
2. **Guide 1 second (caregiving scenes).** With the middle trimmed you have room and know which present-day moments (the diner, the healer, the bowl, the spoon) can serve as entry points. Writing the three scenes also produces the override you need for step 3.
3. **Guide 2 third (the lens on Elizabeth).** It depends on the other two. The plant is the pill scene from Guide 1, the slip is already in Ch. 13, and the recognition needs the leaner Ch. 16 to 18 to land. Doing it last means you are placing three small echoes into a stable structure, not building them blind.
4. **A last, short pass:** delete the stated theme (the narrator's "That was the choice" lines, "You are confusing care with tyranny," "Asking. / Terrible habit"). Do this last because several of those lines are scaffolding that you can only judge once the scenes underneath them carry the weight.

## One practice exercise for this week

**Rewrite the Chapter 3 flashback (lines 409 to 437) around one clock scene of 400 to 600 words, using the pill-and-applesauce situation or one of your own from the inventory.**

Keep the sentences "Nana's house had thirteen stairs, and by the end Elizabeth knew which ones complained" and "listening for someone upstairs" outside the scene as bookends. They are not in question.

Success looks like this:
- A reader can say the day and the hour.
- At least three body details appear (not feelings).
- Nana wins one exchange and it is funny or sharp.
- Elizabeth does one thing she would not want read aloud, and the scene does not comment on it.
- No sentence says what the scene means.
- The last line is an action or an object.
- A friend who reads it can tell you, a day later, what the spoon or the lid was doing. If they remember a physical thing and not a feeling, the approach works.

If that scene works, the same method will carry the death scene, the diner memory and the later echo in Ch. 13 and 18. If it doesn't, the problem is most likely in step 5 of Guide 1 (the unlovely act), and that is the thing to push on.
