# A revision guide: from a 7 toward a 9

This guide covers the three items worth the most in my "gap to a 9":

1. Pathwell's inside, and the weight of what he did to Shade (about +0.75)
2. Explaining the inciting incident (about +0.5)
3. Separating the voices and cutting the quip endings (about +0.5)

I read only the manuscript to write this, and every quotation from it is exact. The two passages marked ILLUSTRATION are sketches that show a technique. They aren't proposed wording.

## Guide 1: Give Pathwell an inside, and let the Shade decision land

### 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

**Where the camera is.** The narration stays with Elizabeth for about 90% of the book. It is in Pathwell's head only twice: the mirror scene (Ch. 6, about 30 lines) and the walk to Stansbury's house and the basement (Ch. 10). Every major decision Pathwell makes happens while the camera is outside him and looking at his face.

- **Ch. 14, the third pin:** "Pathwell closed his eyes. Elizabeth knew what he was going to do before he did it." His decision is shown as an expression Elizabeth reads. We get her certainty about him, not his certainty from inside.
- **Ch. 15, Shade being taken:** "He was close enough to hear Shade calling. His face was turned toward him." Thirty lines later: "His face was turned toward the place where Shade had been." The book's darkest act is two descriptions of a face. Neither tells the reader it is a choice.
- **Ch. 18, the confession:** "I thought that if the creature took him, it would be finished. The draw. The whole business." / "I could have called someone. I didn't." The event comes as reported speech, four sentences long, in a scene that ends with geese about 60 lines later.

**What the van scene (Ch. 10) is made of.** Pathwell's moral history comes out through Elizabeth's questions: "Who?" / "What did they ask you to do?" / "Did they ask you to prune?" He is passive in his own confession, and the most important sentence in it is hers: "And paying for it yourself made you feel like the method was yours to choose." So the book's diagnosis of its central character is spoken by someone else.

**Why this reads as a 7 and not a 9.** The reader gets the most important moral event in the book as information, after the fact, not as experience. Worse, the three restitution chapters (16–17) were read under a false belief: we watched Pathwell copy the smudge and thought we knew everything he was carrying. The Ch. 18 reveal makes those chapters retroactively thinner without making them richer. A 9 makes the reader feel complicit in a character's reasoning before the verdict arrives. Here the reader is only ever a spectator, then a juror.

### 2. The technique

In the report I said "an inside" and "less likeable." Here is what those mean at the desk.

1. **Write down, privately, what Pathwell believes at his four decision points.** These are: buying the books (Ch. 3), the quarantine letter (Ch. 11), the third pin (Ch. 14), and not calling for help (Ch. 15). For each, write one sentence that starts "He believes this is right because..." Then write one sentence that starts "What he refuses to look at is..." If you can't write the first sentence persuasively, you don't know him well enough yet.
2. **Choose one or two of those four for a short section from inside his head.** Use the third pin or Shade's death, or both. Keep each under 300 words. Don't add more, because the book is Elizabeth's.
3. **Inside those sections, write his reasoning as an argument that holds up on its own terms.** Each step should follow from the last. "Less likeable" means this: the reader can follow the logic and half-agree with it, all the way to a wrong act.
4. **Let him borrow the vocabulary of the people who corrected him.** By Ch. 15 he has heard "stop deciding," "ask first," "not yours to choose." A compulsive man doesn't drop that language. He uses it to justify himself. Have him reason with their words.
5. **Withhold the verdict, not the reasoning.** Never have the narration say "he chose not to help." Let him notice the "clean outcome," and then show his hand staying where it is. The reader delivers the verdict.
6. **After his reasoning, no joke and no remorse in the same section.** End on a physical fact. Remorse belongs to a later scene, so the reader sits with the act before he does.
7. **Make the Ch. 15 plant visible enough to suspect.** If you don't add a section from his head, add one concrete observable act: he opens his mouth and closes it, or his free hand is within reach of the brass and doesn't move. Elizabeth can't see this because she's in the archive, so give it to the man from the bucket line, who can mention it later, or to Stansbury.
8. **Give the Ch. 18 confession one consequence on the page.** Decide who else learns it: Stansbury, Mama Baga, or the archive ledger. Or decide deliberately that no one does, and show Elizabeth choosing to carry it. Either way, something has to change in the next scene because of it. The cheapest strong version: Elizabeth's "Do you want to come?" now has to be asked *knowing* this, and the narration should register that it costs her.

### 3. A model

**Le Guin, *A Wizard of Earthsea*: the summoning on Roke Knoll.** Before Ged calls up the dead, Le Guin's narration gives the reader his motive in plain terms: pride, and the specific goad of Jasper's contempt. We are inside his wanting. The act follows. Then come the consequences, which are permanent and borne by others too: the Archmage Nemmerle spends his life sealing the breach, and Ged is scarred and long helpless. The mechanism is that motive is given before the act and cost after it, with no joke in between.

**Ishiguro, *The Remains of the Day*: Stevens's father dying.** Stevens's father is dying upstairs while Stevens goes on serving at the conference dinner downstairs. Stevens narrates the evening as a professional triumph, and the reader sees the horror in what he leaves out. The mechanism is narration that is coherent and sincere, with one omission the reader can see. That is exactly Pathwell's "certainty" from Shade's diner speech: "That he had reached the point where being uncertain no longer mattered to him." Ishiguro shows how to put that on the page from the inside.

### 4. A worked example

**Current text (Ch. 15):**

> Pathwell lay on his side by the collapsed frame, among the loose pale threads. He was close enough to hear Shade calling. His face was turned toward him.

**Applied:** a section break after Elizabeth steps into the archive doorway, then a short passage inside Pathwell's head, then back to Elizabeth at "Her diary lay open in the entrance aisle."

> ILLUSTRATION
>
> The threads were his. He could feel each one go out as the thing took it.
>
> Shade was shouting. *Help.* The word had his own voice in it, which was unfair.
>
> Pathwell could lift a hand. The man from the bucket line was closer; the man from the bucket line was already pulling. He had been told, all morning, to stop deciding things for other people, and here at last was a thing he could decline to decide.
>
> If it took Shade, the draw would end. The blobs would stop confusing them. Nobody would have to be rejoined against his will.
>
> It was, he noticed, the cleanest outcome available.
>
> He noticed that he had noticed it. He kept his hand on the dirt.
>
> Then the word stopped, and the pull in his arm went slack, and it was the quietest his body had been in years.

**What each choice does:**

- **"The threads were his."** This anchors us in his body and his ownership. His first thought is about his own spilled possibility, not about Shade. That ordering is the character.
- **"which was unfair."** This is his only flash of feeling, and it's self-pity, not compassion. That's the "less likeable" move, and it's done with one clause, not a confession.
- **"The man from the bucket line was already pulling."** This is a real, plausible reason to do nothing, and it comes first. Readers half-agree with it.
- **"here at last was a thing he could decline to decide."** This is step 4: he turns Mama Baga's and Elizabeth's lesson into his justification. It's the most damning sentence in the passage, and it contains no judgment.
- **"Nobody would have to be rejoined against his will."** He uses Shade's own refusal as the argument for letting Shade go. It's grotesque, and from inside it sounds coherent.
- **"cleanest outcome."** "Cleanest" is his word from the van in Ch. 10 ("And cleanest, I thought."). The reader who catches the echo feels the trap close.
- **"He noticed that he had noticed it."** This is the one moment of self-awareness, and it changes nothing. That's what compulsion looks like on the page.
- **"He kept his hand on the dirt."** The act is a non-act, shown physically, with no verdict (step 5).
- **The last line is relief, not remorse** (step 6). The reader feels sick, and he doesn't, yet.
- **What I cut:** the outside view of his face. It's no longer needed.
- **Effect on Ch. 18.** The confession changes from new information to the moment he says out loud what we watched. That frees the Ch. 18 scene to be about Elizabeth's response and its consequence, which is where its weight should go.

**The trade-off.** You lose the surprise in Ch. 18 and gain suspense across Chapters 16–17. Every restitution scene is then read knowing what he hasn't told anyone. I think that's the better bargain for this book, because it turns the gentle denouement into something with tension in it. If you'd rather keep the surprise, use step 7 instead (a visible outside plant) and skip this passage.

### 5. How to check it worked

- **The verdict test.** Give Chapters 14–15 to a reader. Afterwards, ask: "Did Pathwell do anything wrong in Chapter 15?"
  - With the inside passage, they should say yes and be able to say what.
  - With the outside plant, they should say "I'm not sure, something was off."
  - After Ch. 18 they should say "I suspected," never "Where did that come from?"
- **The one-sentence test.** For each of the four decision points, can a reader state in their own words what Pathwell thought he was achieving? If they can only state what Elizabeth thought of him, he still has no inside.
- **The count.** Count the words in each section from Pathwell's head. If any is over about 300, or there are more than three in total, you're drifting into a two-hander and diluting Elizabeth.
- **The read-aloud check.** Read each inside section aloud. If any sentence sounds like a lesson ("he realized he had been wrong to..."), cut it.

### 6. Traps

- **Explaining him into sympathy.** A backstory wound ("he had once lost a brother...") turns compulsion into trauma and lets him off. Give him reasons, not excuses.
- **Remorse inside the same passage.** This is the commonest failure. The writer flinches, and the section ends "...and he hated himself for it." That gives the reader the absolution they should be withholding.
- **Too many trips inside his head.** Every one costs Elizabeth's ownership of the book. Use two at most.
- **Making the confession scene redundant without giving it a new job.** If you add the inside passage, rewrite Ch. 18 so its weight is Elizabeth's decision about what to do with the knowledge, not the knowledge itself.
- **Passages to protect:**
  - The Ch. 6 mirror scene ("like hearing your own voice on a recording and recognizing every word without recognizing the man who said them"). It's already the right size.
  - Pathwell's absence during the cookbook donation in Ch. 4 ("Elizabeth waited for Pathwell to appear in the doorway... He didn't."). Don't go into his head there.
  - "Say his name." (Ch. 18).
  - "His right hand hung easy at his side. Nothing pulled at it anymore." (Ch. 18). With the inside passage, this last line becomes devastating. Don't explain it.

## Guide 2: Explain the inciting incident, and signpost what you're leaving open

### 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

**What's withheld, and who holds the question.** Ch. 1 gives us a stranger at Elizabeth's party: "She assumed someone from work had brought him. She had seen him peering into an open box of old notebooks once, before somebody asked for more cake." When she finds him he's reading her diary, and he goes straight for the cookbook: "The old one, the handwritten one with notes. I know there's one around here somewhere." Ch. 3 has her think he was "turning the pages of her diary like he was pricing it." In Ch. 16 he apologizes for "Your grandmother's book, which I came looking for and would have spent without asking."

Nothing in between explains how he knew there was a cookbook, why that party, or why that night. Elizabeth interrogates him relentlessly: "Which prune?" four times, "Tell me what you did," "Whose decision was that?" As far as I read, she never asks "Why were you at my party?"

**The book can answer questions when it wants to.** The first blob's mystery is set up in Ch. 4 ("It was already at the door before you used the cookbook?"), carried through Chapters 5–9, and answered in Ch. 10 ("Came for me. Or for him, following me."). Shade's origin is openly flagged as unknown ("Does anyone?" / "Not that I've found."). Both work.

**Why this reads as a 6 or 7.** Readers tolerate withholding when a character holds the question for them. The prune is a mystery because Elizabeth keeps asking about it. The party question is a hole because nobody asks it. The reader carries it alone, and by the end feels it as an oversight, not a design. The same goes for "A kitchen table. Somebody's hands around a cup." Two characters bring it up, and nobody (not Elizabeth, not the narration) signals whether we're meant to learn whose it was.

### 2. The technique

1. **Write a private causal spine.** This is not for the book: one paragraph for each link, in plain language.
   - Why Pathwell was at that party.
   - How he knew about the notebooks or the cookbook.
   - What he meant to do with the cookbook.
   - What the kitchen-table job was, and whose table.
   - Why the first blob arrived that night.

   You don't have to reveal all of it. You do have to know it, because what you know leaks into his behavior consistently.
2. **Keep a question ledger.** Go through the draft and list every question a character or reader would ask. Mark each one with one of three labels:
   - **ANSWERED**, with the chapter;
   - **DEFERRED ON THE PAGE**, meaning a character says it's open;
   - **DELIBERATELY UNKNOWABLE**, meaning a character says nobody knows.

   Any question that has none of these labels is a hole. My count of holes: the party, the kitchen table, the neighborhood that changed in Ch. 2, the mother in Ch. 6, "two hundred years," and the gold chain. The first two matter most.
3. **Give the inciting question to Elizabeth.** She should ask it early, in Ch. 2 or 3, while she is still asking everything. He evades it in character, she notices the evasion, and she asks again at a point of leverage (the van in Ch. 10, or the apology in Ch. 16).
4. **Answer in layers.** Give the surface answer in the same chapter the question is asked: something true, small and in character. Give the deep answer by the climax, in a way that reframes the surface answer without contradicting it.
5. **Build the answer out of rules the book already has.** The book has already established that Pathwell can sense charged paper ("The ache in the paper reached him from across the table," Ch. 10) and that he hunts records for Camp ("Did Camp Cunnan ask you for all of this?", Ch. 3). An answer drawn from existing rules feels discovered rather than invented.
6. **For anything you leave open, say so in a character's mouth.** Model it on the Shade-origin exchange in Ch. 10, which already does this well. If Pathwell truly doesn't remember whose table it was, make that forgetting cost him on the page: "I'd stopped keeping the faces" should hurt someone.

### 3. A model

**Gaiman, *Neverwhere*.** Richard stops for a bleeding girl on the pavement. The surface reason comes at once: she's hurt and he's decent. Door's reason for being in his London (her family has been murdered, she's fleeing Croup and Vandemar, and she opens doors blindly) comes within the first act. The deepest layer, who ordered the killings, is held back for the climax.

The mechanism is three answers to "why did this start here": what happened, why there, and who's behind it. Each layer is given at a different stage and each reframes the one before. Because the reader is never left without some answer, they trust the book with the rest. The Pathwell–Elizabeth set-up is close enough to Richard and Door that this is the most direct model you have.

**Gaiman, *The Ocean at the End of the Lane*, as a secondary model.** The lodger's death in the car at the end of the lane is a concrete, explained trigger, and Lettie names its consequence early. The strangeness grows from a cause the reader holds firmly.

**What each choice does:**

- **She asks right after "That's it."** She's in interrogation mode, so the question costs nothing to place and the reader's question gets a holder at once (step 3).
- **"Somebody said there'd be cake."** The evasion comes first, in Pathwell's register, and it's funny. It also echoes the ELIZ cake, so the joke works for the scene.
- **"Nobody from my office knows you."** She catches the evasion. That teaches the reader she will keep tracking it.
- **"I go where the paper is good" / "Some people can hear them across a room."** A surface answer built from rules the book already has (step 5): his sense of charged paper in Ch. 10, and his scavenging for Camp in Ch. 3. Nothing new is invented.
- **"Yours was shouting."** This turns a mechanism into a feeling. Grief-soaked paper is loud, which quietly links the cookbook's power to Nana's death and Elizabeth's own grief.
- **"It's most of one."** This signposts that a deeper layer exists (step 6). The reader now knows the question is open on purpose. The deep answer belongs in Ch. 10 or Ch. 16: why that night, and what he meant to spend it on.
- **What I cut:** nothing. This is an addition of about eight lines, so the inciting incident costs almost no length to fix.
- **A caution:** the answer stays within "Then you were never the point" (Shade, Ch. 9). She was the owner of a loud book, not a chosen one. That line must keep working.

### 5. How to check it worked

- **The two-sentence pitch test.** Using only facts on the page, can you say why the story starts at Elizabeth's party? Example: "Pathwell senses strongly charged records and scavenges them for Camp. Elizabeth's grandmother's cookbook was loud enough to draw him to her party." If you need a fact that isn't in the manuscript, it isn't fixed.
- **The ledger count.** After revising, no question that a reader would ask three or more times should be missing all three labels.
- **The reader check.** At the end, ask a reader: "Was there anything you expected to find out and didn't?" Every item they name should be something you meant to leave open, and some character should have said so.

### 6. Traps

- **Making Elizabeth special.** The easiest answer ("her bloodline," "the cookbook was unique") would destroy two of the book's best things: Shade's "Then you were never the point," and the ordinariness that makes MORE IF THEY'RE SICK hit. Keep her incidental. What's special is the paper, and the paper is special because it was ordinary and used.
- **The info-dump.** Don't answer everything in the van. One layer per scene, at most.
- **Answering the unknowables.** How spilled possibility became a person should probably stay open. The book is right to keep that mystery. Fix the holes, and leave the deliberate mysteries alone.
- **Over-explaining the kitchen table.** If you decide it was someone the reader knows, plant it once and let the reader make the connection. A character announcing it would cheapen it.
- **Passages to protect:**
  - The Ch. 3 Nana flashback ("Nana's house had thirteen stairs..."). Don't turn it into exposition about the cookbook's power.
  - The first blob's resolution in Ch. 10, which already works.
  - Shade's "Then you were never the point."

## Guide 3: Separate the voices and cut the quip endings

### 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

**What the dialogue is made of.** Long stretches are single-line paragraphs with almost no beats or tags. The camera sits outside everyone and just records talk. Most exchanges follow one shape: a statement, a contradiction, then a short, dry, declarative correction that wins. Here are four different speakers:

- **Mama Baga (Ch. 4):** "I'm fine." / "That is not a posture."
- **The archivist (Ch. 4):** "That's not very specific." / "Specificity is expensive."
- **Milo (Ch. 4):** "No one accused you." / "You were going to."
- **The healer (Ch. 12):** "I wasn't." / "You were going to."

The same line appears word for word from a boy and a healer. Each of the others could be spoken by any of the four.

**Scene endings.** Here is how scenes and stretches of scenes often end:

- Ch. 6: "Consistency," said Pathwell. / "Stagnation," said Stansbury. / "Branding."
- Ch. 12: "Briefly? Very." / "Excellent." / "Afterward, less." / "Strong sales pitch."
- Ch. 14: "You're listing." / "That's a nautical term." / "You are doing it on land. Sit."

Every one is under eight words. Nobody hedges, nobody misunderstands, and nobody is wrong except Pathwell.

**Why this reads as a 7.** A voice is mostly a blind spot: what a person can't help noticing, and what they can't hear. When every speaker has the same rhythm and wins every exchange, nobody has a blind spot, so nobody is distinct. When every scene ends on a winning line, the reader learns that the book will always rescue the moment, and so stops feeling the moments. Pratchett is a 9 at this because his people lose arguments to each other.

### 2. The technique

In the report I said "one voice." Here is what to do about it.

1. **Make a voice sheet for every recurring speaker:** Mama Baga, the archivist, the healer, Milo, Stansbury, Pathwell, Shade and Elizabeth. For each, write five things:
   - typical line length;
   - what they notice first in a room;
   - one verbal habit;
   - one thing they never say;
   - one thing they're wrong about.

   These are examples, not prescriptions:
   - **Healer:** asks procedural questions, counts, never jokes first, and is wrong about how much people will obey her.
   - **Milo:** long run-ons, literal, keeps score, wrong about fairness.
   - **The archivist:** nouns and categories, precise, wrong in thinking paperwork protects people.
2. **Do the tag-cover test.** Take three pages of Camp dialogue, delete the tags and names, and see whether you can still assign every line. Rewrite any line that could belong to two people, using that speaker's sheet.
3. **Do a button audit.** List the last line of every scene and section. Label each one QUIP, IMAGE, ACTION or SILENCE. Aim for no more than a third QUIPs.
4. **Sort the quips into "deflects" and "reveals."** "I have not reached the axle emotionally" reveals Stansbury, so keep it. "Branding." only extends a rhythm, so cut it. Keep the quips that reveal, and cut most of the ones that deflect.
5. **Let one speaker per scene lose.** Someone misunderstands, is overruled, or doesn't get the joke. A straight man makes the funny line land.
6. **Add beats.** Every four or five lines of dialogue, give the reader a physical action or object. This slows the ping-pong and moves the camera into a body.

### 3. A model

**Pratchett, *Wyrd Sisters*.** The three witches are told apart by what each wants from a conversation.

- **Granny Weatherwax** wants to be right in as few words as possible.
- **Nanny Ogg** wants to enjoy herself, so she digresses into family, drink and songs.
- **Magrat** wants to be taken seriously, so she's earnest and gets overruled.

Magrat losing is the mechanism. Her earnestness is the straight line that makes Granny's dryness cut, and it's also why Magrat is lovable. Mama Baga is already your Granny. You don't yet have a Nanny or a Magrat.

**Gaiman, *Neverwhere*: Croup and Vandemar.** One partner is ornate and talks endlessly. The other is monosyllabic and literal. They are told apart by word count and by literalness alone, so you'd never confuse a line. Your Camp needs that kind of difference in line length.

**What each choice does:**

- **Cut "I will tie you to the wagon" / "Is that allowed?" / "No." / "I will ask you very firmly not to" / "That seems less effective" / "It is what we have."** That's six lines of Camp ping-pong in which the healer and Mama Baga both perform the house wit. It's pleasant, and it's interchangeable.
- **Added counting under her breath, and "Cold cloth every hour. If the fingers go white or cold."** This is the healer's own voice: procedural, numerical, specific. Nobody else in Camp talks in measures.
- **"Not Mama. Me."** This gives her a territory and a pride, a small blind spot (she wants to be the one asked). Now she's a person, not a delivery system for dry lines.
- **"pulled the knot tight enough to hurt, and didn't apologize"** is a beat (step 6) that shows her character through her hands.
- **Kept "The healer... waited."** This is the strongest beat in the original. It echoes Ch. 12 ("looked at her until the answer became less automatic"), and it's the theme done as an act.
- **Kept Elizabeth's furniture line.** She's earned a joke after the fire. The fix isn't to remove humor; it's to stop every speaker competing for it.
- **"The healer didn't smile."** She loses the comic exchange by not playing (step 5). That makes Elizabeth's line land harder, and it makes the healer the straight man the book doesn't have.
- **Ending on "already counting."** The ending is now an ACTION, not a QUIP (step 3), and it returns to her voice-sheet habit.
- **The result:** about the same length, one fewer speaker, two distinct voices instead of three interchangeable ones.

### 5. How to check it worked

- **The tag-cover test,** before and after, on three pages each from Ch. 4, 12 and 16. You're aiming for every line being assignable.
- **The button count.** Count QUIP endings per chapter. If more than a third of section endings are still quips, keep going.
- **The read-aloud check.** Read a Camp scene aloud and listen to rhythm only. If every exchange sounds like a tennis volley of short lines, something is wrong. Each character should have a different breath length.
- **The loser check.** In each scene, can you name one person who didn't win an exchange? If it's always Pathwell, that's not a voice. It's a punching bag.

### 6. Traps

- **Draining the humor.** The goal is fewer and better jokes, not none. If a scene goes grim after your pass, you've cut the revealers along with the deflectors.
- **Tags instead of voices.** Making Milo always say one thing, or Mama Baga's bracelets always click, gives characters props, not voices. A voice is about what they notice and want, not a catchphrase.
- **Making one character the designated earnest one, and then mocking them.** Pratchett loves Magrat. Your straight man must be right sometimes.
- **Cutting world-humor while chasing dialogue-quips.** The cannon, the sock and ELIZ/ABETH are your best comedy and aren't dialogue at all. Leave them.
- **Passages and lines to protect:**
  - Stansbury's lines: "I have not reached the axle emotionally," "My axle is in a drainage culvert. I'm evaluating several positions," "You inhaled like an advertisement."
  - Milo's "Are you Pathwell?" / "No." / "Good."
  - "His sock had finally stayed up. Elizabeth hated that detail immediately."
  - "Archive burned. Categories are temporarily flexible." This one is a quip that carries grief, which shows the real test is reveal versus deflect, not joke versus no joke.
  - Mama Baga's "Same arrogance in a better coat." and "Then don't."

## Revision order

**First: the causal spine and question ledger (Guide 2).** This is structural, and it decides what Pathwell knows and wants from page one. You can't write his reasoning (Guide 1) until you know why he was on Elizabeth's floor. The ledger also tells you which scenes need new lines, so you know where the new material will go before you write any.

**Second: Pathwell's inside (Guide 1).** With his motives fixed, write the one or two passages from his head and the Ch. 15 plant, and rebuild the Ch. 18 consequence. This adds and moves material. That's why it comes before the line pass: there's no point polishing dialogue in scenes whose shape is about to change.

**Third: voices and buttons (Guide 3).** This is line work across the whole book, and it should be last, because it also has to cover the new material from steps one and two. It's also the step most likely to damage what works, so do it when the structure is settled and you can judge each cut against a finished shape.

## One practice exercise for this week

**Rewrite the Ch. 10 van confession,** from Elizabeth getting into the van ("She walked past him to the van, opened the side door and got in.") to "Camp," as she picks the destination. This one scene tests all three guides at once.

- **Guide 2:** Elizabeth asks why Pathwell was at her party, and the scene delivers the deeper layer of that answer, or explicitly defers it.
- **Guide 1:** without leaving Elizabeth's head, Pathwell has to make his confession more actively. He volunteers at least one thing she didn't ask for, and the narration shows one physical tell of his certainty. Cut "And paying for it yourself made you feel like the method was yours to choose." Let the reader get there.
- **Guide 3:** Stansbury gets two or three lines that only he could say. The scene ends on an action or an image, not a quip.

**What success looks like:**
- The scene is no longer than now, and probably shorter.
- A reader who hasn't seen the old version can explain, in their own words, what Pathwell did wrong. If they produce something close to the line you cut, the scene now acts out what it used to state.
- The reader can say why Pathwell came to the party, or knows the book is holding that back on purpose.
- With the tags covered, every line is assignable.

If that scene works, you have a template for Chapters 14 and 16, the other two places where the book argues instead of acting.
