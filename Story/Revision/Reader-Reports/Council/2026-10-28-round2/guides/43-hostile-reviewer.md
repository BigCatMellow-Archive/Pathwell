# GUIDE 1: Make the "no" cost something (Ch 12–14)

## 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

**Who speaks.** At the frame in Ch14, one man argues one side and five people argue the other: Shade, Mama Baga, Stansbury, Elizabeth and the archivist (who sends Milo away). In the roughly 260 lines from Shade's "No." (line 5445) to just after the pin goes in, I count **16 lines that are a bare refusal**: "No," "Stop," "Then no," "Don't." The chorus has one note.

**What Pathwell's case is made of.** Abstract nouns, stated once, never shown:

> "If I leave this unresolved, the draw stays. The blobs stay confused. He stays tied to me and I stay tied to him."

and later:

> "If I stop, the cleanup problem remains." / "Yes." / "The draw remains." / "Yes." / "You remain tied to me." / "Yes."

Every item on his list is either a cost to Shade, who has already accepted it ("I am the person living with it"), or a cost to Pathwell himself. Nothing on the list touches Camp, Milo or Elizabeth. The "Yes / Yes / Yes" rhythm is the author's verdict in sound: the other side answers calmly because it has nothing to lose.

**Where the camera has been.** Ch12–13 show Camp at its safest: stew, a coffee grinder, Hearts, onions. The draw shows up only as Shade's hand dropping onions (line 5027). No blob comes near Camp before the frame. So when Pathwell says the problem "remains," the reader has seen no problem.

**Why that's a 6 or 7, not a 9.** The scene's tension rests entirely on "will he ignore everyone?" That is suspense about a character's flaw, not a dilemma. In a 9, the reader is split at the moment of choice. Here nobody is, so the climax confirms what we already believed rather than testing it.

## 2. The technique, at the desk

1. **Write Pathwell's best case, for yourself, as a list of concrete harms.** Each harm gets a named person, a place and a time: who gets hurt if Shade stays tied to him, where, and how soon. Use only things that are or will be on the page. If you can't produce three, the scene can't work yet.
2. **Put one of those harms on the page before the decision.** In Ch13, during the Hearts game, let the draw bring the cleanup into Camp, and let it touch someone who isn't Shade or Pathwell. One beat, not a battle. It must be clearly *because* Shade is there.
3. **Make the "no" side pay out loud.** Each opposer has to say what they're accepting by refusing. Mama Baga accepts the risk to her people and has to say so to someone who'd bear it. Shade has to face the question of whether he should leave Camp.
4. **Split the chorus.** Give at least one ally a moment of partial agreement with Pathwell, said or shown: Stansbury, the healer or the archivist. Then let that person still say no, or hesitate. The reader needs someone to stand beside.
5. **Ration the refusals.** Each speaker gets one "no," at the point where it costs them most. Delete the repeats. Target fewer than six bare refusal lines from the start of the frame to the pin.
6. **Let the choice happen at its most defensible moment.** He should drive the pin when the case for it is strongest, right after a fresh harm, not after he's been told "Stop" a fourth time. That way the reader half-wants him to.

"Give him a case" means steps 1, 2 and 6. "Make the no cost something" means steps 3, 4 and 5.

## 3. A model

**Susanna Clarke, *Jonathan Strange & Mr Norrell*.** Mr Norrell's sin is Pathwell's sin: he decides for other people. He bargains with the gentleman with the thistle-down hair to bring Emma Wintertowne back from the dead and promises away half her life without asking her. Clarke makes his reasons sincere and partly defensible. He is trying to restore English magic and prove it useful to a country at war. She also lets his caution about dangerous magic be proved right later. The cost falls on a third party we've met, at length, and suffer alongside. The mechanism: **the controlling man's rationale is real, and the bill goes to someone else.** That's why the reader can neither dismiss Norrell nor forgive him.

## 4. Worked example on this manuscript

**Current text (Ch14):**

> Pathwell looked back at Shade. "If I stop, the cleanup problem remains."
> "Yes."
> "The draw remains."
> "Yes."
> "You remain tied to me."
> "Yes."

**ILLUSTRATION.** It assumes a new Ch13 beat, of your own invention, in which the cleanup came out of the tree line during Hearts and blackened Milo's arm before Stansbury burned it off. The wording below is a demonstration, not a proposal.

> Pathwell didn't look at Shade. He looked at the cloth on Milo's arm.
> "It came for him because he was sitting beside you."
> Shade's hand opened toward him. He let it.
> "I know."
> "It will come back. Here. You feel it the way I do."
> "Yes."
> "Then tell me where the children sit while you finish your hand."
> Mama Baga said, "That is mine to decide."
> "Then decide it out loud. Tell him." Pathwell nodded at the archivist, who had not let go of Milo's shoulder all day.
> The archivist said nothing. Elizabeth found she couldn't either.
> Shade looked at Milo for a long time. "Don't ask her," he said. "Ask him whether he wants me gone."
> Milo, who had heard all of it, didn't answer.

**What each choice does:**
- **Cut the "Yes / Yes / Yes" triad.** Its rhythm tells the reader the argument is already won.
- **Replaced "the cleanup problem" with a hurt child we know.** An abstraction became a person. This is Clarke's move: the bill goes to someone else.
- **Pathwell addresses the people who would pay**, not Shade. His case is now about Camp, which makes it a real case.
- **Mama Baga is forced to own the risk aloud.** Her "no" now costs her something. That's step 3.
- **The archivist and Elizabeth go silent.** Two people on the "no" side wobble. That's step 4, and it's where the reader gets permission to half-agree.
- **Shade turns the consent question around: ask Milo.** This keeps the theme, but puts it in a sharper place. The book's own principle (ask the person it affects) now points at Shade's staying, not just Pathwell's working.
- **Milo doesn't answer.** The dilemma stays open. Nobody settles it for the reader.

## 5. How to check it worked

- **The defence-attorney test.** Write one paragraph arguing that Pathwell should drive the pin, citing only events on the page. If it needs facts the reader hasn't seen, step 2 isn't done.
- **Count.** Bare refusal lines from the start of the frame to the pin: currently 16, target under 6. Characters who show any sympathy for his side: currently 0, target at least 1.
- **The beta question.** Ask readers, "At the moment he drove the third pin, did you want him to, even a little?" If nobody says "partly," it isn't fixed yet.

## 6. Traps, and what to protect

- **Making him right.** If the harm is so big that rejoining is obviously correct, you've written an ends-justify-means book, and Shade becomes a problem to be solved. The harm should make the choice *understandable*, not correct.
- **A cheap child-in-peril beat.** If Milo's injury exists only to load the argument, readers will feel handled. Keep it small, and make it the draw's fault, not the plot's.
- **Turning the cozy book into an action book.** One beat, not a siege.
- **Stealing Elizabeth's agency.** Her "Let it fail" (line 5685) must still matter. Her silence in the illustration is a moment, not a surrender.
- **Protect:** Shade's "I am the person living with it," he said. "No." (line 5507); Shade's "Don't make him the reason." and the exchange around it; Mama Baga's "Same arrogance in a better coat."; Shade's "You still think the result gets to decide whether the choice was acceptable." These are the moral spine. The fix should make them harder to say, not unnecessary.

# GUIDE 2: Shade's arc and his death, on the page (Ch 2, 6, 8, 15, 18)

## 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

**Where the camera is when it matters.** Elizabeth turns from Shade to the archive door (line 5802). Everything that decides Shade's fate happens behind her. We last see him like this:

> Pathwell lay on his side by the collapsed frame, among the loose pale threads. He was close enough to hear Shade calling. His face was turned toward him.

That is an image of possibility, not of action. Then the death is reported second-hand by an unnamed man:

> The man looked at the empty place beside him. "We couldn't get him loose."
> He swallowed. "It took him."

**What's withheld, and for how long.** Pathwell's inaction, the most serious moral fact in the book, is held back until Ch18, about 1,600 lines later, and revealed in a page and a half over coffee: "I could have called someone. I didn't." The reader has no image to attach it to. So it lands as information, not memory.

**Shade's arc in screen time.** He first appears around line 2391 (about 30% through) and is gone by line 6011. The mirror scene (Ch6) gives away *what* he is before he arrives, so his arrival has no surprise. Meanwhile, nothing before Ch6 hints that he exists at all.

**Why that's a 6 or 7.** Readers grieve what they witness. A reported death in a book about the cost of choosing for others lets everyone off: Pathwell, Elizabeth, the reader. The Ch18 confession is strong writing in the wrong place, a revelation where there should be a reckoning.

## 2. The technique, at the desk

1. **List every major turn and mark it "seen" or "reported."** Any turn that changes a relationship must be seen by the point-of-view character. Shade's death fails this today.
2. **Make Pathwell's inaction one visible physical act.** Not interior thought, because we're in Elizabeth's eyes. Something she can watch: a mouth that opens and closes, a hand that doesn't reach, a call that doesn't come. The act must be readable without explanation.
3. **Let the victim see it too.** Shade must witness the choice made against him. That turns a misfortune into a betrayal between two people.
4. **Compromise the witness.** Elizabeth sees it and still goes to Milo. Her choice remains right, but it's now made in full knowledge. That's what gives her Ch18 line "I didn't help him either" its weight.
5. **Rewrite Ch18 as a conversation about meaning, not a reveal of fact.** She already knows what he did. The scene is about whether he'll say it, and whether he'll say Shade's name. Keep "Say his name."
6. **Seed Shade's mystery, not his identity, early.** Look for one or two places in Ch1–5 where an unexplained presence could be Shade, without telling the reader what he is. An existing beat could serve: Ch2, line 218, "A shadow filled the space." in her apartment window, which the book never explains. Then consider whether the Ch6 mirror scene gives too much away. It may work better from Elizabeth's view, as an oddity she notices in Pathwell, rather than from inside Pathwell's head.

## 3. Models

- **Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein*.** In Orkney, Victor tears apart the half-made female companion *while the creature watches through the window*. Shelley stages the creator's choice against the created being in front of its victim, and that single witnessed act drives the rest of the novel. That's steps 2 and 3 exactly.
- **Ursula K. Le Guin, *A Wizard of Earthsea*.** Ged's pride releases a shadow that hunts him. The shadow is present from early in the book as a threat, though its nature stays hidden, and the resolution comes when Ged chooses to face it and names it with his own name. This is the useful contrast for Pathwell's rejoining frame. Le Guin's rejoining is chosen by the person who caused the split, facing what he made. Pathwell's is forced onto the made being. Le Guin also shows how to seed a double's presence for a whole book without explaining it: that's step 6.

## 4. Worked example on this manuscript

**Current text (Ch15):**

> Shade was still trying to pull his leg free. He looked toward the archive again, then back at her, and with his free hand he pointed her at the door.
>
> Go.
>
> Elizabeth turned toward the archive door.

**ILLUSTRATION** (a demonstration of the move, not final wording):

> At the threshold she looked back once.
> Stansbury was coming along the far side of the wagon with a bucket in each hand. Ten feet from Pathwell. Ten feet from the frame. Pathwell saw him. Elizabeth watched him see him.
> Pathwell's mouth opened.
> Shade, on one knee now, the black to his hip, turned his head toward that open mouth as if he already knew what it was going to say.
> Pathwell closed it.
> Stansbury went past with the water.
> Shade looked at Pathwell a moment longer. Then he looked at Elizabeth, lifted his free hand and pointed her at the door.
> "Elizabeth!" Milo shouted.
> She went in.

**What each choice does:**
- **The camera stays with Elizabeth, but she turns around.** One look back puts the decisive act in frame without breaking point of view. That's step 1.
- **The inaction is a mouth opening and closing.** It's physical, legible and needs no interior explanation. It matches the Ch18 confession ("I could have called someone") but makes it something she saw. That's step 2.
- **Stansbury passes within reach.** The rescue was possible, which makes the failure a choice and not bad luck.
- **Shade watches the mouth close.** Now it's a betrayal between two people, as in *Frankenstein*. That's step 3.
- **Kept the existing "pointed her at the door."** It was already the best thing in the scene. Placed after the betrayal, it becomes grace: Shade lets Elizabeth go to the child even as he's abandoned.
- **Ends on "She went in." with no narrator verdict.** I deliberately didn't add a "That was the choice"-style sentence. Her knowing the cost is enough. That's step 4.
- **Later, the bystander's "It took him." can stay**, but now it confirms what she saw rather than replacing it.

## 5. How to check it worked

- **The seen/reported table.** For every turn in Ch14–16, mark seen or reported. The death and Pathwell's role in it must both be "seen."
- **The Ch18 test.** Does the Ch18 conversation reveal any new *fact* about the fire? It shouldn't. It should reveal only what he's willing to say. If a reader learns something new about what happened there, the fact belongs in Ch15.
- **Read aloud, back to back:** the Ch15 death beat, then Ch18's "Say his name." If Ch18 hurts more because of what we saw, it worked. If it reads as an announcement, it didn't.
- **The seeding test.** Ask a beta reader after Ch5: "Is anyone else in this story you haven't met?" A faint "maybe" is the goal. A confident "Pathwell's double" means you've over-seeded.

## 6. Traps, and what to protect

- **Melodrama.** Don't lengthen the death or describe the blob's work in detail. The power is in one shut mouth.
- **Unredeemable Pathwell.** A witnessed betrayal can sink the ending. The Ch17 restitution and the Ch18 "Say his name" have to carry more weight, so don't cut them. Look for one gesture of his that faces this, not just chores.
- **Drowning Elizabeth in guilt.** She chose the child, and the book should keep that right. She knows the cost. She isn't punished for it.
- **Over-seeding.** The mirror scene already risks giving away the twist. Seeding should add mystery, not answers.
- **Protect:** "He still owes me a hand." / "SHADE — DIED AT CAMP." / "LIKED POCKETS." / Mama Baga's "Person is the word I have for that." / the Hearts game and "Pockets." These are why Shade's death matters. Don't trim them to make room.

# GUIDE 3: Make the prune concrete and shorten the stall (Ch 5–10)

## 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

**The question ladder has no rungs.** The "which prune" question is raised on line 2150 ("Since the prune that went wrong?"). It's asked or pressed at lines 2154, 2245, 2249, 2261, 2371 and 2489. It's answered at line 3425, about 1,270 lines later, roughly a sixth of the book. Meanwhile there are seven pure deferrals: "Later." (1830), "Not here." (1856, 2080), "Drive," (2160), "We don't know that it matters yet." (2251), "Not while you're driving." (2263), and a second "Later." from Mama Baga (5275). **Between asks, the reader learns almost nothing new.** The only new information is Shade's fragment at the diner (Ch9):

> "I remember the edge of one. A kitchen table. Somebody's hands around a cup. His certainty."

**The payoff has almost no nouns.** The confession in the van (Ch10):

> "I don't remember."
> "I'd done it a lot of times, Elizabeth. People with problems. I'd stopped keeping the faces." He turned the bag of peas over. "I remember a kitchen table. Somebody's hands around a cup. I remember being sure."

There's no name, place, date or problem, and no consequence to anyone but Pathwell and Shade. It repeats the diner fragment almost word for word, so the reveal *adds nothing to what Shade already told us*.

**Unanswered questions on the page.** Why was Pathwell at Elizabeth's party, hunting for a handwritten cookbook (Ch1)? He later says, "Your grandmother's book, which I came looking for and would have spent without asking." (line 6581). Stansbury's "Was it?" about "her door" (line 1482) is left hanging. The prune is the natural place to answer these, and it doesn't.

**Why that's a 6 or 7.** Withholding works when each delay pays out a piece and the final reveal changes how earlier scenes read. Here the delays are stalls and the reveal is a thesis statement. The book knows it's stalling and dramatizes Elizabeth's frustration with it. That's clever, but the reader is still the one being stalled.

## 2. The technique, at the desk

1. **Write the prune as a full scene for your eyes only:** who the client was (name), where (room, objects), when, the problem, what Pathwell did, what it bought and who paid. Make it a page.
2. **Choose the fact that recolors something already on the page.** Test candidates against the existing open questions: why he was at the party; why the first blob was at *her* door before any working ("Was it?"); what Shade is made of. A prune connected to one of these pays off three scenes at once.
3. **Build the ladder.** List every ask (the seven lines above). Give each one new concrete piece: a place, then an object, then a name, then a cost. Never a bare refusal twice in a row.
4. **Cap the stalls at two.** Keep the two that do the most character work. "Drive," is one: it powers the ditch. Cut or convert the rest into partial answers.
5. **Make Shade's fragments nouns from that scene.** He already has a room in Prague, Celia who hated pears and a loose fourth stair. Let one of them belong to the prune, so the diner and the van confession talk to each other instead of repeating.
6. **Retire "I don't remember."** If forgetting the faces is meant to be damning, make it concrete: he remembers the cup but not her face, and that's the confession. Better still, let him remember exactly one face and say the name.

## 3. Models

- **Donna Tartt, *The Secret History*.** The prologue tells you the narrator and his friends killed Bunny. The suspense moves from *what* to *why and how*. Lesson for this book: you can give away the fact of the prune early and keep the meaning for later. Withholding meaning is drama; withholding facts is often just delay.
- **Kazuo Ishiguro, *The Remains of the Day*.** Stevens evades and rationalizes his past for the whole novel, but Ishiguro still gives the reader the concrete scene: two Jewish maids, Ruth and Sarah, dismissed on Lord Darlington's orders, and Miss Kenton's fury at it. The evasion is in Stevens's telling, never in the events themselves. Lesson: a character can avoid his sin while the book is specific about it. Names make the failure undeniable.

## 4. Worked example on this manuscript

**Current text (Ch10):** the passage above, from "I don't remember." to "I remember being sure."

**ILLUSTRATION.** The facts here are placeholders, not proposals; the point is the density of nouns.

> "Who?"
> Pathwell was quiet too long. "A woman named Ada Carrow. A farmhouse kitchen. Oilcloth on the table. A cup she wouldn't drink from." He turned the peas over. "Her son had gone into the river and come out wrong. Not drowned. Wrong."
> "And she asked you to fix him."
> "She asked if he could come back. He could have. Slowly. With her. Years." He looked at the windshield. "I didn't ask if she wanted the years. I paid for a faster way, out of my own pocket, so nobody would have to."
> "Did it work?"
> "He came back exactly as he was before the river. Everything after it, I cut away." A pause. "I left before she noticed what was missing."

**What each choice does:**
- **Cut "I don't remember" and "I'd stopped keeping the faces."** Those lines protected Pathwell, and the author, from specifics. That's step 6.
- **Added a name, a room and objects.** "Ada Carrow," oilcloth, the cup. The cup links to Shade's diner fragment, so the two scenes now talk to each other instead of repeating. That's step 5.
- **The sin is an event, not a theme statement.** He chose speed for someone else and took her choice away. The theme is still there, but now as something that happened. That's the Ishiguro move.
- **The cost lands on a named third party**, the mother, who lost years she might have wanted.
- **"Everything after it, I cut away"** hints at what Shade might be made of, the excess that was cut away. That's one way to tie the prune to step 2's open questions. You'll choose your own.
- **"I left before she noticed"** leaves a forward thread, someone still out there, without stalling.

## 5. How to check it worked

- **The ladder table.** Make two columns: each line where the question is asked, and what new fact the reader gets there. Any row reading "nothing" gets cut or rewritten. Then count bare refusals, currently seven; target two.
- **The noun test for the reveal.** It must contain at least one proper name, one physical object and one consequence to someone who isn't Pathwell or Shade.
- **The recolor test.** Name two earlier scenes that read differently after the reveal. If you can't, step 2 isn't done.
- **The diner/van test.** Read Shade's diner fragment, then the van confession. The second should *add* to the first, not repeat it.

## 6. Traps, and what to protect

- **The info-dump.** A concrete prune can become a lecture on the magic system. Keep it to people and objects; the mechanics can stay vague.
- **Overshadowing Elizabeth's grief story.** If the prune is too sensational, a dead child or a catastrophe, it'll outweigh Nana. Keep it domestic: kitchens, cups, a mother.
- **Killing the ditch.** The ditch in Ch8 is the payoff of the withholding. Shorten the stall, but keep enough of it, "Drive," especially, to motivate the crash.
- **Answering everything.** Let one thread stay open after the reveal: what Shade is, exactly.
- **Protect:** the whole Ch8 ditch scene, "Elizabeth chose the ditch."; "You don't get to make me the part of this you explain around." (Ch10); Shade's "Ask him what he chose." (Ch9); "I have not reached the axle emotionally."

# Revision order

1. **Guide 3 first: decide what the prune was.** Everything else rests on this fact. It determines what Pathwell believes about himself (his case in Guide 1), what Shade is made of (Guide 2), and possibly why Pathwell was at the party and the first blob was at her door. Write the private one-page scene before touching the manuscript. Then redo the ladder in Ch5–10.
2. **Guide 1 second: build the dilemma.** Once you know what Pathwell did and why, you can write his best case in his own logic. That's when you add the Ch13 harm, rewrite the Ch14 frame scene and thin the chorus. You can't stage his inaction well until you know how strong his argument was, because the inaction is that argument's dark mirror: the same logic, used to let Shade go.
3. **Guide 2 third: stage Shade.** With the prune and the dilemma in place, you can seed Shade early, stage the witnessed death in Ch15, and rewrite Ch18 as a reckoning about meaning. Ch18 comes last because it depends on everything before it.
4. **Then the voice pass (item 4).** Each of these rewrites adds new dialogue, so separate the voices after the structure stops moving.

**What success looks like:**
- Bare refusal lines fall from 16 to fewer than 6, and each remaining one costs its speaker something.
- At least one ally visibly half-agrees with Pathwell before refusing.
- Pathwell's argument refers to at least one concrete fact from the prune and one from the Ch13 harm.

If the scene passes those tests, the approach works for this book, and Guides 2 and 3 can be done with confidence.
