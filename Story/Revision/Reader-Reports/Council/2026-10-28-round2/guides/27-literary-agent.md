# Working guide: three revisions from 7 toward 9

## Guide 1: Make the climax Elizabeth's choice, and make it cost her (Ch15)

### 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

The book has told us what Elizabeth's flaw is. She follows men who sound certain. Ch8 says it outright: "Only then did she realize she was following another man who sounded certain." The climax is where that flaw should be tested. Here is how the climax is built now.

**The deliberation paragraph is an inventory, not a decision.**

> "Her right hand had found the foam dagger behind her waist. It had split at the museum and been warm ever since. She had cut Pathwell out of one of these things at the bar. The seam had taken several cuts, and she had needed both arms to pull him free. Her left shoulder hurt inside the sling before she'd moved it at all."

Every sentence is a fact about her equipment. None of them is something she wants, and none weighs one person against another. The paragraph sets up the arithmetic (one arm, a broken tool, a rescue that needs several cuts) and then never does the sum.

**Then the decision is made by someone else, and she obeys.**

> "Shade was still trying to pull his leg free. He looked toward the archive again, then back at her, and with his free hand he pointed her at the door. / Go. / Elizabeth turned toward the archive door."

Look at whose verbs these are. Shade points. Shade says "Go." Elizabeth "turned toward," which is the verb of someone being directed. At the one moment the book has been building toward, the protagonist does what a man who sounds certain tells her to do. That's the opposite of her arc, and the scene doesn't seem to notice.

**The slow motion is spent on the wrong thing.** The prose slows down at the diary ("Elizabeth stopped, for less than a second. / Her body knew the book before her mind finished identifying it…") and at the cookbook ("The shelf was close enough to touch"). The narration then frames this as the moral event: "That was the choice. / Everything else burned afterward."

No reader weighs a diary against a trapped eleven-year-old. Slowing the prose there tells us the book thinks this is the dilemma, and the reader feels the gap. The real dilemma, Shade or Milo, got four short paragraphs and was settled by Shade.

**Why this reads as a 7, not a 9.** The scene is well made at the level of events: fire, rescue, the sock detail, the lever. What it lacks is a choice where both options cost something, made by the protagonist, about exactly her flaw. In a 9, the climax is the moment the book's question can't be dodged any more. Here it's dodged twice: once by Shade deciding, and once by the objects standing in for the dilemma.

### 2. The technique (at the desk)

1. **Write the dilemma as two columns before you touch the prose.** Column A: "If she goes to Milo, she loses ___." Column B: "If she goes to Shade, she loses ___." Fill both with people or real consequences, not objects. If either column is empty or trivial, you don't have a dilemma yet. Right now column B (Shade) is full, and column A only contains a diary and a cookbook.
2. **Make the resources force exclusivity, and let her do the arithmetic on the page.** You've already set this up: the dagger is split, one arm is in a sling, and freeing Pathwell took several cuts and both arms. Write one or two sentences where she adds it up: four cuts here and then the smoke has the door, or the door now. The reader should see that she can't do both, and see her see it.
3. **Keep Shade's "Go," but make her answer it.** His choosing for himself is the best thing about Shade, so protect it. But she has to decide in her own words, for her own reason, out loud or in close thought. She doesn't have to defy him. She has to choose the same thing for a different reason, and say so. That turns obedience into choice.
4. **Move the slow motion to the decision.** Spend the scene's longest sentences on the second where she weighs Shade against Milo. Cut the diary and cookbook passes to one sentence each. They're aftershocks, not the earthquake.
5. **Cut or move the narrator's verdict.** "That was the choice" only works if the choice was hard. Once it is hard, you won't need the line. If you keep the echo of Ch4, put it after a choice that actually cost something.
6. **Make her carry the cost afterward, on the page.** In Ch18 she says, "I didn't help him either." Move that admission into Ch16, to her talk with Milo or with Mama Baga, so she pays for the choice while it's fresh.
7. **Put Pathwell in her line of sight at the moment she decides.** This is the bridge to Guide 2. She should see that he isn't moving.

### 3. A model

- **Tolkien, *The Return of the King*, "Mount Doom."** Frodo reaches the Crack of Doom, and the climax tests exactly the thing the whole book has been loading onto him: the will to give up the Ring. He fails: "I do not choose now to do what I came to do." The mechanism: the climax is a choice by the hero about the hero's specific burden. When the outcome turns, it turns because of an earlier choice (the pity shown to Gollum), not because someone else steps in and decides for him.
- **J.K. Rowling, *Deathly Hallows*, "The Forest Again."** Harry has just seen Snape's memories, so he has full information. Nobody tells him to go. The chapter stays slow and close on his decision to walk into the forest, not on the objects he carries. The mechanism: give the hero full knowledge, remove anyone who could give instructions, and put the camera on the choice.

### 4. Worked example on this manuscript

**Current text (Ch15):**

> "Shade looked at the doorway. Elizabeth had heard both of them. / He looked at her. / Her right hand had found the foam dagger behind her waist. … Her left shoulder hurt inside the sling before she'd moved it at all. / … / Shade was still trying to pull his leg free. He looked toward the archive again, then back at her, and with his free hand he pointed her at the door. / Go. / Elizabeth turned toward the archive door."

**ILLUSTRATION** (it shows the move, not final wording):

> Shade's eyes found hers. "Go."
>
> She already had the dagger out. It was warm through her palm. At the bar it had taken four cuts and both arms to get Pathwell out, and the sling left her one arm and nothing else.
>
> Four cuts here. Then Shade, free. Then the door, with smoke already rolling out of it.
>
> Or the door now.
>
> "Pathwell." He was ten feet from Shade, face turned toward him, hands flat on the dirt. He didn't move.
>
> "Go," Shade said again.
>
> "I'm not going because you said it." Her voice broke on *said*. "I'm going because he's eleven."
>
> She pressed the dagger into Shade's free hand. He'd have to do the cutting himself.
>
> Then she ran.

**What each choice does:**
- **Kept Shade's "Go."** His agency is the best thing about him. It's now something she responds to, not a direction she follows.
- **Turned the inventory into a sum.** "Four cuts here. Then Shade… Or the door now." It's the same facts as your paragraph, arranged so they force exclusivity. The reader watches her see that she can't do both.
- **Added the Pathwell glance.** This plants Guide 2 in the climax itself. Saying his name aloud is a call for help that he doesn't answer, which the reader sees, without the narration explaining it.
- **"I'm not going because you said it."** This is her arc in one line: the same action Shade asked for, but with her reason, so she's no longer following a man who sounds certain.
- **"Because he's eleven."** A plain, concrete reason, and Milo told her his age in Ch4. It isn't a principle.
- **Gave Shade the dagger.** It costs her the tool, gives Shade a fighting chance he chooses to use, and makes his death worse. He had a chance, and the split blade (Stansbury warned her about it) may be why it failed. Whether it fails is your decision.
- **Cut from later in the scene:** most of the diary and cookbook slow motion, and "That was the choice." Those beats can shrink to one line each now, because the real choice has already happened.

### 5. How to check it worked

- **The two-column test.** Fill in what she loses with each option, using only people and consequences. If one column holds only objects, keep working.
- **Underline the decision sentence.** Whose verb is it? If the subject is anyone other than Elizabeth, she hasn't decided.
- **The argue-the-other-side test.** Could a reasonable reader argue for the choice she didn't make? If no one could, it isn't a dilemma.
- **Count words.** Words on the decision versus words on the diary and cookbook. Right now it's roughly 1 to 3. Flip it.

### 6. Traps

- **Melodrama.** Don't turn this into *Sophie's Choice*. A cozy-shelf reader accepts loss, not cruelty. Keep it fast, two or three sentences of weighing, and don't let her deliberate for a page.
- **Erasing Shade's agency.** If Elizabeth simply overrides him, you've made her the next person deciding what Shade is. She has to honor his choice and make her own.
- **Making Shade's death entirely her fault.** The cost should be that she chose, not that she killed him. Pathwell's inaction (Guide 2) carries the guilt. Her share is grief and responsibility.
- **Over-explaining her reasoning in narration afterward.** One line of reason, spoken, then action.
- **Protect:** "His sock had finally stayed up. Elizabeth hated that detail immediately." The lever-and-shoulder rescue mechanics. Milo's "Pathwell stupid?" / "Worse. Mine." "That hand was promising." The whole Ch16 aftermath.

## Guide 2: Put Pathwell's failure with Shade on the page, and let the ending know about it (Ch15, Ch16, Ch18)

### 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

**The act is placed where the camera can't see it.** In Ch15 the narration notes Pathwell twice and moves on: "Pathwell lay on his side by the collapsed frame, among the loose pale threads. He was close enough to hear Shade calling. His face was turned toward him." And later: "His face was turned toward the place where Shade had been." Both are neutral reports. The reader can't tell whether he's helpless or choosing.

**The truth is held back for three chapters and delivered as a confession.** Ch18: "I thought that if the creature took him, it would be finished. The draw. The whole business." … "I could have called someone. I didn't."

**Meanwhile the book's accounting goes ahead without it.** Ch16's apology buries Shade in a list: "The museum. Your shoulder. The working. Shade. The archive." That list is a sentence form that makes a person equal to a cabinet. The tribunal assigns paper, boxes, waxed cloth and cabinets. The curator scene, the drying rack and the yellow wagon all follow. So the restitution chapters read as atonement for property. Then the confession lands one page before "Perfect," with nothing left to absorb it.

**Why this reads as a 7, not a 9.** Holding it back turns a consequence into a twist. Twists ask "what happened?" Consequences ask "what now?", and the back third of this book is entirely about "what now." A 9 would let the reader carry Pathwell's act through Ch16 and Ch17 and watch every repair happen in its shadow. That makes the yellow wagon and the copied smudge mean something heavier. As it stands, the last chapter quietly turns those chapters into the wrong kind of atonement, and the ending's "Perfect" reads as a forgiveness the book never weighed.

### 2. The technique (at the desk)

1. **Write the book's verdict as one sentence before anything else.** For example: "Pathwell let Shade die because it was clean, and that is the same act as the prune." Then a second sentence: "Elizabeth goes with him at the end because ___, knowing this." If you can't fill the blank honestly, the ending has to change, not the confession.
2. **Make the act visible when it happens.** Give it one concrete thing he could have done and doesn't: a voice to shout with, Stansbury thirty feet away, a hand that could reach. Show it through Elizabeth's eyes (Guide 1's illustration does this) or through a short Pathwell point-of-view beat. You've used his POV before, at the mirror and in Ch10, so it's within the book's rules.
3. **Don't explain the motive at the scene.** Show him not moving. The reason comes later, in his own words.
4. **Move the confession to where the community keeps its accounts, in Ch16.** Put it at "For which part?" or in front of Mama Baga's tribunal. Then the restitution chapters are about a person as well as property.
5. **Give the confession a form in the book's own language: paper.** Let Pathwell ask the archivist to add a line to the loss sheet. The book's moral system runs on records, so the confession should become one.
6. **Give Ch18 a new job.** Once the reader knows, the Merritt cookbook he declines to buy and "Do you want to come?" become tests. Have her ask something he can't perform his way out of. "Perfect" then becomes something she chooses with full knowledge, which is her arc completed.

### 3. A model

- **Ursula K. Le Guin, *A Wizard of Earthsea*.** Ged's pride-driven summoning on Roke Knoll is on the page: we watch him do it, out of vanity, in front of witnesses. The shadow it releases is the rest of the book. There's no mystery about the act, only consequence, and the resolution is Ged naming the shadow with his own name. (Note the closeness to Shade and "Say his name." Use it consciously.) The mechanism: show the act when it happens, so everything after is about living with it.
- **Ian McEwan, *Atonement*.** Briony's false accusation is shown from inside her point of view, with her certainty fully in place. The reader watches the error being made as an act of conviction, then spends the novel on whether atonement is possible. The mechanism maps directly onto your "certainty" theme: let the reader see the rationalization from inside, and make the reckoning about repair, not discovery.

### 4. Worked example on this manuscript

**Current text (Ch16):**

> "He looked at her first, then toward the archive. 'Your apartment. Your diary, which I read. Your grandmother's book, which I came looking for and would have spent without asking. The museum. Your shoulder. The working. Shade. The archive.' / 'Those aren't one thing.'"

**ILLUSTRATION:**

> "…The museum. Your shoulder. The working." He stopped. "Shade."
>
> "Those aren't one thing."
>
> "No. That one isn't like the others." He looked at the trampled place by the frame. "He called for help. I was close enough to hear. I had a voice, and Leo was thirty feet away." His hands stayed open on his knees. "I thought, if it takes him, the draw ends. Clean. And I lay there and let it be clean."
>
> Elizabeth said nothing.
>
> "I'm not telling you so you'll say something."
>
> "Then don't wait for it."
>
> That evening, when the archivist read out the loss sheet, Pathwell asked him to add a line. The archivist looked at Mama Baga. She nodded. He wrote it under LIKED POCKETS, and he didn't soften it.

**What each choice does:**
- **The list breaks at "Shade."** The list form is the problem, so the fix is to break it at the person. The rest of your list stays.
- **"I had a voice, and Leo was thirty feet away."** Specific things he could have done make it an act, not a mood. "Leo" is intimate. He uses it when he means it.
- **"Clean."** This deliberately echoes the van confession ("the cleanest way through it"). The reader sees the prune and the death are the same act. You don't have to say so.
- **Hands open, no explanation of feeling.** He reports and doesn't perform. That is his change, shown in behavior.
- **Elizabeth's silence, then "Then don't wait for it."** This matches her own line, "I need you not to make me responsible for whether you become a better person." She doesn't absolve him and doesn't punish him.
- **The loss-sheet line.** The confession enters the book's moral currency: written down, filed beside the thing Shade liked. Ch17's line, "Near the bottom… it still said LIKED POCKETS," now has a second line under it, and the reader feels both.
- **Not written in:** the exact wording on the sheet. Leave it unquoted, or write it plainly. Your call.
- **Knock-on edits:** delete the Ch18 confession, keeping "Say his name" or moving it here. In Ch18, give Elizabeth one direct question before "Do you want to come?"

### 5. How to check it worked

- **The end-of-Ch15 test.** Give Ch1–15 to a cold reader. Can they say, without being prompted, what Pathwell did or didn't do while Shade was taken? If not, it's still hidden.
- **The three-sentence test.** Summarize Ch16–17 in three sentences. Does Shade's death, and Pathwell's part in it, appear? If your summary is all cabinets and wagons, the accounting is still property-only.
- **The last-line test.** Read "Are you ready?" "No." "Perfect." aloud twice, once imagining a reader who doesn't know, once one who does. If the line means the same thing both times, Ch18 hasn't taken on the knowledge.
- **Count the confessions.** It should happen once. Two versions of it is a sign you're afraid it didn't land.

### 6. Traps

- **Making him unforgivable or a villain.** He didn't kill Shade. He failed to act out of the same certainty as everything else. Keep it at the level of omission, stated plainly.
- **The confession becoming a speech.** Keep it under about 80 words, with no self-flagellation. Ch16 already has Elizabeth forbidding guilt that is put to use. Respect that rule.
- **Elizabeth absolving him.** If she says "It's not your fault," the book contradicts itself.
- **Losing the warmth of the ending.** The aim isn't to cancel "Perfect." It's to make it a choice with full knowledge. The ending can be just as sweet and much stronger.
- **Protect:** "Say his name." Stansbury's "Let it be, big cat." The curator scene ("Useful… Not returned."). "LIKED POCKETS" and "Archive burned. Categories are temporarily flexible." The Merritt cookbook scene. The bookend itself.

## Guide 3: Give each character a voice of their own, and cut the quip buttons (whole book)

### 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

Most conversations in the book use one engine: a two-line exchange closed by a dry, one- to three-word button, often a noun or a correction. Everyone runs it. Three examples:

> Ch6: "Consistency," said Pathwell. / "Stagnation," said Stansbury. / "Branding."
>
> Ch12 (the healer): "Briefly? Very." / "Excellent." / "Afterward, less." / "Strong sales pitch."
>
> Ch18 (the shopkeeper): "A month is not a character trait." / "Cruel." / "Accounting."

Brothers, healer and shopkeeper all sound alike, and so does Elizabeth. I counted the shared tics in the manuscript:
- **"That's not / isn't the same thing / as …"** appears 7 times across five speakers: Elizabeth (lines 497, 3565, 5881, 6076), Stansbury (4167), Mama Baga (5267), Pathwell (5459).
- **A bare "Good."** as a full line or line-ending appears about 20 times, from Stansbury, Shade, Elizabeth, Mama Baga and the healer.
- **"Fair."** appears 3 times, from three different speakers (Pathwell, Elizabeth, Shade).
- **"Cruel."** appears 3 times, all Pathwell (a real tic, at least).
- **"Important distinction."** appears twice, both Elizabeth.

**Why this reads as a 7, not a 9.**
- When everyone is witty in the same way, wit stops telling us anything about who is speaking.
- When every exchange ends on a button, every exchange has the same shape, and the reader starts predicting it. That's where I lost focus around Ch14.
- It hides Elizabeth's arc. She starts the book polite and apologetic ("I'm fine," "I try," "I've made quite an entrance"), but by Ch2 she's trading deadpan lines like Pathwell. So her change in voice never shows.

In a 9, you could cover the speech tags and still know who's talking, and a joke lands because only that person would make it.

### 2. The technique (at the desk)

1. **Write a one-page voice sheet for each major speaker.** For each: what they want from any conversation, their default move, two or three words or frames only they use, and one move they're forbidden. A starting point drawn from what's already on your pages:
   - **Pathwell.** Wants to control the shape of the conversation. Default move: performance, excess, deflection by running on. Owns "Lizzy," mock-formal phrasing, historical asides. Forbidden: silence. Until he learns it, which is his arc.
   - **Stansbury.** Wants accuracy. Default move: correction by over-precision and procedure ("Inventoried. Labeled. Counted back in"). Owns "the useful version." Forbidden: flourish.
   - **Mama Baga.** Wants the other person to answer for themselves. Default move: one question, then waiting. Owns short imperatives ("Drink." "Sit."). Forbidden: comebacks. She never plays the button game, and that's where her power comes from.
   - **The archivist.** Wants things recorded. Default move: categories and procedure, with very dry humor ("That explains the filing"). Forbidden: feelings stated directly.
   - **Shade.** Wants to choose. Default move: refusal and plain nouns ("No." "Pockets."). Forbidden: Pathwell's flourishes. Every time he almost uses one, he catches it.
   - **Milo.** Literal kid logic and accusation. Forbidden: irony.
   - **The healer.** Clinical and consent-first. Default move: plain questions, plain answers. Forbidden: jokes. Let her be the one unfunny voice.
   - **Elizabeth.** Starts polite, apologetic and over-explaining. Her lines get shorter and more direct as she changes. Forbidden in Act 1: deadpan comebacks. Let her earn them.
2. **Run a button audit, scene by scene.** Highlight every line that ends an exchange on a comic beat. Keep at most one per beat, given to the character the voice sheet says owns that kind of joke. Replace the rest with an action, a silence, or nothing.
3. **Run the anyone test on every quip you keep.** Could any other character say it? If yes, cut it, or rewrite it in the owner's form: Pathwell overextends, Stansbury over-specifies, Milo accuses.
4. **Break up the shared tics.** Give "not the same thing" to one person, probably Stansbury, and cut it everywhere else. Keep "Good." for one or two speakers. Cut "Fair." to once.
5. **End some exchanges on action.** Make a rule of thumb: in every scene at least one exchange ends with someone doing something, and no one has the last word.
6. **Track Elizabeth's line length chapter by chapter.** In Ch1–4 let her sentences run long and apologetic. Make her first true deadpan line an event: the ditch is a good candidate.

### 3. A model

**Terry Pratchett's witches (*Wyrd Sisters*, *Witches Abroad*).** Granny Weatherwax, Nanny Ogg and Magrat are all funny, each in a different way:
- Granny speaks in short, absolute statements, never apologizes, and wins arguments by refusing to have them.
- Nanny is warm, earthy and digressive, and drifts into innuendo and songs.
- Magrat is earnest, asks hopeful questions, and gets punctured.

You can cover the speech tags on any page and know who's speaking. The mechanism: each character's humor comes from a different place (certainty, vulgarity, earnestness), so a joke tells you who made it. It's the same trio problem you have with Mama Baga, the archivist and the shopkeeper.

**What each choice does:**
- **Mama Baga: one question, a look, silence.** I cut her "No." / "I am getting ahead." / "That is not the same thing." Those are the same quick back-and-forth everyone uses, and they make her one more quipper. Her power is that she doesn't play. The look at the cards and at Shade's hand tells us she has already read the whole situation.
- **Stansbury: over-precision.** "Seventy percent alcohol… Twice." The humor comes from his procedure. Your "I used antiseptic" / "With resentment" became a joke anyone could make.
- **Pathwell: excess, cut off.** He runs on, deflects, reaches for an anecdote, and gets interrupted. His voice is too much. The interruption is the joke, and it's also characterization: no one lets him perform here any more.
- **Cut "I am being medically outnumbered." / "Good."** It's a button on a button. The exchange now ends on Mama Baga walking away, an action, and nobody gets the last word.
- **Cut "That is not the same thing."** It was the sixth of seven uses.
- **What survives:** the content of your scene. She refuses to be handled, she sends him to the healer, she defers it to later. Only the voices changed.

### 5. How to check it worked

- **Cover the tags.** Pick five random pages, black out every speech tag and name, and give them to a reader, or do it yourself after a week away. They should be able to name the speaker of most lines. Right now it's hard for anyone but Milo and Pathwell.
- **Count buttons per scene.** Tally lines that end an exchange on a quip. Aim for one per beat at most. Ch13–14 and Ch17 will show the biggest drop.
- **Count the tics by searching.** "the same thing," "Good.", "Fair.", "Cruel." Compare with the numbers above, and check each survivor belongs to its owner.
- **Read aloud.** Read a scene and listen only to rhythm. Each speaker should have a different sentence length: Pathwell long, Stansbury clipped and listing, Mama Baga very short with pauses, the healer plain. If the rhythm is uniform, the voices are too.
- **Track Elizabeth.** Average her line length in Ch1–4 against Ch15–18. It should drop noticeably, and her first true comeback should come late enough to feel earned.

### 6. Traps

- **Stripping the humor out.** The book is funny, and that's an asset. The aim is fewer jokes that hit harder, owned by the right people, not a solemn book.
- **Turning voices into gimmicks.** A voice is a way of thinking, not a catchphrase. If you find yourself adding signature lines, you're making caricatures. Build each voice from what the character wants in the conversation.
- **Cutting the structural repetitions.** Some repeated lines are plot. Protect:
  - "That's terrible." / "I know." This is the shared memory that finds her in Ch10, and Shade echoes it in Ch13.
  - "posture" (Mama Baga's word in Ch4, which Elizabeth picks up).
  - The spaewives welcome speech and its Ch17 return.
  - "the useful version," as long as it stays Stansbury's.
- **Over-flattening the healer and Mama Baga.** Unfunny isn't the same as dull. Their lines still need to be specific.
- **Protect outright:**
  - The beetle children ("It's practicing." / "Being dead.").
  - "That is generally the opposite of an archive."
  - "Copy the smudge."
  - Milo's "He still owes me a hand."
  - "Pockets." / "I changed my mind."
  - Mama Baga's "Sit before you fall over politely" / "That is not a posture."

## Revision order

1. **First: decide the verdict, then rewrite the Ch15 fire scene (Guides 1 and 2 together).** Write the two sentences from Guide 2, step 1, and the two columns from Guide 1, step 1, before any prose. Draft the voice sheets now too. They take an hour and should guide every new line you write from here. The climax comes first because it decides what earlier chapters have to set up and what later chapters have to carry. Until it's settled, any other revision might be aimed at the wrong ending.
2. **Second: carry the change through the book.** Backward, check that the setups support the new climax:
   - The Ch7 dagger rescue (several cuts, both arms).
   - The Ch8 line about following men who sound certain.
   - The healer's Ch12 "You are not using this arm today."
   - A way to show Pathwell's stillness that the reader can read.

   Forward:
   - Put the confession and the loss-sheet line in Ch16.
   - Trim the Ch17 montage to two scenes, now carrying the knowledge.
   - Remove the Ch18 confession and give Elizabeth her direct question before "Do you want to come?"

   This is also the stage for the other structural work from my report: growing Elizabeth's life, adding a scene for Shade, and merging the two relapses. It all changes what scenes exist, so it has to come before line work.
3. **Third: the voice pass, whole book, using the sheets.** Do it last because line-level work on scenes you're about to rewrite or cut is wasted. By this point the scene list is stable, and the button audit and tic counts can run front to back in one consistent pass. It's also when Elizabeth's line-length arc can be tuned across the whole book at once.

- Pathwell's inaction visible on the page and unexplained.
- Distinct voices under pressure: Shade's refusals, Milo's literal-mindedness, Elizabeth stripped down.

**Success looks like:**
- A cold reader who has read the rest of the book can answer three questions without prompting: What did Elizabeth choose? What did it cost her? What did Pathwell do?
- The decision sentence has Elizabeth as its subject and gives her reason in her own words.
- The diary and cookbook take up three sentences or fewer combined.
- No more than one comic beat in the whole scene, and it's Milo's.
- "That was the choice" is gone, or follows a choice that clearly cost something.
- The scene is no longer than your current version. These fixes usually shorten.
- Read it aloud: if you feel a flinch at the moment she chooses, it's working. If you feel relief, the dilemma is still too easy.
