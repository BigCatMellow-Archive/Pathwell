# Revision guide: from a 7 toward a 9

The three items that were worth the most in my "gap to a 9":
1. Bring chapter 1 up to the standard of the rest. About +0.5.
2. Give each character a distinct voice. About +0.5.
3. Do a pass for repeated gestures and habits. About +0.3.

The fourth item, "stop explaining your best images", was tied with the third. It uses the same cutting method, so I've folded its check into Guide 3 (step 7) rather than writing a fourth guide. Every passage I quote here is from the six chapters I read (1, 4, 9, 12, 16, 18). Line numbers are manuscript line numbers.

## Guide 1: Bring chapter 1 up to the standard of chapters 4–18

### 1. Diagnosis

Chapter 1 contains the book's founding promise: a recipe that works as a spell, and a grandmother's voice in the ink. It is also the least revised prose in the book. You can see it in three places.

**a) The memory (line 71) is built from generic parts.**

> "She was twelve again, baking cookies with her grandmother. Sun shining through an open window above the sink, the fresh smell of rain filled her lungs. Her grandmother smiled at her, and Elizabeth felt something she hadn't felt in years."

What the paragraph is made of:
- a summary ("baking cookies")
- a weather sentence that contradicts itself (sun and rain at once) and has a dangling clause
- a smile
- a feeling named only as "something"

Nana doesn't speak. Nobody is holding anything. Nothing in it belongs to *this* grandmother rather than any grandmother in any book.

Line 69 also promises "A voice that couldn't be there", and then the reader never hears it. The most important sound in the chapter is held back.

Now compare your own chapter 4 (line 919):

> "Seventeen, maybe, sitting at Nana's table with the flu, furious at being sick and more furious that Nana had been right about it. A bowl between both hands. Steam in her face. Nana pretending not to watch until Elizabeth took the first bite."

That paragraph has an age with a hedge, a feeling that contradicts itself, an object in her hands, a sensation, and an action that only Nana would do. You already have the skill. Chapter 1 just hasn't been revised to it.

**b) The action beats lean on stock body reactions.**

> "The blob rippled and recoiled, the stench of wet-rot splattered across the room. Elizabeth ran for the bathroom, her stomach fighting to keep its contents to itself." (85)

Each sentence follows the same pattern: a main clause, then a comma-spliced add-on, then a body reaction. Chapter 1 has "her racing heart", "her stomach fighting", "Her stomach dropped", "Her heart skipped" and "felt something", all in about 1,200 words.

Body reactions are what a draft reaches for when the writer hasn't yet decided what the character *notices*. Later in the book you notice instead. In chapter 12, "Elizabeth saw the inside of her own skull for a second" is a perception, not a pulse.

**c) The threat has only one sound.** "Thud" appears five times. A sound that never changes stops reading as a growing threat and becomes a beat.

**Why this reads as a 6.** Every element is in the right place, but the emotional engine is described rather than shown. A reader who opens the book on page one meets a less skilled writer than the one who wrote chapter 4.

### 2. The technique

1. **Read your later work aloud, then chapter 1.** Read chapter 4 lines 893–919 aloud, then read chapter 1 aloud straight after. Pencil a mark wherever your voice speeds up, stumbles or goes flat. Those marks are your work list.
2. **Audit the body reactions.** Circle every heart, stomach, breath or "something". For each one, ask what Elizabeth notices at that moment that nobody else would. Swap the body reaction for that perception, or cut it. Aim for at most one body reaction in the chapter.
3. **Rebuild the memory with four parts taken from chapter 4:**
   - an exact age (keep "twelve", because chapter 4's "Not twelve. Seventeen" depends on it)
   - one object in her hands
   - one line of Nana's actual speech
   - one feeling that contradicts itself

   Then delete the sentence that names the feeling.
4. **Write Nana's line in the voice of her margin notes.** Your notes have a grammar: an instruction, then a reason that hides the care. "MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH." Her spoken line should follow the same pattern, so the voice in chapter 1 matches the handwriting in chapter 4.
5. **Let the reader hear the voice.** Line 69 promises a voice that couldn't be there, so the reader must hear it once, briefly. That sets up "Why could I hear my Nana?" (143).
6. **Plan the threat's sounds before revising.** List them in order, for example: a knock with weight behind it, wood starting to give, the frame failing, silence, then something down the hall. Each sound appears once. "Thud" appears at most twice.
7. **One action per sentence in the fight.** Search for a main clause followed by ", the" or ", her" and split it or cut the add-on.
8. **Keep the length.** Chapter 1's speed is an asset. Aim to stay within 10% of the current word count. The memory should stay at five or six lines.

### 3. Models

- **Neil Gaiman, *The Ocean at the End of the Lane*.** The boy's memories of the Hempstock kitchen are rooted in exact dishes and in a particular woman's way of serving them (porridge with jam stirred in, for one). The book never says "warmth". The warmth comes from the specific food and the specific manner. That is the move for line 71: the feeling travels inside a detail only this family would have.
- **Gaiman, *Neverwhere*, opening.** Richard Mayhew finds a bleeding girl on the pavement while he's on his way to an important dinner with his fiancée. The ordinary obligation stays alive during the uncanny event, and the comedy comes from the collision. You do this with the cake and the banner. Keep doing it: the cake surviving is your best laugh.

### 4. Worked example: lines 69–73

Current text:

> "Everything went white, and a scent hit her: buttery and sweet. Followed by a taste… and something else, someone else. A voice that couldn't be there.
> She was twelve again, baking cookies with her grandmother. Sun shining through an open window above the sink, the fresh smell of rain filled her lungs. Her grandmother smiled at her, and Elizabeth felt something she hadn't felt in years.
> Her body moved back before she did…"

**ILLUSTRATION** (shows the technique; the final wording is yours):

> Everything went white, and the white smelled of butter.
> Twelve. A kitchen chair dragged to the counter, because the step stool was "for grown women with bad knees." Nana's hand over hers on the cutter, not pressing. Just there.
> "Thin ones for the neighbors. Thick ones for us. They'll never know."
> "Isn't that lying?"
> "It's baking."
> Rain on the window. Flour on her sleeve that wouldn't brush off.
> Then Nana's voice came again, not from the memory but from somewhere close to Elizabeth's own ear, and it was still talking about cookies.
> Her body moved back before she did…

What each choice does:
- **"the white smelled of butter"** joins the flash and the scent into one image. It replaces the list ("a scent hit her… Followed by a taste… and something else, someone else"), which had three hesitations in a row.
- **I cut the sun and kept the rain.** That removes the contradiction and the dangling clause. Keep whichever weather you prefer, but keep only one.
- **The step-stool quote and the hand on the cutter** give the reader an object and a touch. "Not pressing. Just there." is the relationship in one gesture, the same job "pretending not to watch" does in chapter 4.
- **The thin/thick exchange** is Nana's line in her margin-note grammar: an instruction, then a reason that hides affection and a bit of mischief. It echoes the family arguments in the Merritt book in chapter 18 ("RUTH LIED ABOUT THE LARD"), so the book's idea of how families write in recipes is set up on page one.
- **"Elizabeth felt something" is gone completely.** The joke plus "flour that wouldn't brush off" carries the feeling. The reader feels the "something" without being told about it.
- **The last line pays off "A voice that couldn't be there".** The uncanny moment is the voice stepping out of the memory into the room. "Still talking about cookies" keeps chapter 1's comic register even in the eerie moment.
- **What I kept:** "twelve", sugar cookies, and the bodily recoil line, which is already good.

### 5. How to check it worked

- **Swap test.** For each sentence in the memory, ask: "Could this sentence appear in another novel about another grandmother?" If yes, rewrite it or cut it.
- **Counts.** At most one stock body reaction in chapter 1. At most two uses of "thud". At most two ellipses.
- **Read-aloud pairing.** Read chapter 4 line 919, then your new line 71. They should sound like the same writer, with the chapter 1 memory smaller and happier.
- **Callback check.** Does chapter 4's "A different memory this time. Not twelve." still land? It should feel like a turn from joy to care.

### 6. Traps

- **Overwriting the memory into a set piece.** If the memory runs longer than the fight, the chapter's speed is gone.
- **Adding backstory.** Don't explain Nana's death or Elizabeth's grief in chapter 1. The funeral belongs to chapter 12 (4534).
- **Stealing from chapter 4.** No soup, no illness. Chapter 1's memory is play; chapter 4's is care.
- **Smoothing out Elizabeth's fluster.** "They're… they're" (fixed to the singular) and the uptalk question mark are where she starts. Her later dry wit needs that starting point.
- **Naming Pathwell.** Keep him unnamed in chapter 1.

**Protect:**
- "half a sheet cake that now only read ELIZ"
- "reading her diary like a magazine in a waiting room"
- "Gold ran along the ink… where the pen had hesitated"
- "The impact sang."
- "The cake, somehow, was fine."
- the "we should be going" loop
- "Ready?" / "No." / "Perfect." (the bookend with chapter 18)

## Guide 2: Give each character a distinct voice

### 1. Diagnosis

> "Your account remains at zero."
> "You say that as though waiting for weather."
> "A month is not a character trait."
> "Cruel."
> "Accounting." (ch. 18, 7477–7485)

> "Good. This is going to take a while."
> "Define a while."
> "Longer than you want."
> "Everything does." (ch. 16, 6316–6322: the healer and Stansbury)

Compare Elizabeth in chapter 12 (4349): "Define using."

The mechanism is the same everywhere: a statement, then a reply that redefines one of its words, then a one-word verdict. Three habits are shared across almost the whole cast:
- no contractions under pressure
- redefining the other person's word ("That is not a posture." "That is different." "Attached and working are different questions." "Important distinction.")
- the one-word noun verdict ("Accurate." "Accounting." "Noted." "Better." "Convenient.")

Each line is good on its own. Together they become the narrator's wit handed out across the cast, so characters turn into delivery systems for the author's humour.

**Why this reads as a 7.** Cover the speech tags and readers can't tell who is talking. It also weakens your best device. Shade is made from pieces of Pathwell, and the book points out their resemblance. That overlap should be the only place two voices blur. When everyone blurs, it loses its charge.

### 2. The technique

1. **Pull out each speaker's lines.** Copy every line of dialogue for each named speaker into a separate document: Elizabeth, Pathwell, Shade, Mama Baga, the archivist, the healer, Stansbury, the shopkeeper, Milo. Read each one on its own, start to finish.
2. **Write a voice card for each, answering six questions:**
   - Do they use contractions: always, never, or only when rattled?
   - What is their default sentence length?
   - What is their comic mechanism? (Not "dry". Name the move: literalism, lists, procedure, a pointed question.)
   - What vocabulary do they draw from? (records, money, the body, kin and food, theatre)
   - What is the one verbal habit they own that nobody else uses?
   - What do they never do? (explain, apologise, joke first, ask)
3. **Give each shared habit one owner.** The verdict, the redefinition and the no-contraction formality each go to one or two characters, chosen on purpose.
4. **Go through scene by scene.** For every line, check whether it fits the speaker's card. If it fits someone else's card, rewrite it in this speaker's mechanism or cut it. Keep how often the scene is funny; change *how* it's funny.
5. **Decide Pathwell's arc on purpose.** In chapters 1 and 4 he uses contractions and floods the room ("Did I say runemasters?"). In chapters 16 and 18 he is clipped and formal. If that's growth, his formal speech should sound like *effortful restraint*: a flourish starting and being stopped. "I am trying very hard to have been house-trained by consequences" already does exactly this. It shouldn't sound like Shade or the shopkeeper.
6. **Run the blind test** (see part 5).

Here are my suggested cards. I've only read six chapters, so treat these as starting points to argue with:

| Character | Engine | Owns | Never |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elizabeth | Office politeness applied to magic ("Strong sales pitch." "I disagree with your use of the word.") | "Define ___" | Speaks in pronouncements |
| Pathwell | Excess: lists, flourishes, answering questions nobody asked. His growth means shortening | The grand catalogue | One-word answers (until he earns one) |
| Shade | Inventory: nouns without adjectives, careful with "I" and "he" | The one-word verdict | Jokes for fun. His humour is accidental precision |
| Mama Baga | Orders and pointed questions ("Are you being dragged?") | The question that exposes | Explains herself; stock grandmother phrases ("Hush now") |
| Archivist | Categories and procedure ("That explains the filing.") | Filing as a worldview | Comforts directly |
| Healer | Literal and conditional ("If the fingers numb, if…") | Plain truth | Jokes first. She's funny only by being literal |
| Stansbury | Clerical: queues, forms, schedules | "Noted." | Redefines words (that's Elizabeth's) |
| Shopkeeper | Money and ledgers, distaste for novelty | Accounting | Sentiment |

### 3. Models

- **Terry Pratchett, *Guards! Guards!*** The Night Watch: Carrot is earnest and quotes the law literally, Colon has muddled sergeant's authority, and Nobby deflates everything grubbily. When something absurd happens, each man reacts in a way only he could. Pratchett runs long exchanges with very few tags because the *kind* of line tells you who said it. The mechanism is a different relationship to rules and words for each character, not different accents.
- **T. Kingfisher, *Nettle & Bone*.** The dust-wife is blunt and caustic, Fenris is courteous and self-deprecating, and Agnes the godmother is fussy and kind. All of them are funny, in incompatible ways. This is your genre's proof that a cast of dry wits doesn't have to share one dry wit.

### 4. Worked example: chapter 16, lines 6316–6324

Current text:

> "The healer nodded. "Good. This is going to take a while."
> "Define a while."
> "Longer than you want."
> "Everything does."
> She began cleaning the edges. Stansbury's jaw tightened, and he didn't pull away."

**ILLUSTRATION:**

> The healer nodded. "Good. This is going to take a while."
> "Then do the boy first, if he's waiting," Stansbury said. "I can be scheduled."
> "He's done."
> "Then I'm next. Noted."
> She began cleaning the edges. Stansbury held still the way he might for a signature.

What each choice does:
- **"Define a while" goes back to Elizabeth.** It's her office-politeness move, and she used it in chapter 12. Taking it away from Stansbury lets her own it.
- **Stansbury's engine is now the queue:** "do the boy first… I can be scheduled." He treats his own pain as an appointment. That shows on the page what the narration currently *tells* twice (6292 "a clerical inconvenience" and 6302 "damaged a tool he owned"). Now one of those similes can go, which is also a Guide 3 cut. Putting Milo first also gives him some quiet decency without stating it.
- **The healer becomes literal: "He's done."** No redefinition, no comeback. Her flatness is the comedy.
- **"Noted." is Stansbury's owned word**, so the verdict move now belongs to a specific person.
- **The redefinition volley is gone**, and so is the house rhythm.
- **"jaw tightened… didn't pull away"** is replaced with one action that fits his card. That removes a gesture habit and a restraint beat.
- **What it costs:** "Everything does." is a good fatalistic line with no owner. Bank it for someone whose card it fits, or let it go.

### 5. How to check it worked

- **Blind attribution.** Take a two-page dialogue scene (chapter 16 6652–6748 is ideal). Delete every tag and beat. Put it away for a few days, then attribute each line yourself, or hand it to a reader. Aim for 80% or more correct.
- **Swap test.** For each witty line, ask: "Could another character say this unchanged?" If yes, it belongs to the house voice and needs rewriting.
- **Ownership counts.** Count "Define", "distinction", "Noted.", "Better.", "Accurate." and similar lines by speaker. Each should cluster on its owner.
- **Contraction count** per speaker per chapter. It should match each card, and any change should be one you chose.

### 6. Traps

- **Catchphrases.** If an owned habit fires every scene, you've recreated the sock problem in dialogue. Use each one at most once or twice per chapter.
- **Differentiating by surface.** Accents, spellings or "dearie" are not voice. Voice is how a person thinks.
- **Draining the wit.** Redistribute it; don't delete it. Every character keeps at least one moment of humour that is theirs.
- **Elizabeth becoming the only funny one.** If everyone else turns into a straight man, you've swapped one problem for another.
- **Breaking the Shade/Pathwell overlap.** Keep it, and sharpen it. Their shared habits are the one licensed blur, for example the "met you on the road twenty-five minutes ago" runner and Shade's "Pathwell would have done that. Probably."

**Protect:** each of these already fits a plausible card.
- "He still owes me a hand." (Milo)
- "You inhaled like an advertisement." (Stansbury)
- "Don't become decorative." (Mama Baga)
- "Person is the word I have for that." (Mama Baga)
- "Specificity is expensive." (the archivist)
- "It's practicing." (the beetle children)
- "I am trying very hard to have been house-trained by consequences." (Pathwell)

## Guide 3: Repeated gestures and habits (with the "stop explaining" check)

### 1. Diagnosis

> "Elizabeth came closer. "Who's Stansbury?"
> Pathwell looked at Mama Baga. Mama Baga looked at Pathwell." (ch. 4, 1216–1218)

> "Shade looked toward the trees. His right hand opened a fraction. He closed it.
> Mama Baga noticed and didn't ask. "Inside," she said.
> […]
> Mama Baga waited. Shade waited. Nobody reached for her." (ch. 12, 4238–4244)

The camera reports every head-turn between lines as stage directions. Most of these beats exist to make a pause, not to give information.

"Looked" appears 154 times in about 15,400 words. That's roughly one every 100 words, and one every 70 in chapter 16. "Nodded" appears 30 times, "considered" about 14 and "waited" about 13.

The restraint beat ("noticed and didn't ask", "Nobody reached for her") reports something that *didn't* happen, so it's really the narrator commenting on a character's virtue. It appears 30-odd times. It's thematic, and the best uses are superb.

**Why this reads as a 7.** At this frequency the reader stops seeing the beats, which means your best beats, the box label at 6824 and "He did not say Lizzy" at 6563, stand out less than they should. The repeated callbacks (the sock five times, "mouth moved at one corner" twice, "Important distinction" twice, "Elizabeth corrected herself" twice) turn from rhymes into tics.

### 2. The technique

1. **Highlight.** Search each chapter for: looked, glanced, nodded, stared, considered, waited, "didn't [verb]", "Nobody [verb]", "noticed". Highlight every hit.
2. **Sort each hit into one of four bins:**
   - (a) *Blocking*: the reader needs it to know where people are.
   - (b) *Meaning*: what the character looks at tells us something (7524, "He was looking at the same book").
   - (c) *Pause filler.*
   - (d) *Restraint statement.*
3. **Bin (c): delete it.** If the pause really matters, use a paragraph break or let the next line arrive later. Use at most one "the world went on" ambient beat per chapter. Your 2915 and 4323 are the right kind.
4. **Bin (b): keep it, but put the object first.** Show the thing seen rather than the act of looking ("Shade's right hand opened toward the trees" rather than "Shade looked toward the trees. His right hand opened").
5. **Bin (d): keep it only if the reader has seen the temptation first.** At 6824 Pathwell turns the label out, *then* turns it back, so the restraint costs him something and the reader watches it happen. Otherwise, swap the non-action for something the character actually does. In 4240, "Inside," she said already *is* her not asking, so "noticed and didn't ask" can go.
6. **Set a budget per chapter, as a working target:**
   - at most one "looked" per 300 words
   - at most two restraint statements per chapter
   - at most five one-line reaction paragraphs ("Elizabeth waited.") per chapter
7. **Ending check (from item 4 in the report).** Cover the last sentence of each scene and chapter. If the scene still ends, delete that sentence. This catches 2999, 6876, 951–953 and 6415.
8. **Callback ledger.** List every repeated running gag. Each one appears at most twice exactly, and any appearance after that must change it. The sock, for instance: establish it in chapter 4, pay it off in chapter 16 (6181), and cut or vary the three in chapter 12.

### 3. Models

- **Kazuo Ishiguro, *The Remains of the Day*.** While Stevens's father is dying upstairs, Stevens keeps serving at the great conference downstairs. The narration never says he holds back his grief. What the reader sees is him carrying on with his duties, and someone remarking that he looks as though he's been crying. Restraint shown through action, its cost revealed through someone else's eyes, never named by the narrator. That's the model for your bin (d).
- **Elmore Leonard, for example *Get Shorty*.** Long dialogue scenes run with "said" and almost no head-turns or nods. The lines bump straight against each other, and the rare action beat is specific business, not a glance. That's the model for bin (c): trust the dialogue to pace itself.

### 4. Worked example: chapter 12, lines 4224–4246

Current text:

> "Mama Baga came down the wagon steps before they reached them.
> She took in Elizabeth's face, the arm held too still, the dust and glass on her coat, and Shade beside her.
> Her bracelets clicked once. "Where is Pathwell?"
> "Museum," Elizabeth said.
> Mama Baga looked at the shoulder again. "Of course he is."
> "That sounded like you already knew what happened."
> "I know what he looks like when he is not here and somebody else is injured."
> Shade looked toward the trees. His right hand opened a fraction. He closed it.
> Mama Baga noticed and didn't ask. "Inside," she said.
> Elizabeth started up the wagon steps. The first one sent a bright line through her shoulder, and she stopped.
> Mama Baga waited. Shade waited. Nobody reached for her.
> Elizabeth tried again and made it inside."

**ILLUSTRATION:**

> Mama Baga came down the wagon steps before they reached them. She took in Elizabeth's face, the arm held too still, the glass on her coat, and Shade beside her.
> Her bracelets clicked once. "Where is Pathwell?"
> "Museum."
> "Of course he is."
> "That sounded like you already knew what happened."
> "I know what he looks like when he is not here and somebody else is injured."
> Shade's right hand opened toward the trees. He closed it.
> "Inside."
> The first step sent a bright line through Elizabeth's shoulder, and she stopped. Mama Baga's hand closed on the rail beside her and stayed there.
> Elizabeth tried again and made it inside.

What each choice does:
- **Cut "Mama Baga looked at the shoulder again".** "Of course he is" already shows she has read the injury and who is to blame. That's bin (c).
- **Cut "Elizabeth said".** With two speakers and one word, the tag only slows things down.
- **Two Shade sentences became one.** The look was filler; the hand opening toward the trees is the information. The object comes first, as in step 4.
- **Cut "Mama Baga noticed and didn't ask".** Her one-word order *is* the not-asking. It also fits her Guide 2 card: orders, no explanations.
- **Three sentences of waiting became one visible action.** "Mama Baga waited. Shade waited. Nobody reached for her." is now a hand gripping the rail. The reader watches her want to help and not help. The restraint costs her something, and the narrator never has to call it restraint.
- **The tally:** "looked" 2→0, "waited" 2→0, restraint statements 2→0. The beat is now more emotional, not less.

### 5. How to check it worked

- **Delete test for each beat.** Remove the beat and read the exchange aloud. If you don't miss it, it stays deleted.
- **Temptation test for each restraint beat.** Ask: "Has the reader seen the temptation before the restraint?" If not, cut it or show the temptation.
- **Counts** against the budget in step 6, chapter by chapter. Chapter 16 is the stress test.
- **Last-sentence test** on every scene ending (step 7).
- **Callback ledger:** no running gag appears verbatim more than twice.

### 6. Traps

- **Thesaurus swaps.** Replacing "looked" with gazed, eyed or glanced fixes nothing.
- **Blocking loss.** Over-cutting turns scenes into a radio play, and readers lose track of who is where. Keep bin (a).
- **Ping-pong.** Once the beats go, the one-word volleys are exposed: 44 lines in my six chapters, 19 in chapter 16 alone. Do Guide 2 before this pass, or alongside it.
- **Cutting the meaningful repeats.** The hand motif (2634, 2997, 4238, 7771) is deliberate. Keep it, but make every use different.

**Protect:**
- 6824, the box label turned and turned back
- 6848, "Mama Baga didn't touch her. She stayed beside her."
- 6563, "He did not say Lizzy."
- 6575–6577, "Elizabeth waited. / Pathwell didn't add a because."
- 2915 and 4323, the "world goes on" beats (one per chapter)
- 6814–6818, "Checking. Nothing. / She let it happen this time."

## Revision order

1. **Voices first (Guide 2).** This is where the decisions get made: Pathwell's contraction arc, what kind of wit Elizabeth has, who owns which habit. The other two steps depend on those answers. Rewriting dialogue also changes which beats are needed, so doing the gesture pass first would mean doing it twice.
2. **Gestures and habits second (Guide 3).** Once the dialogue is stable, cutting beats is mechanical and safe. Distinct voices also mean fewer tags and beats are needed to show who's speaking, so the cuts go deeper. The ending check belongs in this pass too.
3. **Chapter 1 last (Guide 1).** Write the opening to the standard the rest of the book now has. By this point you know exactly how Pathwell and Elizabeth sound, so chapter 1 can set up their voices correctly. The callbacks it plants (twelve, the cookies, "Ready?/No./Perfect.", the cake) are ones you have now checked on the receiving end in chapters 4 and 18.

## Practice exercise for this week

**Rewrite Mama Baga's wagon scenes in chapter 4: lines 781–821 and 1005–1027** (from the tea through to the gold chain).

These two short passages test all three guides at once:
- **Voice.** Mama Baga drifts here between "That is not a posture" and "Hush now… I won't hear another word about it".
- **Specificity.** The gift of the chain is the same generic-warmth problem as chapter 1's memory.
- **Gestures.** It has the restraint beat "Mama Baga didn't ask…" and a catalogue makeover paragraph (1011).

**Success looks like this:**
- With the tags removed, every one of Mama Baga's lines is recognisably hers, and her contraction use follows the card you chose.
- The chain gift has one detail or line that could only come from her. No stock grandmother phrasing is left.
- "looked" appears at most twice across both passages. Any restraint beat that survives has its temptation shown first.
- 1011 shows Elizabeth reacting to the clothes, rather than an inventory of them.
- These stay untouched: "Sit before you fall over politely", "That is not a posture", the onion crate, "something tea had become after a long argument with bark", and the ending, "Nothing matched. It was the most comfortable she'd been all night", which echoes line 704.

If this scene comes out sharper but still recognisably yours, and no shorter on laughs, the approach works and you can apply it to the whole book.
