# GUIDE 1: Give the opening act an engine (Ch 1–10). Worth about +0.75

## 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

From Ch 4 to Ch 10, the book's main question is "what did Pathwell do, and why was the thing at her door?" Two people on the page know most of the answer. Elizabeth asks; they refuse; the scene ends or something interrupts. The refusals are the same handful of lines, used again and again:

- Ch 6: "Later." / "Not here."
- Ch 7: "'Not here,' Pathwell said. / 'You said that inside.'" Then: "'Drive,' Pathwell said."
- Ch 8, after she has crashed the Cadillac on purpose to stop the car:

> Elizabeth pointed at him. "The prune."
>
> Pathwell looked toward Stansbury.
>
> Elizabeth let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "You really can't help yourself."
>
> "I'm thinking."
>
> "You always seem to do that in someone else's direction."
>
> Pathwell looked back at her. Then he went still so abruptly that she stopped talking.

Then headlights, and Shade. Her biggest action so far buys nothing.

Here is what that is doing:

- **Who holds the information:** the brothers. Elizabeth is always the one asking. The reader sits in her seat, so we are asking too.
- **What sustains the suspense:** a character declining to speak. A refusal holds suspense once. By the fourth time, the reader stops wondering about the secret and starts resenting the people keeping it.
- **What the camera shows:** Pathwell's face "going still" and the brothers exchanging looks. Those are pictures of somebody withholding, not clues. The reader gets nothing to work with.
- **Ch 5's best hook gets dropped by its own narrator:** "Was it?" / "Elizabeth almost asked whose door it was, if it wasn't hers. She didn't."

A 6 or 7 builds suspense from "someone won't tell me." A 9 builds it from "I know enough to be afraid of something specific." Mystery works when the reader holds partial information and is working on it. This stretch withholds information without giving the reader anything to work on.

## 2. The technique (at the desk)

1. **Build a question ledger.** On one sheet, list every question the text raises in Ch 1–10. Give each a row with five columns:
   - the question;
   - the chapter it's raised in;
   - the chapter it's answered in;
   - who knows the answer at the time;
   - why they withhold it.

   Expect about a dozen rows, for example: whose door; what pruning is; what Pathwell sold; why the blob came early; who Stansbury is; what the pull is.
2. **Sort each row into one of three types:**
   - **(a) Nobody knows.** This is a genuine mystery. Keep it.
   - **(b) Someone knows and has a reason to hold it back that you could state in one line.** Keep it, but put that reason on the page once.
   - **(c) Someone knows and has no in-world reason.** Convert it, using step 3.
3. **Turn type (c) refusals into trades.** Each time Elizabeth spends something to get an answer (asking twice, getting angry, crashing a car), she gets one true fact and one sharper new question. The rule is that every cost she pays buys something.
4. **Split the answer into layers and spread them out:**
   - **What happened:** a future was spent, and part of it went astray. Give this by Ch 8.
   - **What that made:** Shade. Keep this at Ch 8's end.
   - **Whose choice it was, and that nobody asked for it:** keep this for the van in Ch 10. It's the moral answer, and it should stay the big beat.
5. **Plant the physical tell early.** Shade's right hand opening toward Pathwell is the best tell in the book, and Pathwell has no equivalent before Ch 6. Give Pathwell one small, visible, unexplained physical habit in Ch 1–3 that Elizabeth sees and misreads. Pay it off at the Ch 6 mirror.
6. **Put the decisions on the page.** Elizabeth's choice to leave the bookstore for Camp happens between Ch 3 and Ch 4 and is summarised in one line. Write it as a scene, even a half-page one.
7. **Give Elizabeth a want of her own by Ch 2.** It should be in the present and be more than "go home" or "get answers." Then each chapter has a second question besides "what's the secret": whether she'll get what she wants.

## 3. A model

- **Kazuo Ishiguro, *Never Let Me Go*.** Kathy uses the words "carers," "donors" and "completing" from the first pages, as if we already know them. The reader learns the *shape* of the horror long before the details. The suspense is in watching her understand what we have already pieced together. The move to borrow: release the shape early and hold back the specifics.
- **TJ Klune, *Under the Whispering Door*.** Wallace learns he is dead almost at once, and the reaper and the ferryman tell him plainly. The book doesn't run on "what's going on?" It runs on "what will he do now that he knows?" The move to borrow: give away the premise, and get your suspense from choices.

## 4. Worked example: the ditch (Ch 8)

Current text: the passage quoted in the diagnosis, from "Elizabeth pointed at him. 'The prune.'" to "he went still so abruptly that she stopped talking." Shade's headlights arrive right after.

**ILLUSTRATION** (it shows the move, not final wording):

> Elizabeth pointed at him. "The prune."
>
> Pathwell looked toward Stansbury.
>
> "Not him. You."
>
> He looked at the car, at the hubcap in the grass, at her. "I paid for a job with a future I hadn't finished using. Most of it went where I sent it."
>
> "And the rest?"
>
> "I never found it." His right hand had lifted from his side, palm turning toward the road. He pressed it flat against his coat as if it had spoken out of turn. "That's what I know. The rest is guessing."
>
> "Whose job?"
>
> He opened his mouth.
>
> Headlights came around the bend behind them.

**The choices in it:**

- **Cut:** the beat where Pathwell's glance at Stansbury closes the exchange, and the "went still" stage direction. The glance is now a reflex she overrules ("Not him. You."), so the refusal becomes a beat of her agency.
- **Added: one fact, in Pathwell's register.** A future was spent and part of it went missing. That's the "what happened" layer only.
- **Added: the hand.** It mirrors the tell Shade is about to show. Ten lines later the reader sees Shade's hand do the same thing and makes the connection before anyone says it. The reader is now working on the mystery, not waiting for it.
- **Kept: the interruption,** but moved. It now cuts off the *next* question ("Whose job?"), which stays held for the van. The headlights also answer "And the rest?" visually: the rest just pulled over. The interruption pays out instead of cheating.
- **Protected:** "the van" confession still owns the moral layer ("They did not ask me to prune. I did not ask whether they wanted me to.").

## 5. How to check it worked

- **Run the question ledger on your new draft.** No type-(c) question should stay open longer than about two chapters without either a partial answer or a sharper version of itself.
- **Count refusal lines** ("Later," "Not here," "For now," "I can't yet," "Not while you're driving") in Ch 1–10. Aim for three or fewer, each with a reason the reader can state.
- **Ask of every scene in Ch 1–10:** "What does Elizabeth know when it ends that she didn't know when it began, and what did it cost her?" If the answer is "nothing" twice in a row, the next scene must pay.
- **Cold-reader test:** at the end of Ch 8, ask a reader what they think Shade is. If they have a guess, the planting worked. If they say "no idea," it didn't.

## 6. Traps

- **Over-answering.** If Pathwell explains Shade before Shade arrives, you lose the roadside meeting. Release *what happened*, never *what it made*, before Ch 8 ends.
- **Turning Elizabeth into an interrogator.** If every scene is her demanding answers, she becomes as monotonous as the refusals. Let her get information sideways too: by watching, from Camp people, from objects.
- **Flattening Pathwell's evasions.** His dodges are charming when they carry information. Protect "If I knew that, I'd make worse decisions with more confidence" (Ch 3) and "Just a really weird Tuesday for you" (Ch 2).
- **Spoiling Ch 9.** "Then you were never the point" (Ch 9) depends on Pathwell sincerely believing the blob was after her until then. Don't let early answers reveal that it wasn't.
- **Protect:** the Ch 3 Nana flashback; the archivist scene in Ch 4; the bar chapter's sibling observation (Ch 6: "Stansbury knew which dart Pathwell would reach for before he reached for it"); the Ch 6 mirror scene; the Ch 10 van confession.

# GUIDE 2: Rewrite the loud magic in the book's own register (Ch 1, 3, 5). Worth about +0.5

## 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

Ch 5, the portal:

> He reached behind him and pulled out a handful of folded pages, stacked together in a mess. He gripped them tight, crumpling them in his hand, and a yellow light danced out in every direction from the edges of the paper. The fingers of his left hand turned in a circle, twisting and spinning. He opened that hand wide and snapped it shut, catching the light. A few more twists of his palm, a few flicks of his fingers, and he drew a circle on the ground around them.

Ch 3, the pruning: "Pathwell screamed in a million voices, voices lost to time, burning out to become memories never made. One by one they fell quiet, until all that was left was a harmony of extinguished cries."

What those paragraphs are made of:

- **No identified source.** It's "a handful of folded pages." Whose? What did they say?
- **Generic motion verbs** (danced, twisting, spinning, flicks) and abstract light nouns. Nothing comes from this book's world.
- **Choreography without meaning.** We're told each gesture but not what any of them does.
- **The camera is on the effect,** not on Elizabeth's senses.
- **Large, vague quantities:** "a million voices," "voices lost to time."

Compare the soup page (Ch 4): "Broth. Pepper. Flour dust against the back of a hand." Or the quarantine letter (Ch 11): "Care that meant stay away." There the effect comes straight from what the document meant, and the reader could almost have predicted it.

That's the line between a 6 and a 9. Your loud passages could be moved into any fantasy novel unchanged. Your quiet ones could only exist in this book.

## 2. The technique (at the desk)

1. **Write a working card for every working** (one line each):
   - **Document:** what it is, whose hand, when it was written.
   - **Meaning:** what the writer felt or wanted when they wrote it.
   - **Effect:** how that meaning becomes physical.
   - **Cost:** what is lost.

   If you can't fill in "Meaning," the scene isn't ready to draft.
2. **Make the effect follow from the meaning,** the way you already do with the stay-away letter becoming a wall and the errand list becoming directions. Ask: "If this feeling had a body, what would it push, pull, warm or open?"
3. **Show the reader one line of the document.** One line, as with "MORE IF THEY'RE SICK." That single line does more than a paragraph of light.
4. **Put the camera in Elizabeth's senses.** You've already established that she receives impressions. Give two or three concrete ones from the writer's life (a smell, a place, a bodily feeling) and say that they aren't hers.
5. **Give each sentence one clear spatial action.** If a gesture doesn't change anything, cut it.
6. **Keep a short list of banned words for magic passages:** danced, burst, swirling, wild, million, lost to time, glow (used as a noun), and so on. Allow yourself one.
7. **Budget the words:** cut the description of the effect to about a third, and spend what you save on the document and the impression.
8. **For pruning, where Pathwell spends his own future rather than a document:** make the "document" his unlived possibilities, named concretely. Shade's list in Ch 9 is already the template: "A room in Prague I have never entered. A woman named Celia who hated pears." If Ch 3 burns three or four specific futures that Shade later remembers, the tree becomes setup instead of spectacle.

## 3. A model

- **Susanna Clarke, *Jonathan Strange & Mr Norrell*.** Norrell's first public magic makes the stone figures of York Cathedral speak, and each says something petty, specific and local. Later he makes illusory warships out of rain to scare a blockading fleet. Clarke barely describes the mechanism at all. She describes the odd, concrete *result* and how the witnesses react. The move to borrow: magic is known by its consequences and its particulars, not by its light show.
- **Your own Ch 11–12 museum collapse is a better model than anything I can cite.** Each torn record floods the room with its own feeling ("Someone needed eggs"; "a quartermaster's rather than a battlefield's" panic). Treat that scene as your style sheet.

## 4. Worked example: the portal (Ch 5)

Current text: the Ch 5 paragraph quoted in the diagnosis.

**ILLUSTRATION** (it shows the move, not final wording):

> He pulled a bundle of postcards from his coat, held by a rubber band gone soft. Every one was addressed to the same house in the same careful hand. The top one said WRONG TRAIN AGAIN. STAYING ANYWAY. Pathwell crushed the whole bundle in his fist.
>
> Elizabeth smelled diesel, then sea air, then a bus station at two in the morning. Under it was the particular lift of not knowing where you'd sleep and not minding. None of it was hers. Her feet wanted to walk anyway.
>
> The grass around them flattened in a ring, as if a great many people had just set down their bags.
>
> "Whose were they?"
>
> "A man who never once got where he was going," Pathwell said, "and liked everywhere he ended up."
>
> The wind came up out of the ring.

**The choices in it:**

- **Cut:** all the hand choreography and the "yellow light danced." Nothing in it was doing work.
- **The document follows your own rule for this portal.** Ch 10 says it goes "where we need, not where we'd like." So the paper is a traveller who always ended up somewhere unplanned. The reader now understands *why* they land in a school broom closet, and that's funnier.
- **It's distinct from the grocery lists in Ch 10.** Lists are deliberate, ordered routes; postcards are undirected arrival. That keeps the system consistent without repeating itself.
- **One line of the document:** WRONG TRAIN AGAIN. STAYING ANYWAY.
- **The camera is in her senses,** and it says the feeling isn't hers. That's a light echo of the bookstore panic in Ch 3, so the rule is reinforced.
- **The physical effect comes from the meaning:** the ring of set-down bags.
- **Kept for what follows:** the wind, the hug, the bandana singed in half. That's your stakes beat, and it already works.

**The same method applied to Ch 1:** the blob fight needs one sentence telling us what the sugar-cookie working was *for*. For example, the warmth makes a kitchen-shaped space the rot can't enter. Then her pulling the book splits it visibly. "Don't. Think." then pays off in Ch 14 as the rule it already is, instead of explaining Ch 1 after the fact.

## 5. How to check it worked

- **The four-question test,** for every magic passage. Can a cold reader answer:
  - What paper was it?
  - Whose was it?
  - What did it mean?
  - Why did it do this?

  Four yeses is the bar.
- **The swap test.** Read the passage aloud and ask whether it could go into another fantasy novel unchanged. If it could, it fails.
- **Count** the abstract light and motion words against the concrete nouns from the document's world. Concrete should win by at least two to one.
- **The prediction test.** Show a reader the document line alone and ask what it will do. Getting close is good. You don't want them right every time, but you do want them to feel the logic once it's shown.

## 6. Traps

- **Over-explaining.** One line of document and two impressions is enough. Each working shouldn't become a lecture.
- **Preciousness.** If every document is a heartbreaking letter, the book turns sentimental. Mix in the funny and the mundane (shoe polish, Hess's rain argument, RUTH LIED ABOUT THE LARD).
- **Turning the impression-flood into a formula.** Not every working should hit Elizabeth. Some she should only watch, as she watches the soup page.
- **Losing speed.** In Ch 1 especially, the fix has to be shorter than what it replaces, not longer.
- **Protect:**
  - "Don't. Think." and the twelve-year-old memory in Ch 1;
  - "The cake, somehow, was fine.";
  - "No flash. No burst of gold." (Ch 4);
  - the love note "that reminded her of a boy she knew in middle school" (Ch 3);
  - "Not burned. Spent.";
  - the grocery lists (Ch 10);
  - the quarantine letter and SAM HOME TODAY (Ch 11);
  - the bandana singed in half (Ch 5);
  - the cannon creeping toward the gift shop.

# GUIDE 3: Make Pathwell's third override a new mistake, and give the Shade confession room (Ch 14, 16, 18). Worth about +0.5

## 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

Pathwell overrides an explicit "no" three times, and the scenes share the same skeleton: a warning, Elizabeth's "no," his promise that he can manage it, then disaster. The text even admits the repetition:

- Ch 11: "'I can contain this.'"
- Ch 14: "His fingers tightened. 'I can contain it.' / 'That's what you said at the museum.'"

His stated reasons are also interchangeable: "I am separating the problem" (Ch 11) and "I am trying to make this right" (Ch 14). The Ch 14 scene's only open question is *whether* he'll do it, not *why*. The answer to "why" is the same as last time, so we learn nothing new about him, and he starts to look stuck rather than tragic.

Then the darkest fact in the book arrives in Ch 18:

> "I thought that if the creature took him, it would be finished. The draw. The whole business."
>
> ...
>
> "I could have called someone. I didn't."

About a page later, the scene has moved on to five possible exits and geese that "have learned commerce."

The best tool for fixing this is already planted, in the Ch 6 mirror scene: "His own face in the mirror suddenly felt less like evidence than like an accusation."

A 7 repeats the flaw at a higher price. A 9 shows a deeper layer of the flaw each time, so the late confession feels inevitable in hindsight.

## 2. The technique (at the desk)

1. **Map the three overrides.** For each, write four things: what he says he wants, what he actually wants, who pays, and what the reader learns that's new.
   - **The prune:** he wants to finish a job cleanly; he pays himself.
   - **The museum:** he wants to protect Elizabeth (fear); other people's history pays.
   - **The frame:** right now this repeats "to fix it." It's the row that needs a new answer.
2. **Give the third override a different motive: to stop being accused.** The paternal flaw ("I decide for others") gives way to a selfish one underneath: he wants the evidence of his mistake gone. That's what the mirror planted. The frame is then exactly what the Ch 18 confession describes: an attempt to make Shade not exist as a separate person. The two scenes become one line instead of two surprises.
3. **Put the new motive on the page through someone else, once.** Shade is the right person to name it. For example, he can point out that Pathwell isn't trying to fix the draw, only to stop seeing his own face. Pathwell doesn't deny it. Name it once only.
4. **Give Elizabeth one moment where she sees it.** She has seen the mirror moment's aftermath at the bar. One line of recognition is enough.
5. **Move the confession into the Ch 16 apology,** where his list of wrongs already contains "Shade." Let the list break at that name. Then Elizabeth's existing lines ("Those aren't one thing," "I need you not to make me responsible for whether you become a better person") answer the worst thing he did, and they gain weight.
6. **Change Ch 18 to fit.** Without the confession, Ch 18's beat becomes "Say his name" with nothing new revealed, or Pathwell saying "Shade" unprompted, which shows growth. Then add one full beat of silence or action before the exits and geese.
7. **Decide what Elizabeth feels, and show it with one physical action** (standing up, putting down the bowl, walking away). Don't explain it.

## 3. A model

- **Kazuo Ishiguro, *The Remains of the Day*.** Stevens keeps returning to the same failure, his loyalty to Lord Darlington and his reserve with Miss Kenton. Each return peels back one more layer: professional pride, then complicity, then the love he never admitted. When he finally says, briefly, that his heart was breaking, it's devastating because every earlier scene narrowed toward it. The move to borrow: each recurrence of the flaw shows a deeper motive, never the same one again.
- **Ursula K. Le Guin, *A Wizard of Earthsea*.** Ged is hunted by a shadow he released, and the ending makes it whole by naming it with his own name. That's a useful contrast for you. Pathwell tries to write the Earthsea ending (rejoin the shadow and make it his), and your book says no, because Shade has become a person. Knowing that's the familiar resolution lets you have Shade point out that this ending has been written before, and refuse it.

## 4. Worked example: the Ch 16 apology

Current text:

> "I am sorry," he said.
>
> ...
>
> "For which part?" she asked.
>
> He looked at her first, then toward the archive. "Your apartment. Your diary, which I read. Your grandmother's book, which I came looking for and would have spent without asking. The museum. Your shoulder. The working. Shade. The archive."
>
> "Those aren't one thing."

**ILLUSTRATION** (it shows the move, not final wording):

> "For which part?" she asked.
>
> "Your apartment. Your diary, which I read. Your grandmother's book. The museum. Your shoulder. The working." He stopped.
>
> She waited for the next word. It didn't come.
>
> "Shade," she said.
>
> "He was calling for help." Pathwell's eyes stayed on the trampled ground past the frame. "I was close enough. I thought if it took him, the draw would end, and I wouldn't have to—" He didn't finish that either. "I could have called someone. I didn't."
>
> Somewhere behind her, Milo's broom handle scraped the dirt. Elizabeth found she was standing.
>
> "Those aren't one thing," she said. Her voice came out level, which frightened her more than shouting would have. "Don't make them one thing because one apology is easier."

**The choices in it:**

- **The list breaks at Shade.** Ch 16's original list rolls past "Shade" in a run of nouns, which is what an evasive man would do. Breaking the list there shows the guilt through the shape of the sentence, not through an explanation.
- **Elizabeth says the name.** That sets up the Ch 18 reversal, where Pathwell can say it himself without being asked.
- **"Wouldn't have to—" is left unfinished.** It points back to the mirror ("an accusation") without stating the motive outright. A reader who has done the Guide 3 Ch 14 revision will finish the sentence for themselves.
- **One physical reaction, without explanation:** she's standing. Plus one sensory anchor from Camp (Milo's broom), which keeps the scene grounded and reminds us who she chose to save.
- **"Those aren't one thing" is reused** and now answers something unbearable. Her next lines in the current draft ("I need you not to make me responsible...", "You don't get to use being funny...") follow unchanged and land harder.
- **Cut:** "which I came looking for and would have spent without asking." It's good, but it cushions the list. That admission can move to Ch 18, where a smaller one fits.

## 5. How to check it worked

- **The blank test.** For each override scene, complete the sentence "This time he does it because ___." If any two blanks match, revise.
- **The hindsight test.** After reading Ch 18, a reader should be able to say "of course" about the Shade confession and point to the mirror and the frame as the reasons. Ask a reader whether it surprised them. You want "yes, but I should have seen it."
- **The spacing test.** Count lines between the confession and the next joke. Leave at least the rest of the scene. If a joke comes within twenty lines, it should be Elizabeth's and should be clearly a coping mechanism.
- **The sympathy check.** Read Ch 14 aloud and ask whether you'd still want to read a second book with Pathwell in it. If not, add back one moment of his self-knowledge, not an excuse.

## 6. Traps

- **Making him a villain.** The selfish motive is one layer, not his whole self. Keep his wit and his restitution scenes.
- **Over-explaining the motive.** Shade names it once; nobody repeats it.
- **The confession turning into a self-flagellating monologue.** Keep it under five sentences. Pathwell's guilt should be blunt, not eloquent.
- **Sliding into forgiveness.** The book's refusal to forgive him on schedule is one of its best decisions. Protect it.
- **Protect:**
  - "Same arrogance in a better coat."
  - "You still think the result gets to decide whether the choice was acceptable."
  - "I am trying to make this right." / "For who?"
  - "Don't make him the reason." (Shade releasing Stansbury from blame, Ch 14)
  - Stansbury's "Yes." to "I did that." (Ch 11)
  - "Say his name" (adjust as above)
  - The geese ending. It can stay once the confession has moved.

# Revision order

1. **Guide 3 first (Pathwell's motive).** It decides what the whole book is planting. Once the third override is driven by "remove the accusation," you know what Ch 1–8 must seed: the mirror, the hand, the unease about his own reflection. Doing the opening first would mean planting for a motive you later change.
2. **Guide 1 second (the opening act).** With the motive settled, build the question ledger and move information and tells into place. This step will move, merge or cut scenes, including possibly some magic set pieces.
3. **Guide 2 last (the loud magic).** This is scene- and sentence-level work, and the scenes it touches (Ch 1, 3, 5) may change shape in step 2. Polishing a set piece that then gets cut is wasted effort. It also benefits from step 1: the Ch 3 tree can burn the specific futures Shade later remembers, which you only know once his role is fixed.

# Practice exercise for this week

**Rewrite Ch 1, from "A thud hit the front door. Wet. Heavy." to "Then nothing."**

This scene tests all three guides at once:
- **Guide 1:** plant Pathwell's tell, and give Elizabeth one choice of her own.
- **Guide 2:** make the sugar-cookie working legible.
- **Guide 3:** he came for a cookbook and would have spent it without asking, so this is the first instance of the flaw. Make sure what he wants here can be stated, and is different from the frame's motive later.

**What success looks like** after a cold reader finishes the new Ch 1:
1. They can say in one sentence what the recipe page was doing and why it broke when she pulled the book.
2. They noticed one odd thing about Pathwell's body or attention that the chapter doesn't explain.
3. They can name one thing Elizabeth chose rather than had done to her.
4. The cake ("ELIZ"), "Don't. Think.", the Nana memory and "The cake, somehow, was fine." all survive.
5. The new version is no longer than the old one.

If all five hold, the approach works, and you can apply it to the rest of the book.
