# Working guide: the three highest-value fixes from "gap to a 9"

I used only the manuscript. I did not write any files, because file writes are blocked. The three items below are the ones I scored highest in my own gap list. Numbers are my estimates and are not additive. Quoted lines from your manuscript are exact. Quotations from published books are kept to a minimum or left out; where I am describing a scene from memory, I say what I am sure of.

Revisiting item 1 honestly: in the first report I wrote "by Ch3". On re-reading my notes, the book's engine (potential spent as payment, the copy that is not the thing, the habit of asking first) is already on the page in Ch3 and Ch4. What is missing in Ch1 to Ch2 is Elizabeth's will and a comic voice that comes from the world. What drags is Ch5. So item 1 is narrower than I made it sound, and cheaper.

The three items:
1. **Rebuild the first act so Elizabeth drives it and every scene pays** (about +1.0). Mostly: halve Ch5, tighten Ch1 to Ch2.
2. **Give the book a narrator and make the world a joke engine** (about +0.8).
3. **Dramatize Pathwell's flaw earlier, and cut the stated theme** (about +0.6).

## Guide 1: Rebuild the first act so every scene pays and Elizabeth drives

### 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

Ch1 to Ch4 work. Ch1 is a hook with a real image (the page, Nana's voice). Ch2 has the best "thing she loses" beat (the dry-cleaning ticket). Ch3 introduces the ledger, the cost and the cat. Ch4 delivers the archive and the decision to give up the cookbook. These scenes each change something for Elizabeth.

Ch5 is where the first act sags. Mechanically, from the portal to the Ken scene it is made of:
- Pathwell acts, Elizabeth objects, he deflects, nothing is decided.
- The camera stays on the two of them in a space (closet, hallway) with nothing in it that teaches the reader a rule.
- The same joke is run in several costumes: Pathwell guesses at safety. Quote: "'What do you mean, 'looks clear'? Where did you take me? Are we not supposed to be here?'" then "'Air's fine.'" then "'Fine compared to what?' Elizabeth yelled in a whisper." Then the "I don't know" run in the hallway.
- The magic is shown as spectacle with no rule: "A few more twists of his palm, a few flicks of his fingers, and he drew a circle on the ground around them."

Why this reads as a 6: each beat is individually pleasant and none changes anything. If you cut any one of them, the scene is unchanged. In a 9, cutting any beat leaves a hole. Nothing in the portal or closet teaches the reader something they will need later, so the scene is entertainment, not engine.

There is a good scene inside the bad stretch: the Ken exchange. Pathwell says "Oh, there's no need for that" and "began to reach into the back of his coat." Elizabeth steps in with "I'm the missus." That is the book's whole dynamic in miniature: he reaches for the clever, unilateral fix; she solves it with a person. Protect it and give it room.

### 2. The technique (what to do at the desk)

1. **Make a scene card for each scene in Ch1 to Ch7.** One index card or one spreadsheet row per scene (a scene is a stretch with one location and one goal). Three fields:
   - Elizabeth wants (one verb phrase).
   - At the end she has gained or lost: (one phrase, concrete).
   - The reader learns this about the engine: cost / copy-versus-original / asking, or "nothing".
2. **Mark every card with a blank or a repeat.** A repeat is a card whose third field says the same thing as an earlier card. Ch5's field-sleep, portal and closet cards will say "nothing". The swords-in-the-parking-lot card says something new (children's work energy is sold, and "Nobody's future goes into mine"). The drive card repeats Ch3 ("Future. He pays with it") and should be a short reminder, not a re-explanation.
3. **Start each surviving scene at its turn.** Find the one sentence where something changes. Cut everything before it, and re-supply any needed fact in one clause. For Ch5 the turn is the hallway full of children's art, not the circle on the grass.
4. **Collapse a run of the same joke to one use plus its capper.** Count the "I don't know" and "looks clear" exchanges in the hallway. Keep the one with the best last line ("I'm going to run out of ways to say 'I don't know'") and cut the others.
5. **Give Elizabeth a verb in every scene.** She must do something that changes the outcome: lie, drive, refuse, choose. The Ken scene has it. The closet and hallway do not (she hisses and follows). Where she can't act, give her a discovery she makes with her own senses and which Pathwell does not explain to her.
6. **Show a rule before the character says it.** If Stansbury's explanation in the parking lot ("I harness the latent magic energy created by the children's work") can be preceded by the reader seeing or feeling it in the hallway, you can halve the dialogue explanation.
7. **Check the laugh source in each scene.** Where the scene's only laugh is Pathwell's patter, replace it with one that comes from a thing in the room.

### 3. A model

**Good Omens, the opening on the wall of Eden.** In one scene between two characters, the book establishes its engine: an angel and a demon who have gone native and like humans more than their own sides. The move: each line of dialogue changes what the other is willing to say next, so the scene is a negotiation, and the rule is delivered as a confession. The angel admits he gave away his flaming sword; that single fact, told lightly, is the book's premise (these two will not follow orders). Nothing is spectacle. I am sure of this scene and of its function; I am not quoting it.

**A Wizard's Guide to Defensive Baking (Kingfisher)** is a good secondary model for the same job. In its first chapters its heroine does one small, specific, slightly absurd thing with her magic (it animates dough), and that thing is both the joke and the thing the plot will later need. I'm confident of that overall shape and not of line-level detail. The move: the first magical act is small enough to be funny and important enough to be Chekhov's gun.

### 4. Worked example

**Current text (Ch5, after the portal):**

"'What do you mean, 'looks clear'? Where did you take me? Are we not supposed to be here?'
Pathwell shrugged. 'Don't know. But it looks clear, and we aren't going to figure out much standing in here.'
He opened the door and stepped out, took a few short, quick breaths, sniffing the air, then leaned back in. 'Air's fine.'
'Fine compared to what?' Elizabeth yelled in a whisper."

**Technique applied:** steps 3, 4, 5 and 6.

**ILLUSTRATION (about 100 words, not final wording):**

>
> "Stop that," Elizabeth whispered.
>

**Explanation of choices.**
- Cut: "looks clear", "Air's fine", "Fine compared to what?", and the guess that it is an elementary school. Four beats of one joke, none carrying information.
- Added: the warm drawings. The reader feels Stansbury's racket before anyone explains it, so the parking-lot explanation can be half as long.
- Added: Elizabeth does the discovering. She holds her palm an inch away instead of touching, which keeps the "ask first" habit (the book's own motif) on the page without a word about it.
- Kept: the bell, which ends the scene on a turn and brings the children.
- Laugh source: "eleven fingers" is the decorative laugh; "like being given a trophy for sitting still" is the world laugh, and it is the one with a meaning.
- Withheld: "Practically, they're my brother's" leaves a question that the Ken scene and the car answer. The reader leans forward.

### 5. How to check it worked

- **The scene-card test.** Every surviving scene must have a non-blank third field that is different from every card before it. Count blanks; target zero in Ch1 to Ch7.
- **The cut test.** Delete the scene and read the next one. If the reader is lost, the scene is needed; if not, cut it. Do this one scene at a time.
- **Word budget.** My rough estimate is that Ch5 from the portal to the departure from the school is about 1,500 words. A target of about 700 to 800 for the same material is realistic.
- **Count Elizabeth's verbs.** For each scene, write the verb she performs. If it is "follows", "listens" or "objects", rewrite until it is "lies", "touches", "chooses" or "refuses".

### 6. Traps, and what to protect

- **Trap: cutting the connective tissue that makes the book feel inhabited.** The Ch6 bar scene looks like the sort of scene this method would cut ("nothing happens"). It is the opposite: it plants the brothers' silent care (moving the beer), the mirror, and "Don't confuse restraint with virtue." Run the scene cards and notice that its third field is full.
- **Trap: turning everything into plot.** The book's warmth lives in scenes where little happens. Do not cut the fish scene, the archive's "Specificity is expensive", or the diner.
- **Trap: making Elizabeth a superhero.** She gets to drive; she does not have to be right. The ditch works because it is a bad idea she chooses.
- **Protect:** the Ken exchange ("I'm the missus"), Stansbury's racket and "Nobody's future goes into mine", the car ride's "Future... Not years, exactly", Ch6 in full, Ch4 in full.

## Guide 2: A comic narrator, and a world that makes its own jokes

### 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

Nearly all the comedy in Ch1 to Ch10 is dialogue: two speakers, one setup line, one capper. The narration mostly reports action and Elizabeth's sensations. When it does something funny it is almost always in Ch4 onward, and it comes from Elizabeth's eye, not a narrator who knows how this world works.

Look at the Ch3 counter scene. The shopkeeper names a price:

"'We could settle today, past bills and all, for… a Rai Stone.'
'Oh, come on. Be serious. A Rai Stone?'
'I'm afraid I am.' The shopkeeper removed his glasses. 'And quite frankly, Mr. Pathwell, you already owe more than I allow most.'
'Surely we can barter here.'
'Outside of a significant Rai Stone, I could do it for, say, thirty Lydian coins.'
'Okay, now you're just messing with me.'"

Mechanically: both speakers use real-world currencies the reader must recognize (the island of Yap's stone money, the ancient Lydian coin). The joke is an allusion. A reader who knows them gets a nod; a reader who does not gets nothing; and either way the exchange tells you nothing about how payment works in this place. There is no rule underneath, so the reader cannot predict anything, so the next price cannot surprise them. Then, three paragraphs later, the same scene's narration swings to epic register with no comic undertow: "Pathwell screamed in a million voices, voices lost to time, burning out to become memories never made."

Why this reads as a 6 or 7: the best lines in this scene ("He brought outside property into an open transaction, and I dislike paperwork", "Will that be paper or plastic?") are funny because they are in-world and deadpan. The weaker ones borrow from outside. And no narrator is in the room to turn the shopkeeper's rule into a generalization about institutions. In Pratchett and Adams, the narration is where the world's logic is stated at its most deadpan; here the narration is silent at the moment it should speak.

### 2. The technique

1. **Write a rule card for each of the book's three institutions** (the Space Between, Camp, the museum). Five lines each: what is accepted; who sets the price; what is never accepted; what the keeper cannot do; what the petty penalty is. For the Space Between you already know most of it: payment is in things that cannot be replaced (potential, original records); outside property in an open transaction is a paperwork problem; the cat takes a tribute.
2. **Apply the grand rule at the smallest scale.** For each rule, write the most petty, bureaucratic consequence you can. This is the move behind "I dislike paperwork" and "Your account remains at zero... A month is not a character trait." The grand fact (you pay with your future) meets a small fact (the shopkeeper has a form).
3. **Put one rule in the narration per scene, as a deadpan fact.** Structure it in three beats: the rule, an absurdly precise detail, a human consequence. Keep it to one to three sentences, then return to the scene.
4. **Give the narrator permission to generalize, with a limit.** The Ch5 town paragraph ("You would be forgiven for not stopping in the town...") is the model. House rules: at most three asides a chapter; each under about 60 words; each moves from this scene to "how people or institutions are"; each followed immediately by plot; none in an emotional peak.
5. **Replace outside-world allusions with in-world derivations.** Wherever a joke depends on the reader knowing something from our world (Rai Stone, Lydian coin, Bakhtak), ask: what would the equivalent be in a world where payment is irreplaceable things? Then make the joke a consequence of the rule.
6. **Move setup from dialogue to narration to halve the exchange.** For each exchange of four or more short lines ending on a capper, ask whether one narration sentence could carry the setup and leave the capper alone. This also thins the "one voice" problem, because narration is a different voice.

### 3. A model

**Small Gods (Pratchett).** The book states one rule early and plainly: gods run on belief, and a god with few believers is weak. Every subsequent joke in the world's institutions (the Church's cruelty, the god trapped in a tortoise) and the plot itself are consequences of that rule. The move: one economy, stated once, deadpan, then applied at every scale from cosmic to petty. I am confident of this structure. 

**Going Postal (Pratchett).** The old Post Office is full of decades of undelivered mail, and the book treats the backlog as a physical, slightly haunted condition of the building (the letters are not silent). The move: take an institutional failure (a backlog) and make it literal and atmospheric, then make the protagonist's job depend on it. That is almost exactly your archive-and-unsent-letters material, played comically. I am confident of the premise; I am not quoting it.

### 4. Worked example

**Current text (Ch3):** the Rai Stone and Lydian coin exchange quoted above.

**Technique applied:** steps 2, 3 and 5.

**ILLUSTRATION (about 105 words, not final wording):**

> The Space Between accepted payment in anything that could not be replaced, which is a stricter standard than it sounds. Money could be replaced, so it was refused with the faint distaste of a hotel being offered a sandwich. Pathwell's account had passed the point where the shopkeeper stopped writing numbers and started writing adjectives.
>
> "Today's balance," the shopkeeper said, "is settled by one significant item."
>
> "How significant?"
>
> "Past bills and all?" He removed his glasses. "Something you'll miss."
>
> Pathwell looked at his own right hand as if it had been mentioned.

**Explanation of choices.**
- Cut: Rai Stone, Lydian coins, "Be serious", "you're just messing with me". They are outside-world winks, and the haggling is a sitcom beat that does not change anything.
- Added: one narrator rule ("payment in anything that could not be replaced"). It tells the reader how this economy works, so every later price becomes a hook and a possible joke, and the Ch3 ritual that follows is now a consequence rather than a surprise.
- Added: the hotel-and-sandwich simile gives the narrator an attitude, and "started writing adjectives" is the petty bureaucratic consequence of a grand rule (step 2).
- Kept: the glasses, "past bills and all" and the shopkeeper's deadpan. Those were the in-world parts.
- Added: Pathwell glances at his right hand. It foreshadows what the payment will be and replaces the haggling beat with something physical.

### 5. How to check it worked

- **Narration-only read.** Read only the narration paragraphs of Ch1 to Ch5, skipping dialogue. Count how many make you smile. My count on first read was about two (the cake, the Ch5 town paragraph). Aim for at least two per chapter.
- **Joke-source tally.** For each laugh in a chapter, tag it: C (a character's idiom), W (a world rule), N (the narrator). Aim for at least one of each per chapter from Ch3 onward.
- **Allusion test.** Underline every joke that depends on knowledge from outside the book. Each should be either replaced or paired with an in-world version that works for a reader who doesn't know the reference.
- **Rule prediction test.** Ask a friend, after Ch3, what they think Pathwell will have to pay for the next big thing. If they can guess, the rule is on the page.

### 6. Traps, and what to protect

- **Trap: pastiche.** Do not add footnotes or "one" constructions to sound like Pratchett. Your narrator should sound like the one in the Ch5 town paragraph, which is plain and warm.
- **Trap: over-explaining.** A rule stated twice stops being a joke. State once; show consequences.
- **Trap: silliness that kills cost.** If the Space Between becomes a theme park of bureaucratic gags, the Ch3 ritual and the Ch14 pruning stop mattering. Keep one rule that is never funny: the payment really is lost.
- **Trap: narrator interrupting grief.** No asides in Ch3's Nana flashback, Ch11's "Someone needed eggs", Ch15's sock, or Ch16's funeral lines.
- **Protect:** the shopkeeper's "He brought outside property into an open transaction, and I dislike paperwork"; "Will that be paper or plastic?" / "Surprise me."; the Ch4 line about Camp repairs ("someone who believed replacement was a personal failure"); the archivist's "Specificity is expensive"; the Ch5 town paragraph; the Merritt cookbook margins in Ch18, which are the book's best world-made jokes.

## Guide 3: Show Pathwell's flaw early, and stop naming the theme

### 1. Diagnosis, mechanically

Pathwell's flaw is deciding for other people because he is sure he knows the cleanest way. The text names it first in Ch9, through Shade: "Not that he was right... That he had reached the point where being uncertain no longer mattered to him." Elizabeth names it again in Ch10 (the van) and Ch14 (the pins) and Mama Baga names it in Ch16.

Before Ch9, the flaw is on the page but is framed as charm. Examples: "Your house has now been marked. Magically speaking." "Shoes. Now. I did say please." He takes her diary and cookbook. He buys Camp's books with his future without asking. The camera treats each as a comic imposition on Elizabeth. The one place a cost lands on someone else is Ch4: "'We did not ask for this.' / 'You needed it.'" and "You appointed yourself a delivery." / "It makes me correct." That scene is exactly right and is rare in the first half.

Then look at how the theme is delivered in the middle and late book. In the van (Ch10):

"'He thinks you'll decide for him.'
Pathwell shifted in the passenger seat. 'I heard him.'
'That's not the same as listening.'"

and a few lines later:

"Then she said, 'And paying for it yourself made you feel like the method was yours to choose.'"
"Finally Pathwell said, 'At the time, I would have called that responsibility.'"
"'What do you call it now?'
His hand tightened around the peas. 'I don't know yet.'"

And in Ch14: "'You still think the result gets to decide whether the choice was acceptable.'"

Mechanically: a character summarizes in an abstract sentence what the reader has just seen. The nouns are "method", "responsibility", "choice", "decide". The camera stays on faces and speech. The peas are doing real work (his thumb presses a dent into the bag), but the lines around them tell the reader what to conclude.

Why this reads as 7 not 9: the beats are right, the material is strong, and the delivery is argument. A 9 lets the reader do the concluding. It also cheats you out of the cost: when Pathwell overrides every "no" in Ch14, the reader has seen him impose on Elizabeth and Camp but has seen only one earlier cost to someone else (Ch4), so the Ch14 refusal reads partly as a plot lever and partly as tragedy.

### 2. The technique

1. **Inventory his unilateral decisions in Ch1 to Ch9.** One line each, with who bears the cost and whether the cost is visible on the page. From my read: marking her house and removing her (Ch1, cost: her home, shown); taking the diary and cookbook (Ch1 to Ch3, cost: her trust, shown); loading Camp with books (Ch3 to Ch4, cost: Camp's reaction, shown, good); "You can stay here" (Ch4); the appletini order (Ch6, no cost); withholding what he found in the mirror (Ch6 to Ch8, cost: Elizabeth's right to know, shown through the ditch); "You're not going with him" (Ch8).
2. **Build an escalating chain, with a different person bearing the cost each time.** The chain is Camp (Ch4, small) then Elizabeth (Ch8, medium: she has to crash a car) then the museum's records (Ch11, large) then the archive and Shade (Ch14, largest). The gap is Ch5 to Ch10, where the only cost is Elizabeth's irritation. Add one mid-weight cost to someone else in that stretch. The Ken scene (Ch5) is the natural place: he starts to reach into his coat for a magical fix, and the cost of that fix, had it happened, is visible on someone who is not him (an unnamed guard).
3. **Plant one physical tell and use it three times.** The book already has it. In Ch6: "Pathwell moved Stansbury's beer six inches back from the edge of the bar without looking." In Ch16: "Once he turned a box so its label faced out, looked at it, and turned it back." That gesture, small quiet management of other people's things, is the flaw in miniature. Plant it once in Ch1 to Ch3 (he straightens or moves something of hers), keep Ch6, use it in the Ch10 van (his hand on the peas), and then pay it off in Ch16 and Ch17. The reader who has seen it three times will read the Ch14 pin as the same gesture at scale, with no sentence to say so.
4. **Run the theme-sentence highlighter.** Print the chapters. Highlight in one color every sentence in which a character uses an abstract theme noun (choice, decide, responsibility, certainty, ask, method, result). Then sort the highlighted lines:
   - Keep if it is in the speaker's own idiom about a concrete thing (Shade's "Pockets", Mama Baga's "I didn't ask what would help").
   - Keep if it is the single best statement of the idea (Shade's Ch9 line). Allow one per act.
   - Cut or replace if it summarizes what the reader just watched happen.
5. **Replace each cut with an object or a question.** For each cut summary, find the concrete thing already in the scene (the peas, the pins, the sling, the dumpling) and let it carry the beat, or have the other character ask a concrete question ("Who else?") that the reader answers silently.
6. **Cut the response line, not the revelation.** In the van, the revelation is Pathwell's "I did not ask whether they wanted me to." Keep that. Cut Elizabeth's tidy interpretation after it.

### 3. A model

**Emma (Austen).** In the first chapter, Emma takes the credit for having made a match, and the narrator lets her be charming and mostly right. Her flaw, managing other people's lives because she is sure she knows best, is planted as a pleasure. It escalates through failures that each cost a different person (Harriet, then Miss Bates). The reckoning at Box Hill is a single specific cruelty followed by a brief correction from Knightley, not a lecture on Emma's character. The move: plant the flaw as a charm, escalate with a new victim each time, and make the climactic moment an act with a witness rather than a summary. I am confident of this structure.

### 4. Worked example

**Current text (Ch10, the van):**

"Then she said, 'And paying for it yourself made you feel like the method was yours to choose.'
Pathwell looked at her, and didn't answer. Elizabeth waited. Stansbury did too.
Finally Pathwell said, 'At the time, I would have called that responsibility.'
'What do you call it now?'
His hand tightened around the peas. 'I don't know yet.'"

**Technique applied:** steps 4, 5 and 6.

**ILLUSTRATION (about 70 words, not final wording):**

> "Who else paid?" Elizabeth asked.
>
> "I did."
>
> "That isn't what I asked."
>
> Pathwell opened his mouth for the answer he had been using for two centuries and found it wasn't there. He turned the bag of peas over. They had thawed into one cold lump.
>
> "Nobody was asked," he said.
>
> In the front seat Stansbury kept his eyes on the windshield, which was how she knew he'd always known.

**Explanation of choices.**
- Cut: "the method was yours to choose" (Elizabeth supplies the thesis), "responsibility" and "What do you call it now?" (an abstract question inviting an abstract answer). They told the reader what to conclude and gave the scene a second ending.
- Added: "Who else paid?" It is concrete, and the reader answers it silently (the person at the kitchen table, Shade, Camp). That is the moment the reader does the work.
- Kept: the peas. They were already the right object; "one cold lump" gives the object an image of what happened to something once held.
- Kept: Stansbury's eyes on the windshield, from the original ("Stansbury kept his eyes on the windshield"). I added the consequence that the reader learns he had known, which sets up Ch14's confrontation.
- Added: "Nobody was asked." It names the theme, but in Pathwell's mouth, after he has been made to see it, shorter than the line before it. That is the one stated line I would allow in this scene.
- Dropped: Elizabeth waiting and Stansbury waiting, a stage direction that told us the pause was heavy.

### 5. How to check it worked

- **The silent-film test.** Reread the scene with all dialogue deleted. Do you still know who was hurt and who knew? If yes, the dialogue can be trimmed.
- **The highlighter count.** Per chapter, count theme nouns spoken by characters. A rough target: no more than two per chapter in Ch9 to Ch16, and none that explain what the previous paragraph showed.
- **The chain test.** List Pathwell's unilateral acts with the person who bears each cost. No two consecutive costs should land on the same person, and each should be larger than the last.
- **The tell count.** Find the beer-moving gesture in three places before Ch14. If a reader cannot recall it by Ch14, plant it once more.

### 6. Traps, and what to protect

- **Trap: making Pathwell unlikeable.** He needs to stay charming and often right, the way Emma is right about Miss Taylor. If every early imposition fails, there is no temptation in the Ch14 choice.
- **Trap: front-loading costs and killing the comedy.** One mid-weight cost in Ch5 to Ch8 is enough. Do not turn Ch5 to Ch8 into a series of small tragedies.
- **Trap: leaving Elizabeth as the only one harmed.** The chain works because costs land on others (Camp, the museum, Shade).
- **Trap: over-cutting the statement.** Do not cut every spoken statement of the theme. Shade's "Not that he was right..." is the best one in the book and should stay. Cut the ones that restate it.
- **Protect:** Ch4's "You appointed yourself a delivery." / "It makes me correct."; the Ch6 beer gesture; the ditch ("That seemed reasonable at the time." / "So did the ditch."); Ch16's "Don't make them one thing because one apology is easier."; the Ch17 rack-and-notebook sequence, which is the payoff in comic form; Ch11's "I did that." / "Yes."; Ch18's "Say his name."

## Revision order

1. **Guide 1 first (scene cards and cuts in Ch1 to Ch7, mainly halving Ch5).** It decides which scenes exist. Doing the other two first would mean polishing scenes you may cut. It also makes the next steps cheaper: a shorter first act has room for new material, and you will know where the plants can go.
2. **Guide 3 second (the chain, the tell, the theme highlighter).** Once the scene list is fixed you can place the planted costs and the gesture in scenes that will survive. The highlighter pass in Ch9 to Ch16 comes after the plants, because only once the reader has seen the flaw can you safely cut the sentences that explain it.
3. **Guide 2 last (the narrator and rule cards).** This is a line-level pass over the final text. It depends on the first two because it adds a voice on top of a stable structure, and it is the easiest to overdo if you do it early.

If you have time for only one of the three, do Guide 1; it is also the cheapest.

**Rewrite Ch5 from the closet door to the end of the Ken scene and Stansbury's "Maroon car by the loading dock. Give me twenty minutes."** By my rough count that stretch is about 1,500 words now.

**Success looks like:**
- About 700 to 800 words, by my estimate of the current length.
- The scene begins at the hallway of children's art, not at the circle on the grass or inside the closet.
