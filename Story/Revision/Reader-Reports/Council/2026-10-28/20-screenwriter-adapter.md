# Adapter's report: 6-episode limited series

Reader: screenwriter/adapter. Reading condition: cold, whole book (18 chapters, about 45,800 words, read in order in 400-line chunks). I opened only the manuscript file. After finishing I ran one grep on the same file to count a few repeated phrases (counts below); I did not use it to read ahead. No other file, folder or site was opened.

---

## 1. Logline

A grieving woman, three weeks into a new apartment she can't unpack, is dragged into a hidden world of cunning folk and a bookstore that trades in other people's handwriting by a charming thief who sold his own future to keep a camp alive. To learn what is hunting him she has to decide what she will give up, and what he never learned to.

Shorter, for a pitch deck: *A thief spends other people's memories to fix his mistakes. The woman whose grandmother's recipe he burns has to teach him to stop deciding for everyone else.*

---

## 2. The spine

**Her want.** This is the book's first problem. On the page Elizabeth wants, in order: to get her things back (Ch 1-3), to understand why "something impossible had come through her door" (Ch 4), to stop being "the driver" while two men explain around her (Ch 7), and to make Pathwell say what he chose (Ch 9-10). Under all that is a want the book never states but I'd build the series on: she wants to keep Nana, and "Nana" has become a cookbook, a diary and a spoon. The first thing the magic does is give her Nana's voice. Ch 2 ends on "He still has it", and that sentence is about the voice, not the cookbook.

**The opposition.** Two layers, and only one of them is a character.
- *Plot opposition:* the black cleanup creatures. They have no face, voice or agenda, and I never felt a person behind them.
- *Real opposition:* Pathwell. His reflex is "I can contain this", "quickest, cleanest", and deciding the price on someone else's behalf. Shade is that reflex given a body.

**What the climax decides.** The decisive scene is Ch 14-15. Pathwell is told no by Shade, Mama Baga, Stansbury and Elizabeth in turn, and forces the rejoining frame anyway. Elizabeth, in the same fire, walks past her diary and Nana's cookbook to lift a shelf off a child with a dislocated shoulder: "That was the choice. / Everything else burned afterward." The two arcs mirror each other cleanly. He can't let go of control; she can let go of objects. That mirror is the best thing in the book's architecture, and it's strong enough to carry a series.

**A structural problem the series would have to fix.** The point-of-view character (Elizabeth) is not the character who changes most. Pathwell changes: the restitution chapters, the catalog he throws away, the yellow wagon he leaves yellow. Elizabeth mostly witnesses, judges and chooses well. She chooses a lot: the Cadillac in the ditch, the diner, "Camp", Milo. But those are mostly acts of refusal. A series needs her to have a thing she's trying to *get*.

## 3. What I'd cut (before anything else)

I would not cut a chapter wholesale, because most chapters carry one irreplaceable beat. But I'd gut these for screen:

1. **Ch 13 (Hearts / coffee / pockets) down to one scene.** About 750 lines of Shade becoming adorable. It matters (Shade must become a person for his loss to land), but it's four scenes doing one job: coffee, jacket, cards, onions. Keep the card table and "I know Hearts" / "I don't remember playing." Fold the jacket into it. Cut the rest. In a series this is where an audience checks their phone, because the pressure of the plot has been off for a whole chapter and a half (Ch 12-13) with no new question being asked.
2. **Ch 17 (restitution) down to about 10 minutes.** It's charming and the right emotional answer to Mama Baga's "What do you owe?", but it's a montage written as chapters: rack, transcription, apartment, museum statement, catalog, wagon, family arrives, museum reopens, wagon moves in. On screen, keep (a) the Henry Vale letters he nearly offers as replacement and withdraws ("They are not Daniel's"), (b) the welcome speech with runemasters remembered, (c) "Label crooked." Cut the apartment unpacking and the walk.
3. **Ch 10, first half.** The brothers walking, the "next time I call you an idiot, you smack me right in the face" bit, the peas. Keep the unsent letter ("If I use this, it's gone." / "It makes the cost mine to approve.") and the grocery-list trick, which is a lovely rule-of-the-world scene. The slapping gag is the book at its most indulgent.
4. **Ch 5, school portal.** The Ken/walkie-talkie/"Mrs. Pathwell" sequence is farce and fun, but it's the book stopping to do a bit. Keep Stansbury's entrance ("Maroon car by the loading dock. Give me twenty minutes.") and the foam-sword demonstration. Drop the corridor wandering. The weekday joke (Saturday / Friday) is worth one line.
5. **Ch 6, bar.** Keep it, but halve it. The fish/batter/"historically" banter is a fine two-hander for two actors who like each other, and I'd keep Stansbury's "You didn't ask for them" and the mirror. The rest of the bar conversation repeats what Ch 5 already established about the brothers.
6. **The trailing end of Ch 16.** Pathwell's apology, Mama Baga's tribunal, Stansbury's scar temptation and the night-time grief are each strong. Together they are four scenes of one note (the accounting), stacked after the climax. In my cut, the tribunal and the scar scene live; the formal apology and the Shade eulogy get merged.

I'd also cut the **therapy-register dialogue** wherever it appears. Examples:
- "And paying for it yourself made you feel like the method was yours to choose." (Ch 10)
- "You still think the result gets to decide whether the choice was acceptable." (Ch 14)
- "I need you not to make me responsible for whether you become a better person." (Ch 16)

These are all *good lines*. Together they mean the book says its theme out loud in every act. On screen, an actor's face does this for free. I'd keep exactly one of them and let the other two be silence.

## 4. Six-episode breakdown

Roughly 7,500 words of source per episode.

### Episode 1: "Marked" (Ch 1-4)
- Opening: the crash, the stranger reading her diary, the cookbook, the blob through the door, the dumpling stand, the dry-cleaning ticket lost to the gutter, the Space Between (Boots, the cost, "Rai Stone", the burned tree), the cart to Camp, the sick woman on the cot, Elizabeth handing over "The whole book."
- **Ending hook, as written:** Ch 4 ends "I'm coming." That's a resolved, soft ending; it doesn't pull. **My ending:** cut at the archive. She watches Nana's cookbook go onto the reserve shelf, "Not hidden. Not displayed. Available." The emotion is a hook, but a quiet one. Better: end on Mama Baga and Pathwell at the tree line saying "Stansbury", with Elizabeth overhearing that something was at her door *before* the working.
- Tone to protect: wry, warm, strange.

### Episode 2: "The Wrong Door" (Ch 5-6)
- Portal to the school, "Mrs. Pathwell", Stansbury and the foam swords, the drive, the bar, the mirror in the back hall.
- **Ending hook:** Ch 6 ends: "Then, from the far side of the Cadillac, a thud. / Wet. Heavy." This is the best-built hook in the book. It echoes the first chapter and it breaks a comfortable scene.
- Note: in the source Ch 6 ends with that thud and Ch 7 opens inside the fight. Keep the cut exactly there.

### Episode 3: "Shade" (Ch 7-9)
- The parking-lot fight, Elizabeth cutting Pathwell out, the car argument ("There she is. The person driving the car."), the ditch, Shade's headlights, the diner.
- **Ending hook:** Ch 9: she leaves the diner on foot with the receipt: "She had a question now, and this time she intended to make him answer it." That's a decision, so it works as a closing image; I'd prefer to end on the earlier beat, Shade stepping out of his headlights ("Wrong."), and put the diner at the *start* of Ep 4. The reveal of Shade is the series's first big shiver. Don't bury it under an hour of road.

### Episode 4: "What He Chose" (Ch 10-11)
- Library working (unsent letter, grocery lists), the road, the van confession, the museum (socks, "SAM HOME TODAY"), Pathwell's relapse with the quarantine letter, the destroyed cases, Elizabeth's shoulder, the graffiti door to Camp.
- **Ending hook:** "I'm still choosing it." / "Then she stepped through, and the wall closed behind her." Strong, though what follows (Stansbury saying "I did that" / "Yes") is the sharper last line. Cut to black on "Yes."
- This is the series midpoint and the emotional hinge: the confession tells us what he did; the museum shows he'd do it again.

### Episode 5: "Rejoining" (Ch 12-15)
- Healer sets her shoulder, the diary donated, Shade at the card table, Pathwell returns, the rejoining frame, the forced working, the fire, Milo under the shelf, Elizabeth walks past her diary and Nana's book.
- **Ending hook:** Ch 15 ends with the archive roof collapsing and "Shade did not either." But I'd put the break earlier, at the end of Ch 14: "The archive was burning. / And inside it, Milo shouted again." That's the best cliffhanger in the manuscript.
- Compress Ch 12-13 to one-third of their length (see cuts) so the fire arrives at roughly minute 30, not minute 45.

### Episode 6: "Owed" (Ch 15 second half-18)
- Aftermath, "He still owes me a hand", Mama Baga's "What do you owe?", the scar temptation, the restitution montage, the Space Between again, Pathwell's confession ("I could have called someone. I didn't."), Elizabeth's matching confession, the goose door, "Are you ready?" / "No." / "Perfect."
- **Ending hook:** none in the source, and that's honest: it closes on a deliberate echo of Ch 1 ("Ready?" / "No." / "Perfect."). It's a lovely last beat. But a six-episode series that *ends* on a closed circle has no reason to be renewed and no reason to be talked about. See section 8.

## 5. Trailer scenes

In rough order of how hard they'd sell:

1. **Ch 1:** The sugar-cookie page lifts gold off the paper under his palm, she pulls the book, "The sugar-cookie page tore free, sticking to his palm", and for one second she's twelve in Nana's kitchen. Cut to the door bowing in its frame.
2. **Ch 3:** The folded paper pressed to a concrete wall, light spidering outward until a doorway stands where the wall was: "Like I said, a bookstore."
3. **Ch 3:** The bargain. Silver pages glowing blue, roots of light up Pathwell's forearm, then the tree burning, "Pathwell screamed in a million voices, voices lost to time." Cut on the shopkeeper's "Will that be paper or plastic?" That cut from awe to dry comedy is the show's whole tone in two seconds.
4. **Ch 4:** The handcart arriving at the ring of wagons; the old woman lifting the crate "with no effort in it".
5. **Ch 4:** "The whole book." Her arms out, shaking.
6. **Ch 8:** The Cadillac off the road. "Elizabeth chose the ditch." Then the line under the crash, one beat: *It was the crash that woke her.*

## 6. What the book withholds that a screen version would have to show

This is where most of my substantive notes are, because prose can hide things a camera can't.

1. **Why Pathwell was in her apartment.** This is the largest gap. He was a guest at her party who stayed behind to read her diary and open her boxes, and the shopkeeper later says the cookbook and diary "were never his to spend." In other words, he came to steal them and sell them to pay for books for Camp. The book never has Elizabeth confront this. She forgives or tolerates the *museum* and the *fire* far more explicitly than she ever tackles the original trespass and the lie ("Your house has now been marked"). On screen, that first scene is a home invasion, and the audience will keep asking until someone pays for it.
2. **Whose door was it.** Three times, characters note the first blob was at her door *before* any magic went wrong ("Her door." / "Was it?"; Ch 5, Ch 7, Ch 9). The book answers by inference only: it was hunting him. I never got a scene that confirms it. And the apartment's weird neighbourhood in Ch 2 ("a shuttered laundromat she'd never seen before", a park "that shouldn't have been there") is never explained at all.
3. **The original job.** "Someone asked me for help." Who? What problem? What did the working do? We never see the client, the original prune, or what Shade is made from. For a mystery whose emotional answer ("what did he choose?") is built to land, the actual event stays offscreen. A screen version needs at least a flashback. This is the most valuable missing scene in the manuscript.
4. **Elizabeth's life.** Her job, her nine o'clock meeting, her coworkers, her mother ("My mom used to always tell me that"), what happened in the "few months" that "became years" at Nana's. She says "I have a meeting at nine" in Ch 2 and the book never says what it was or what she lost by missing it. In Ch 17 her inbox has forty-seven messages. Nothing was ever at risk. A screen version needs *something* in the ordinary world that pulls, so that the choice to stay in the magic world costs her.
5. **Nana as a person.** She is vivid in two memories ("They're your dumplings." / "Then you made them wrong."), and the handwriting works. But on screen we need her in the pre-title scene, before the cookbook is a MacGuffin.
6. **The cleanup creatures.** What are they? Who made them? The book says they clean up "loose magical waste" and then lets them kill Shade, who by that logic *was* waste. That's tragic and clever, but it's said in a line, not shown. On screen the creature needs rules we can see.
7. **Rules of the magic.** The best conceit in the book is that the power is spent *with the original*, and copies carry the words but not the thing (Ch 10, Ch 16). The prose makes this clear through smell and taste, which a camera can't do. A series needs a visual grammar: what does ink look like as it goes? How does a spent letter look different from a read one?
8. **Pathwell's age and history.** "Seventeen years" since the bar, "decades" at the Space Between, banned by a curator who is now long dead. Is he two hundred? The book plays that as a joke and never as a fact. A face on screen can't leave it ambiguous.
9. **Shade's body.** The "draw" (his right hand opening toward Pathwell) is the best physical device in the book, and entirely filmable. His death, though, happens out of sight under a blob while the camera is on Elizabeth and a child. Screen version: either show it, or earn the offscreen.
10. **The gold chain.** Mama Baga clasps it around Elizabeth's neck in Ch 4, and Elizabeth's hand goes to it twice in Ch 17. It's set up like a plant and never pays off. I don't know whether it's meant to.

## 7. Top ten, ranked

1. **Make the series about Elizabeth wanting something.** The mirror (he can't let go of control, she lets go of objects) is the book's real spine, but her *want* is reactive for most of the run. She needs an external goal, ideally one that costs her the ordinary life she keeps checking on. Right now she is the best witness in the story, not its engine. (High confidence.)
2. **Confront the theft.** Pathwell came to her apartment to take her grandmother's book and her diary, and then lied about the "marking". Nobody makes him account for that. The forgiveness arc is built on the museum and the fire; the first sin is still unpaid. Put it in the second act, as a scene between the two of them. (High confidence.)
3. **Show the original prune.** The question "What did he choose?" sets up a payoff the book doesn't deliver. The answer is "I pruned ahead... Someone asked me for help" and then nothing. The reader gets a feeling, not a fact. A single flashback scene would let Shade's existence cost something. (High.)
4. **The ending is closed to a fault.** The echo of Ch 1 ("Ready?" / "No." / "Perfect.") is a beautiful rhyme, but every real question is either answered by inference or left politely alone. Nothing is pulling a viewer toward more story. I don't know whether the author wants a sequel; but as a limited series I'd want one dangling thread that bites. (Medium.)
5. **The pacing sags in the middle-late book.** Ch 12-13 gives us a healer scene, a diary donation, a coffee scene, a jacket scene, a card scene and an onion scene with no new question. The climax then comes at about 73% of the way in, and the last quarter is aftermath (Ch 16-18). Ch 17 and most of Ch 18 are a long exhale. Move the fire earlier or shorten the lull. (High.)
6. **Everyone talks in the same register.** Pathwell, Shade, Stansbury, the archivist, Mama Baga, the healer, the waitress, a bartender and an eleven-year-old all deliver dry, deadpan, quick-return dialogue. The repeated moves are very noticeable: "I know." appears about 28 times as a full-line reply, "Good." 11, "Fair." and "Cruel." 3 each, and "All right" about 12. The cast needs different rhythms. On a page, this is a stylistic signature; on a call sheet, it is a casting problem. (High.)
7. **The antagonist is a wet shape.** Blobs are fine as texture but cannot carry a series: they never speak, want, plan, or appear in a scene that is *about* them. The real opposition is Pathwell's character, which is better, but a six-episode show needs one human or humanlike face on the other side of the table. (Medium-high.)
8. **The book states its choices out loud.** Twice the narration announces a decision as a decision ("That was the choice. / Everything afterward was consequence." in Ch 4, and "That was the choice. / Everything else burned afterward." in Ch 15). The first one is stronger because we haven't been told to care. The second works because it echoes the first. Use the line once on screen and let the cut carry the other. (Medium.)
9. **The emotional high points are earned, so protect them.** The cookbook scene in the wagon, the archive's "Not hidden. Not displayed. Available.", Milo's "He still owes me a hand", the archivist adding "LIKED POCKETS." These are the best things here, and each works because it's quiet. Don't let a production add music. (High confidence in the praise.)
10. **Specificity is the voice, so keep it in the adaptation.** The grocery lists ("SHOE POLISH"), the museum card corrected three times ("19TH VIRGINIA?"), the coffee that tastes like studying, the creek notebook ("HESS SAYS FOUR"). That documentary detail is what makes the fantasy feel lived-in and what a studio would be tempted to cut for "pace". It's the show's calling card. (High.)

## 8. The best version

### 8a. What to protect

- **The opening move.** "It was the crash that woke her." and its structural echo in Ch 8, when she is the one who crashes. Keep both.
- **The cost grammar of the magic.** "If I use this, it's gone." / "The copy keeps the words." / "Not the thing." (Ch 10). The copy-versus-original rule is the book's whole philosophy. It explains the archive, the museum, the cookbook and Shade.
- **The wagon scene.** Elizabeth, holding the cookbook, saying "The whole book." and refusing to let herself find "a reason this page is the wrong one" (Ch 4).
- **The archive as institution.** "Specificity is expensive." / "That is generally the opposite of an archive." Also the crooked label and the line "Copy the smudge."
- **Mama Baga's tribunal.** "Then stop naming impossible payment as if saying a large enough number is accountability."
- **Milo.** The sock, the boot, "He still owes me a hand."
- **The ending mirror.** "Ready?" / "No." / "Perfect." Keep it as the closing line of the series, and not as the last thing the series has to say.
- **Elizabeth's refusals.** "Was that a question?" (Ch 8), "I don't need to be rescued." (Ch 11), "Don't let Pathwell decide the restriction expired." (Ch 12).
- **Documentary props.** The grocery lists, the accession cards, the soldier's sock letter, "SAM HOME TODAY".

### 8b. The changes, in order of impact

1. **Give Elizabeth a want that costs her something.** *Where:* Ch 1-4 to set it, Ch 7-11 to drive it. *What:* Make the series about her trying to recover Nana, specifically the voice, from the thing Pathwell took, and have her pursuing that even as it turns out the page is already spent. Give her one pull from the ordinary world (the job, the meeting, the landlord who keeps calling) that gets harder to ignore every episode. *Effect:* she stops being the best witness and becomes the protagonist; the Milo choice is then a real renunciation of what she's been chasing, not just a good deed. *Size:* **large** (new through-line, reshaping Ch 1-4 and Ch 12-15).
2. **Stage the confrontation about the theft.** *Where:* new scene in Ch 9-11, most likely replacing the van confession or sitting right before it. *What:* Elizabeth puts the first night on the table: he was in her apartment because her grandmother's cookbook was valuable, and he sold what wasn't his ("These were never his to spend"). Pathwell cannot joke his way out. *Effect:* the forgiveness conversation in Ch 16 then has a floor to stand on. *Size:* **medium**.
3. **Show the original prune.** *Where:* a flashback sequence in Ch 10, before or after the van scene; a short, silent, mostly visual sequence. *What:* the client's face, the working being built, the payment released, the thing that was left behind that becomes Shade. Keep it incomplete: leave out why the client needed it. *Effect:* "what he chose" becomes something we've seen, and Shade's grievance ("For him to stop deciding what I am before I get to") hurts more. *Size:* **medium**.
4. **Compress the lull, move the fire earlier.** *Where:* Ch 12-13 to about a third of their length; Ch 14-15 stay. *What:* one healer scene, the diary donation, one card-table scene. Lose the coffee-making, the jacket and the onions. *Effect:* the climax lands at roughly 60% rather than 73%, which gives the aftermath room to be tense rather than elegiac. *Size:* **medium** (cutting is easy; keeping Shade's humanisation intact is the skill).
5. **Differentiate the voices.** *Where:* throughout, especially Ch 5-9, Ch 14, Ch 16. *What:* rewrite for distinct rhythms. Let Stansbury be exact and dry; let Shade be spare and literal; let the archivist be procedural; let Mama Baga be plain and slow; let Pathwell, and only Pathwell, run on. Remove most "I know." / "Good." / "Fair." / "Cruel." one-line replies. *Effect:* scenes stop sounding like one clever person talking to themselves, and the surprise of the characters' disagreements goes up. *Size:* **medium** (line-level pass, but it touches almost every page).
6. **Give the cleanup a face.** *Where:* a short scene in Ch 7 or Ch 9 and a reappearance in Ch 15. *What:* either a human cleanup practitioner who "works for" the thing or a clear sequence showing the creatures following a rule. The cleanup must be legible (what does it eat, what does it ignore). *Effect:* the antagonist stops being weather, and Shade's death reads as a decision by the world rather than an accident. *Size:* **medium-large**.
7. **Resolve or deliberately re-seed the dropped threads.** *Where:* the weird neighbourhood (Ch 2), the "door" question (Ch 5-9), the gold chain (Ch 4, 17). *What:* answer one, pay off one, cut one. For the last, I'd cut. *Effect:* a reader finishing the book stops wondering which of these was a plant. *Size:* **small**.
8. **Give the ending one live wire.** *Where:* Ch 18. *What:* put one unresolved thing in the last scene that isn't a shared joke: the "client" resurfacing, a creature at the geese door, a page of Nana's still out there. The Ch 1 echo remains the final beat. *Effect:* a viewer gets the satisfaction of the circle and the itch of a question. *Size:* **small**.
9. **Cut the narrator's announcements of choices.** *Where:* Ch 4 and Ch 15, the two "That was the choice" lines; and the thematic summaries in dialogue (Ch 10, 14, 16). *What:* keep one, cut the others. *Effect:* the audience does the work, so the moments hit harder. *Size:* **small**.
10. **Trim Ch 5, Ch 6 and Ch 17.** *What:* described in section 3. *Effect:* pace. *Size:* **small to medium**.

### 8c. The shape of the best version

A reader opens on a woman woken by a crash in a half-unpacked apartment and a stranger reading her diary. Within two chapters she has had her grandmother's voice returned to her for one second, lost the thing that carried it, and been told she can choose where to go next, which she has never once felt in the previous three weeks. The first third of the book plays as a road story with a thief: the Space Between, the camp, the school, the bar, the ditch. The jokes are still there but they now have somewhere to land: she knows exactly what she wants back, and she can see him not saying what he did.

The middle turns when the van confession gives us what Pathwell did and the museum shows he'd do it again. Her anger is specific: the apartment, the diary, the lie. The camp sequence is shorter and sharper; Shade becomes a person in one scene at a card table, not four. The rejoining frame hits roughly when a reader's patience for banter would run out. Elizabeth has to choose between the thing she came for and a child under a shelf, and she makes the choice we hoped she would, but by then we know exactly what it costs. Shade is lost in a way that feels like the world's decision, not an accident. The last quarter is the accounting: Mama Baga's tribunal, the lemon wagon, a man who learns to ask first. It is quieter and more earned than the original because the book has not been apologizing for a hundred pages. The last scene is the door and "Perfect.", and there is one small, unexplained thing on the other side that makes you want to open it.

Shade's capture is off-camera and abrupt; I didn't believe it was real, and then the book asked me to.
- **Ch 16.** "He still owes me a hand." devastated me. "SHADE — DIED AT CAMP. / LIKED POCKETS." is the best joke-that-isn't-a-joke in the book. Mama Baga's tribunal is superb. Perhaps too many scenes of aftermath stacked up.
- **Ch 17.** Warm, funny, and structurally a montage. The Henry Vale letters near-miss is the best beat. I was never worried, which is a problem.
- **Ch 18.** Returns to the beginning. "I could have called someone. I didn't." is the confession I'd been waiting for since Ch 10. The goose door is silly in a good way. "Ready?" / "No." / "Perfect." Satisfying, closed, and no more questions.
