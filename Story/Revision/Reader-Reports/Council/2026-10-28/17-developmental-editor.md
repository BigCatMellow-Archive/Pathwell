### Lines 1-400 (Ch 1 - early Ch 3)

- Ch 1 opens fast: "It was the crash that woke her." Fine, functional. The party detail is the best thing on page one: "half a sheet cake that now only read ELIZ, because her coworkers had started at the wrong end." That's a writer who sees things.

- "reading her diary like a magazine in a waiting room" - good, characterizing image.
- The blob attack is clear enough but the staging gets muddy: "The man threw himself across the room, and the door slammed hard enough to rattle the chain. The bolt shot home." Who shot the bolt? Is it magic? I reread it twice.
- The cookbook magic is the hook and it works: a memory as fuel. "Gold ran along the ink in little starts and stops... even through the crooked places where the pen had hesitated." Lovely - the hesitation in Nana's handwriting made visible. Protect.
- The tear of the page - she causes the damage by grabbing. Good: agency and consequence in the first chapter. The memory flash at twelve is generic though ("felt something she hadn't felt in years") - the most important emotional beat of the chapter is written in the vaguest language.
- "The cake, somehow, was fine." Got a smile. Tone: urban fantasy with a comic-quippy mentor. Pathwell reads as a Doctor Who type (Doctor/companion dynamic is very visible: "Ready?" "No." "Perfect.").
- Pathwell's banter in Ch 1 runs long: "Understandable reaction. Reasonable, even. Unfortunately. We really need to be going." There are about four beats of "we need to go" / "I'm not going". I felt the scene stall there.
- Ch 2: The grief arrives - "A life she'd packed into garbage bags and moved across the city because staying felt worse than leaving, but leaving didn't feel like anything at all." That's the emotional thesis, I think. Good sentence.
- Dry-cleaning ticket blowing away - nice small, concrete breaking point. Earned cry. This is the best scene so far.
- The neighborhood changing (no coffee shop, park that shouldn't be there) - intriguing, unexplained. Noting as setup.
- Shadow in her apartment window - setup.
- Pathwell's dumpling bit: "Oh, these aren't for you... and I don't share." Charming the first time. The "perfectly crappy" speech is the character's philosophy in miniature (things not trying to be what they're not). Possibly a setup for theme.
- He calls her Lizzy - and Nana called her Lizzy. He knows things from the diary. Ch 3 confirms: "Isn't that what your grandmother called you?" Good creepy edge, but the book plays it as cute. I want her to be more unsettled by that.
- End Ch 2: "He still has it." Motivation hook - she follows for the cookbook, not curiosity. Good, gives her a reason that isn't passive.
- Ch 3: Folding-paper door is a nice visual. The bookstore of "lives" - journals, letters, recipe cards. So the magic system is memory/personal writing. Clear concept. Feels adjacent to The Invisible Library / Starless Sea / Midnight Library, which is a crowded shelf. Need to see what makes this different.
- "a silence that felt like it had been here practicing for years" - nice.
- Emotional pressure in the stacks ("pressing against the inside of her ribs") - setup that the books leak feeling.
- Overall after 400 lines: voice is warm, scene-to-scene clarity is mostly good, prose has some clunky constructions ("She knew him, a little, he'd been at the party"; "sat on the floor"). Protagonist is mostly reactive so far but has a concrete want (get the cookbook back). Pathwell carries the energy.

## Part 2: Editorial letter

(Written after finishing the manuscript and re-reading my log once. Process note: apart from this brief, the only file I opened was the manuscript. I read it in order, in chunks of about 400 lines, start to finish. The only other thing I ran was a line and word count on the manuscript file before I started.)

Dear author,

I've finished the book, and I read it the way I'd read for a paying client. Here's the short version. This manuscript has real sentence-level talent and a genuinely good idea about what magic costs. Its two best set pieces (the museum in Ch 11, and the archive fire through to LIKED POCKETS in Ch 14-16) are as good as similar scenes in books I've seen sell. Its problems are structural, not cosmetic. They sit in three places: how late and how vaguely the external plot arrives, how the book handles Pathwell's guilt at the end, and where Elizabeth's biggest emotional beats fall. None of these is fatal. All three need a real revision, not a polish.

### What the book is

It's a gentle, comic urban-fantasy novel, about 45,800 words long. A grieving young woman is pulled into a world where handwritten personal records (recipes, letters, grocery lists, diaries) hold charge that can be spent as magic, and spending a record destroys it. On the surface it's a companion-and-mentor romp: Elizabeth and Pathwell, with a strong Doctor-and-companion flavor (Ch 1 "Ready?" / "No." / "Perfect."). Underneath, it's a book about consent and about the dignity of ordinary writing. It asks who gets to decide what someone else's record, body or future is for. The real antagonist is Pathwell's certainty that he knows the cleanest way through, and the book knows that, which is to its credit.

On the shelf it sits near The Ten Thousand Doors of January and The Invisible Library for premise, near the found-family warmth of The House in the Cerulean Sea for tone, and near Pratchett-lite for its dialogue rhythm. What makes it different from that crowded shelf is the specific texture of its records ("RUTH LIED ABOUT THE LARD," "HESS SAYS FOUR," "SAM HOME TODAY. THIN AS A FENCE RAIL. ATE THREE EGGS."). It is also different in its refusal of magical consolation. When the cookbook burns, "Ordinary flame moved through old paper and flour-stained cardboard, because that was what fire did" (Ch 15). That's your book's signature. Lean on it.

### What it does best (briefly)

- **Small records as emotional delivery systems.** Nana's "MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH" (Ch 4), the Ellison unsent letters (Ch 10), the Vale socks and the household notebook (Ch 11), the Merritt cookbook (Ch 18). Every time the book puts a real person's handwriting on the page, it works.
- **The magic system's texture.** The grocery-list tracking working in Ch 10 ("The list carried almost no feeling at all, and that was what made it useful") is the best magic-system writing in the manuscript. Different kinds of writing do different things, and the rules come out of character and theme rather than lore dumps.
- **Shade.** He is the best character in the book. The card game in Ch 13 ("stopped playing from remembered rules and started playing the people at the table") is a complete arc in a page. "That's your plan. Let me find mine." "Person is the word I have for that." (Ch 16).
- **Setups and payoffs at the scene level.** The museum cases loaded and then destroyed in order, the terrible coffee as the shared memory that lets Pathwell find her, the sock, the runemasters. You plant well.
- **Comic dialogue.** "I have not reached the axle emotionally." "It's a series." "They have learned commerce."

### The three biggest structural problems

**1. The external plot arrives late, stays abstract, and ends by accident.**

The book's first real question, why the thing was at her door before any working, only surfaces at the end of Ch 4, about 15% in. The real engine is the prune that went wrong and its by-product, Shade. That doesn't appear until Ch 8, about 31% in. Before that, Ch 1-6 run as a string of locations (apartment, bookstore, Camp, school, bar). The connective tissue is "go and see Stansbury," and a reader moves through them on charm.

Then, once the central question exists, it gets deferred by every character who could answer it. Pathwell says "Not here," "Later," "Not while you're driving." Stansbury stays quiet. Shade says "Ask him what he chose" (Ch 9). By the time the van confession comes in Ch 10, I'd spent a third of the book being managed. The confession gives the moral shape of what Pathwell did ("They did not ask me to prune. I did not ask whether they wanted me to.") but not its content. Who asked for help? What was the problem? What result did he build the working around? "Someone asked me for help" and "They had a problem. A real one." are the answers, and the book never comes back to them. The central act behind the whole plot is a diagram.

The external threat also resolves without anyone choosing anything. The cleanup creatures take Shade, the draw ends, and the blobs stop. That fits the theme (choices don't guarantee outcomes). But plot-wise the threat just switches off, and the Ch 2 setups (the coffee shop that isn't there, the park "that shouldn't have been there," the shadow in her window) never resolve.

Confidence: high.

**2. Pathwell's reckoning is built for one ending, and the manuscript has a different one.**

Pathwell relapses three times after stating the lesson: the backstory prune, the museum (Ch 11, right after the confession), and the rejoining frame (Ch 14, over four separate "no"s). That's defensible. He's addicted to fixing, and the museum relapse in particular is excellent. Then Ch 16-17 build a careful, mature accountability structure. Camp defines what he owes ("Defined by whom?" "Not me."), Elizabeth refuses to make his guilt useful, the curator refuses the Henry Vale substitution, and he copies the smudge.

Then, about 150 lines from the end, he tells Elizabeth that he watched Shade being taken while Shade called for help, and stayed silent because "if the creature took him, it would be finished." That is the darkest fact in the book. It changes what every earlier reckoning was about. Yet it goes only to Elizabeth, in a shop, and is followed within a page by trading geese and the reversed "Ready?" / "No." / "Perfect." bookend. I didn't believe the distance between that confession and "Do you want to come?" could be crossed in an hour of offstage breathing in the stacks.

The book's own ethic says accountability is defined by the people harmed. That ethic says Stansbury, Mama Baga, the archivist and Milo have to know. As written, the ending honors its theme everywhere except at the point where it matters most.

Related craft issue: Pathwell's point-of-view sections (the mirror in Ch 6, the library and the van in Ch 10) arrive unannounced in a book otherwise in close third on Elizabeth. In Ch 11 the book cheats with that access. We were inside Pathwell's head in Stansbury's library and never saw him pocket the quarantine letter ("That's from my library. You took that while I was upstairs."). Either commit to dual point of view from early on and play fair with it, or keep Pathwell external.

Confidence: high on the confession's placement and handling, medium-high on the POV question.

**3. Elizabeth's biggest beats are front-loaded, repeated, and, at the climax, weighted on the easy choice.**

She gives the whole cookbook to the archive in Ch 4. That's beautifully written ("If I keep holding the rest, I'm going to spend the next ten minutes finding a reason this page is the wrong one"). But it's a letting-go-of-grief beat at 15%, to strangers she's known for an hour. It resolves her only concrete want (get the cookbook back), and it's psychologically fast. In Ch 12 she does it again with the diary: same archivist, same "Name?" / "Family name?", same two-handed receiving. The second time is better, and "DONOR PERMISSION REQUIRED. PATHWELL IS NOT DONOR" is a great payoff. But the shape doesn't escalate.

Her actual act-one choice, to go with Pathwell to Camp after the shopkeeper offers her the sidewalk, happens offstage between Ch 3 and Ch 4 ("She had put them there herself, after the shopkeeper had offered to send her back to the sidewalk"). It's the most important decision in the opening, and we don't see her make it.

At the climax (Ch 15) the real dilemma is Shade versus Milo. She has the one tool that has ever cut someone out of a blob, and only one working arm. The book spends a few lines on that choice and much more on walking past the diary and the cookbook. Walking past a book to save a child is not a dilemma for any reader. Leaving Shade is. Shade looks at her, and the manuscript leaves open whether that look meant "go." That ambiguity is wasted. It is the moment where consent, the book's whole subject, could decide the climax.

Confidence: high on the climax weighting and the offstage choice, medium on the early cookbook gift. Some readers will love how early it comes; I think it needs either more resistance or a later echo that costs more.

### Pacing by act

Rough positions by line count, for orientation:

- **Act One (Ch 1-5, 0-20%).** Strong opening images and a strong grief core (the dry-cleaning ticket, the Nana flashback in Ch 3). It's slowed by effects paragraphs that repeat themselves (the tree ritual in Ch 3, the portal's double wind-up in Ch 5), slapstick filler (lost in the woods, falling twice, in Ch 4), and set-piece comedy (the school closet in Ch 5). It's also episodic. I was charmed but not pulled.
- **Act Two A (Ch 6-10, 20-46%).** The book finds momentum: the bar and the blob, the ditch, Shade on the road, the diner, the grocery-list tracking. This is where the withholding problem bites. The van confession is the midpoint, and it's emotionally earned but thin on information.
- **Midpoint peak (Ch 11, 46-53%).** The museum. The best set piece in the manuscript. This is the chapter I'd show an agent.
- **Act Two B (Ch 12-13, 53-66%).** Decompression at Camp: sling, stew, coat, coffee, Hearts. I flagged this as idling while reading. In hindsight it's load-bearing, because it's what makes Shade's death hurt. It's still about a third longer than it needs to be, and nothing new is in question here except the draw getting stronger.
- **Climax (Ch 14-15, 66-79%).** Strong. "Can." / "Not will." The fire. Milo. The structure of a mentor's flaw becoming the act-three threat is bold and right.
- **Falling action (Ch 16-18, 79-100%).** More than a fifth of the book. Each beat is good: the funeral line, the hearing, transcription, the curator, The Lemon, the Merritt cookbook. Together they're too long, and the one beat that needed the most room (the Shade confession) gets the least. Published comparables would run the denouement at roughly half this length, or give it a second spike of tension.

### Setups and payoffs

**Paid, and paid well:** the terrible coffee (Ch 3 -> 9 -> 10 -> 13 -> 18); the separation dagger (Ch 5 -> 7, and the restraint in Ch 14); Milo's sock (Ch 4 -> 15 "His sock had finally stayed up. Elizabeth hated that detail immediately."); the runemasters speech (Ch 4 -> 17); the Hess notebook (Ch 4 -> 15 -> 17); Prague (Ch 9 -> 14); "REGIMENT UNKNOWN" -> "UNKNOWN" on the loss sheet; the healer's "You are not using this arm today" (Ch 12 -> 15); the diary-reading violation (Ch 1 -> 12 permission note); chicken and dumplings and the young woman's bowl (Ch 4 -> 16); Nana's spoon (Ch 2 -> 12 -> 17); Shade's "That hand was promising" (Ch 13 -> 15); "It was the crash that woke her" (Ch 1 -> 8).

**Set up and not paid, or paid by implication only:**
- Who asked Pathwell for help, and what the original problem was (Ch 10). This is the biggest one.
- Why the street changed in Ch 2 (no coffee shop, a shuttered laundromat, "a park that shouldn't have been there") and the shadow in her window. (A laundromat turns up as the entrance in Ch 18. If that's the payoff, make it legible.)
- Whether her apartment is still "marked." The door is answered in Ch 18; the marking never is.
- Mama Baga's gold chain (Ch 4). A witch's gift sets up an expectation. It's touched in Ch 9 and Ch 17 and never matters.
- Elizabeth's mother ("My mom used to always tell me that," Ch 6). Why was Elizabeth Nana's sole caregiver for years? The gap reads as an omission rather than a choice.
- Stansbury harvesting children's work (Ch 5). Elizabeth draws the moral parallel to Pathwell's pruning, and the book never comes back to it. It's a natural thread for the brothers' ethics.
- The romance hint ("Mrs. Elizabeth," "He keeps acting like you're something he can hold onto"). I couldn't tell whether there's an undercurrent. Decide.
- "Big cat" / "grande gatto" (Ch 10, 16). An unexplained sibling nickname that reads as an in-joke.
- Rai Stone and Lydian coins (Ch 3). Real-world money used as in-world currency, or a joke? I couldn't tell.

### What to cut

In rough order of value:

1. **Ch 16-17 denouement:** about a third. Keep the funeral line, the apology exchange, the hearing (trim the list of terms by half), transcription and the smudge, the curator and Henry Vale, the spoon, The Lemon, and Pathwell losing at Hearts. Fold the archival-board catalog, Milo's boots and the plants into a sentence or cut them.
2. **Ch 13:** about a third of the Camp business (coat selection, repeated hand-opening beats, the second and third Hearts hands). Keep "playing the people," "Pockets," "Let me find mine," "I want to stay here."
3. **Ch 12-16, narrator glosses on consent:** "checking the knot rather than steering her body," "after asking permission in exactly the same tone." The actions already say it. Cut the commentary.
4. **Ch 1:** the "we need to go" / "I'm not going" loop (about four exchanges), and the paragraph of party backstory, since Ch 3 retells it better from inside her.
5. **Ch 3:** the tree ritual's stacked adjectives ("sick and breathless," "sitting sickly and worn," "a harmony of extinguished cries"). Same image, louder each time, not clearer.
6. **Ch 4:** lost-in-the-woods slapstick ("The taste of dirt kissed her lips"). Get her to Mama Baga in a paragraph. Also the first bark-tea joke, which dilutes the coffee motif.
7. **Ch 5:** the portal's second wind-up (keep the singed bandana), and trim the school-closet business. Cut the second-person town paragraph in the drive ("You would be forgiven for not stopping...") unless that narrator appears elsewhere.
8. **Ch 10:** the slapping gag ("Bank it."). It's off-tone with an injured man just pulled out of a monster.
9. **Repeated signature beats:** the "Lizzy" / "Elizabeth" correction after its second use; the second "That was the choice" (or the first, but not both, unless the echo is going to be explicitly remarked); the third quotation of the FIND NANA'S SPOON list.

### What to build

1. **The original prune.** One concrete person, place and problem. It doesn't need to be long. It needs to be specific enough that the van confession in Ch 10 delivers information as well as feeling. Ideally it also connects back to the cleanup creature at her door in Ch 1.
2. **Elizabeth's on-page choice at the end of Ch 3.** Let the shopkeeper offer the sidewalk, and let us watch her choose the forest.
3. **The Shade-or-Milo decision in Ch 15.** Give it room. Settle, on the page, what Shade's look meant.
4. **The aftermath of Pathwell's Shade confession.** Either move it earlier, into Ch 16, so the hearing and the apology account for it, or give the ending the space to carry it: other people told, a consequence defined by someone other than Pathwell, and time before Elizabeth's invitation.
5. **Voice differentiation.** Nearly everyone (Mama Baga, the archivist, the shopkeeper, Stansbury, Shade, the healer, Elizabeth) speaks in the same dry deadpan-correction register: "That is not a posture." / "That is generally the opposite of an archive." / "It's also a worse description." / "That wasn't the question." / "A low standard." It's a good register. It shouldn't be everyone's. Shade and Stansbury in particular are close to interchangeable on the page, which is costly in a book whose premise is that Shade is a different recipe from the same ingredients.
6. **A name for the young woman Nana's dumplings saved.** I accept "roles not names" as a texture choice for Camp. She's the exception: she is what the cookbook bought.
7. **Elizabeth's life outside grief.** Her job, the missed nine o'clock, her mother. One or two beats each, so the return to ordinary life in Ch 17 means something.
8. **Length.** At about 46,000 words this falls between a novella and an adult novel. The builds above would add perhaps 10-15k, and the cuts would remove perhaps 5-8k. Landing in the 55-70k range would let you pitch it as a short adult novel in the cozy or upmarket fantasy space. Confidence: medium. Category conventions vary, and a novella line is also a legitimate target if you'd rather stay lean.

### Distance to publishable

The sentence-level writing is close to professional at its best and uneven at its worst: Ch 1 and Ch 3-5 are clunkier than Ch 9 onward. The structure needs one substantial revision. Ch 1-5 need tightening, the prune needs concrete content, Ch 15 needs re-weighting, and the ending needs rebuilding around the confession. After that it needs a line edit for the early chapters and for voice differentiation.

My honest estimate: one major revision and one line pass from being ready to query. That's two serious drafts, not five. This is not a book with a broken premise. It is a book whose best material arrives from Ch 8 onward, and whose ending hasn't caught up with what it found out about its own hero. Confidence: medium-high.

## Part 3: Ranked top ten

1. **The late Shade confession (Ch 18) changes the whole moral account, and the book doesn't reckon with it.** It goes only to Elizabeth, after every hearing has been held, and is followed by geese and an invitation. Move it earlier, or give the ending the room and the witnesses it needs.
2. **Make the original prune concrete (Ch 10).** Who asked, what problem, what result he built the working around. Right now the plot's central act is a moral diagram.
3. **The engine starts too late.** The real plot (Shade, the draw) appears at about 31%. Tighten Ch 1-5 and seed the prune and the double earlier, so the opening is pulled by a question and not just carried by charm.
4. **Re-weight the climax (Ch 15) onto the hard choice.** Shade versus Milo is the dilemma. Books versus a child is not. Decide what Shade's look meant.
5. **Put Elizabeth's act-one choice on the page (end of Ch 3).** The most important decision in the opening happens between chapters.
6. **Differentiate the voices.** Everyone deadpans the same way. Stansbury and Shade especially need distinct speech.
7. **Cut the falling action (Ch 16-17) by about a third** and spend what you save on item 1.
8. **Decide on point of view and play fair.** Pathwell's POV sections are unannounced, and Ch 11 relies on hiding something we would have seen from inside his head in Ch 10.
9. **The two donation scenes (Ch 4, Ch 12) have the same shape.** Either give the first more resistance or make the second differ in kind and cost.
10. **Trust the reader on the theme.** Consent and choice are stated in nearly every scene from Ch 12 on. Cut the narrator's glosses and some of the dialogue restatements. The actions already carry it.

## Part 4: The best version

### 1. What to protect

Do not lose these in revision:

- The Nana flashback in Ch 3, whole: "Nana's house had thirteen stairs, and by the end Elizabeth knew which ones complained." / "to carry it back down mostly full." / "as if the pages were running out before she was." / "The pepper was the best part, Lizzy." / "she noticed she was listening for someone upstairs. / There was no upstairs."
- "MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH." / "She believed soup was an argument you could win." (Ch 4)
- "If I keep holding the rest, I'm going to spend the next ten minutes finding a reason this page is the wrong one." (Ch 4)
- The Hess notebook: "Hess has been dead twenty-two years." / "He'd still argue." (Ch 4) and its payoff, "HESS SAYS FOUR." / "This is not information. This is a family argument preserved as infrastructure." / "Copy the smudge." (Ch 17)
- "Not cruel. Worse. Useful." (Ch 7) and "Elizabeth chose the ditch." (Ch 8)
- "Pathwell filled silence because silence looked undefended. Shade seemed willing to let it suffer." (Ch 8)
- The Ellison letters: "She wrote to the same man for six years and mailed none of them. He married somebody else in year two." / "It makes the cost mine to approve." (Ch 10)
- The grocery-list working: "The list carried almost no feeling at all, and that was what made it useful... Nothing more romantic than errands." (Ch 10)
- The museum chapter, nearly entire. Especially "SAM HOME TODAY. THIN AS A FENCE RAIL. ATE THREE EGGS." / "For her, perhaps that was the end of the war." / "History books make everyone sound like they spent all day becoming history. Mostly they were worrying about socks." (Ch 11)
- Shade at Hearts: "stopped playing from remembered rules and started playing the people at the table," and "That's your plan. Let me find mine." (Ch 13)
- "Can." / "Not will." and "Don't make him the reason." (Ch 14)
- "Her own ordinary handwriting disappeared without being used for anything." (Ch 15)
- "SHADE — DIED AT CAMP. / GREEN JACKET, CAMP ISSUE — LOST WITH HIM. / LIKED POCKETS." / "Archive burned. Categories are temporarily flexible." (Ch 16)
- "Person is the word I have for that." (Ch 16)
- "I am not going to make your guilt useful." (Ch 16)
- "Then stop naming impossible payment as if saying a large enough number is accountability." (Ch 16)
- The cookbook burning without magic: "Ordinary flame moved through old paper and flour-stained cardboard, because that was what fire did." (Ch 15)
- Pathwell putting the Merritt cookbook back "square to the edge" without a speech. (Ch 18)
- The comic spine: "I have not reached the axle emotionally." "It's a series." "Do not anthropomorphize the evidence." "They have learned commerce."

### 2. The changes, in order of impact

1. **Rebuild the ending around the Shade confession** (Ch 15, 16, 18). Large job.
   - Set it up so we and Elizabeth suspect it earlier. Ch 15 already has the image.
   - Have the confession happen where the people Shade's death affected can hear it (Camp, Stansbury, Milo), so the hearing in Ch 16 accounts for it.
   - Let the Ch 18 Space Between visit become what follows that reckoning, not where it happens.
   - What it does: the ending becomes an earned, uneasy grace rather than a tonal swerve. The reversed "Ready?" bookend then lands as the culmination of a reckoning, not a lid on it.
2. **Make the original prune concrete and connect it to Ch 1** (Ch 6, 8, 10, possibly Ch 1). Medium-large.
   - Give the person who asked for help an identity and a problem specific enough to picture.
   - Let one fragment of it reach the reader early: through Shade's memories in Ch 9, or the "Was it?" exchange in Ch 5.
   - Make the cleanup creature at Elizabeth's door in Ch 1 legibly part of that same mess.
   - What it does: the withholding in Act Two becomes suspense instead of management, and the midpoint pays out information as well as feeling.
3. **Tighten and re-pull Act One** (Ch 1-5). Medium.
   - Cut the loops listed above.
   - Put Elizabeth's choice to go with Pathwell on the page at the end of Ch 3.
   - Seed the double or the draw earlier: one beat of Pathwell's pull before the bar, or a glimpse from outside Elizabeth's point of view.
   - Make the Ch 2 street-change setups pay or remove them.
   - What it does: the first fifty pages are carried by a question as well as by charm. That's what gets a manuscript read past page fifty.
4. **Re-weight the climax onto Shade versus Milo** (Ch 15). Small-medium.
   - Spend the page-time on that choice and less on the book pass-bys.
   - Make Shade's look mean something definite. Most likely it is his consent, which would make the climax enact the theme instead of illustrating it.
   - What it does: Elizabeth's climactic act becomes the hardest choice in the book, not the easiest.
5. **Differentiate the voices** (whole manuscript, concentrated in Ch 4, 5, 6, 9, 12-16). Medium.
   - Give each speaker a distinct verbal habit, rhythm or blind spot. Shade and Stansbury first, then the archivist, healer and shopkeeper.
   - Keep the dry deadpan for Mama Baga and one other at most.
   - What it does: scenes stop sounding like one clever person talking to themselves, and Shade's "different recipe" becomes audible.
6. **Decide on point of view** (Ch 6, 10, 11 and wherever else it's needed). Medium.
   - Either establish Pathwell as a second POV early (one short section in Act One) and use it honestly, including in the library scene, or render those scenes from outside him.
   - Fix the Ch 10 / Ch 11 letter cheat either way.
   - What it does: readers trust the narration, and the late confession can be prepared from inside his head if you choose dual POV.
7. **Differentiate the two donations** (Ch 4, Ch 12). Small-medium.
   - Let the first one be harder: one more beat of resistance, or a cost she notices later.
   - Let the second one be visibly different in kind, not just better-conditioned.
   - What it does: Elizabeth's arc reads as a progression rather than a repeated beat.
8. **Cut the falling action by a third, and the Ch 13 domestic material by a third** (Ch 13, 16, 17). Medium.
   - Use the freed space for change 1.
   - What it does: the back fifth of the book stops plateauing.
9. **Fill Elizabeth's life** (Ch 2, 6, 17). Small.
   - The mother, the job, the missed meeting, one beat each.
   - A name for the young woman in Ch 4 and 16.
   - What it does: Elizabeth's return to ordinary life in Ch 17 has stakes, and the cookbook trade has a face.
10. **Line pass for the early chapters and the theme glosses** (Ch 1-5, Ch 12-16). Small-medium.
    - Fix comma splices, effect-paragraph inflation and narrator commentary on consent.
    - Use the "Lizzy" / "Elizabeth" correction and "That was the choice" each only as often as they gain meaning.
    - What it does: the early prose matches the quality of Ch 9 onward.

### 3. The shape of the best version

A reader opens it and gets the same party, the same ELIZ cake and the same man reading a diary on the floor. But by the end of Ch 3 they have watched Elizabeth refuse the sidewalk and choose the forest. Somewhere in those first chapters they've felt a faint wrongness about Pathwell (a pull, a reflection, a name he won't explain) that makes the comic mentor slightly uncanny from the start. The road trip has a spine. Everyone is circling a specific thing Pathwell did for a specific person. When he finally says it in the van, the reader learns something as well as feels something. Shade arrives as the living consequence of that act, and he sounds like no one else in the book.

The museum is still the museum. The Camp chapters are leaner and still long enough to make us love Shade. At the climax Elizabeth faces the one choice the book has been building toward. Shade's consent is part of it, and she carries Milo out knowing exactly what she left.

Then the book does the hard thing in public. Pathwell's silence while Shade called for help comes out where Camp can hear it, and the reckoning that follows is defined by the people he harmed. Only after that, shorter and quieter than now, does Elizabeth walk him to the Space Between. He leaves the Merritt cookbook on the shelf, and she asks if he wants to come. "Ready?" "No." "Perfect." now reads as something both of them have paid for. The reader closes it thinking about their own family's handwriting. That's the book this already almost is.
