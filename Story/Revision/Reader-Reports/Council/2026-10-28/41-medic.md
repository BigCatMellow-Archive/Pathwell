## What's medically off, and whether a general reader would notice

Confidence is in brackets. "General reader" means would a non-clinical reader notice.

1. **The wagon crush recovers overnight.** Ch4 (l.853–871) describes "shallow and slow" breathing, a "washed-out" colour, and fingers "curled and still." The page takes that to "sore and hungry" (l.981), and she's walking in Ch16 (l.6397). A clinician would not expect that from a crush with compromised breathing. [High] General reader: maybe. The line is specific enough to read like a joke.
2. **Pathwell functions after a chest and head insult with almost no assessment.** Grey skin, a wet cough, a crushed rib band, a weak voice (Ch7, l.1974–1998). The only follow-up is pupils and pulse (l.2032), then quips. Then he rides in the back of the car, walks, and stands in the museum. [High] General reader: no. He's the magic-hard man, and the reader expects him to take it.
3. **No neuro check after the temple laceration.** Pathwell has a cut and blood down his face (Ch11, l.3982), is "on one knee," and later "swayed" and was "listing" (Ch14, l.5177–5183). Stansbury cleans and tapes it (l.4111–4113), and that's the whole plan. [High] General reader: no.
4. **Pathwell's chest impact and "unable to stand."** A pin strikes his chest "hard enough to throw him backward" (Ch14, l.5673). He is "conscious and unable to stand" (Ch15, l.5727), and nobody goes to him for a long while. [High] General reader: maybe, since he's walking within hours.
5. **Elizabeth's head.** Four possible head events, none followed up: face-first in roots (Ch4, l.676), faint onto knees (Ch3, l.407), head on door frame (Ch5, l.1585), and driven backward with Pathwell on top (Ch7, l.1984). No headache, nausea, light sensitivity, or confusion anywhere. [Medium. She may be fine, but the book has to say so.] General reader: no.
6. **"concussion" appears once, and it's ambiguous.** "I am deciding whether the concussion was permanent" (Ch17, l.7040). No character is diagnosed with one earlier, and I can't tell whose it is. [High that it's unclear.] General reader: maybe.
7. **The shoulder reduction is good, with one gap.** The healer's exam (l.4213–4233), the joint's "partly out" (l.4255), the reduction, the neurovascular checks (l.4295–4299), and the return precautions (l.4368) are all right. The gap is no imaging and no deltoid or axillary-nerve check after a case hit the shoulder hard. "Nothing I can feel says broken" is a plausible camp-healer line. [Medium] General reader: no. The later strain, "That was a problem for later" (l.5941), pays off properly in Ch16 (l.6223–6233).
8. **Stansbury's burn.** "One strip near the thumb already pale in a way Elizabeth didn't like" (Ch16, l.6278). A pale strip on a hand burn can mean deep injury, and the book then has him "permanent" with stretching (l.6731–6747). The contracture plan is correct; the depth question is never settled. [Medium] General reader: no.
9. **Milo: smoke and a pinned leg, with only the ankle examined.** He's "coughing" and "thin with coughing" (l.5768, l.5975). Elizabeth also went through the smoke at the door and was never checked. The ankle is "Not broken" with no imaging (l.6406). [Medium-high] General reader: no.
10. **Stansbury's ribs disappear.** Bruised in the crash, walking home with a hand on them (l.3009–3011), then he "hit her from the side" and tackled her (l.3986). Nothing after. [Medium] General reader: no.
11. **Pathwell's neck.** Unbelted, something struck the roof (l.2285), hand at the back of his neck (l.2301). Peas (l.3106). No check for the cervical spine, and nobody asks. [Medium] General reader: no.
12. **The chest "thread" in Ch6 (l.1787–1791).** Tightness behind the ribs that "tightened hard enough to stop him," with the right hand going to the sleeve (l.1816). A nurse triages that as a possible cardiac presentation until proven otherwise. The book may mean it as magic, or as a choice not to look. I can't tell which. [Low-medium] General reader: no.
13. **Shade's palm.** Glass cut, bleeding in the first scene (l.4012), then washed and wrapped (l.4332–4342). Nobody looks for retained glass or checks tendon function. He's also "thrown backward into the low barrier" (l.3950), with a smear on his collar later (l.4620). [Low-medium] General reader: no.
14. **Cat claw (Ch3, l.457).** Cat-scratch wounds carry infection risk and usually need cleaning. Pathwell's paper closes it, which is fine in this world, but the book never mentions cleaning or infection. [Low] General reader: no.

## Where an injury should still be costing a character something and isn't

- **Pathwell's arm.** This is the big one. In Ch17 he carries wet archive boxes (l.6805–6806) and copies route notes with "a pencil" (l.6908). The arm should show in those scenes. Instead the cost goes quiet until the last chapter.

## Top ten

Most important first.

1. **Pathwell's body is never examined after the blob, the museum, or the pins.** Grey skin, a wet cough, a crushed rib band, a temple laceration, a chest impact, and a neck strike are all on the page, and the only exam is one summary line (Ch16, l.6308). **Cut** one of the three quips at Ch7 l.1992–1996 so the first exam can land. Give him one real exam with one or two findings the characters act on. The cost would then feel like a cost, and his later sacrifice would hit harder.
2. **Elizabeth's head has four impacts and no cost.** Cracked on the door frame (Ch5, l.1585), face-first in roots (Ch4, l.676), a faint onto her knees (Ch3, l.407), and a backward fall under Pathwell (Ch7, l.1984). Give one of these a headache or nausea that she notices. "Concussion" appears only once, unattributed (Ch17, l.7040). Either establish it for someone or cut the line.
3. **Pathwell's right arm is contradictory and then goes silent.** Ch3 says "there wasn't a mark on it" (l.607). Ch18 says his left hand rests "over the place where the roots had gone in" (l.7586). A reader will take "the place" as a mark. Decide whether the damage is visible. Then show one function limit in the Ch17 pencil-copy scene (l.6908–6932), where it would cost him the most.
4. **The wagon crush is too cheap.** "Then she wakes up sore and hungry" (Ch4, l.981) is the most serious injury in the first half, and it resolves into a joke. **Cut** that line or replace it with a stated, lasting deficit. Keep the page's price on Elizabeth, where it already sits.
5. **Stansbury's ribs need to be paid twice.** Once after the tackle in Ch11 (l.3986), and once in Ch16 when he's stretching the hand. Right now he's the one injured man who gets off.
6. **Milo's smoke cough and Elizabeth's own smoke exposure go unchecked.** One healer line, a chest listen, and a night of watching would do it (Ch15, l.5768 and l.5975; Ch16, l.6159).
7. **Pathwell's neck after the crash.** Hand at the back of the neck (Ch8, l.2301), peas (Ch10, l.3106), then nothing. One neck check, or one stiff-neck line, would close it.
8. **The chest "thread" in Ch6 needs a decision.** It reads as cardiac triage and is written as a magical pull. Either make it clearly magic, or give it a sign that marks it as not cardiac. Right now a clinical reader will flag it and a general reader won't notice.
9. **Protect the reduction scene (Ch12, l.4213–4303).** It is the model for how the rest of the book should handle injuries: the specific check, the honest "Joint's partly out," and "There was no paper in it anywhere." Use its rhythm elsewhere.
10. **Prose tics around pain repeat.** "pain went white" (l.4032, l.5887), "drove the breath out of her" and "pulled the breath out of her" (l.1984, l.1283), and "pain so bright" (l.3988). Vary or cut one each. Small, but the injury language is where the reader's attention lands.

In Ch17, show one function limit in the pencil-copy scene (l.6908–6932), and give the tremor a trajectory. Size: **small**. Reader gain: the book's central cost stops going quiet.
3. **Give Elizabeth one headache.** Chapter 8 to 11, or the dirt in Chapter 16 (l.6139). One sentence about light, sound or nausea, and one line in Chapter 17 saying whether the head is fine. Size: **small**.
4. **Replace the crush ending.** Chapter 4 (l.975–981) and Chapter 16 (l.6397). Name a lasting deficit, or let the cost sit on the page and on Elizabeth. Size: **small**.
5. **Pay Stansbury's ribs twice.** Chapter 11, after the tackle at l.3986, and Chapter 16, with the burn dressing. Size: **small**.
6. **Check Milo's chest and Elizabeth's.** Chapter 15 (l.5768, l.5975) and Chapter 16 (l.6159). A chest listen and one night of watching. Size: **small**.
7. **Settle the concussion.** Establish it for Pathwell at the museum (Ch11, around l.4111–4117), or cut the l.7040 line. Size: **small**.
8. **Decide the chest "thread."** Chapter 6 (l.1787–1816). Make it clearly magical, or give it one sign that rules out the cardiac reading. Size: **small**.
9. **Add a neck check.** Chapter 8 (l.2301) to Chapter 10 (l.3106). Size: **small**.

### The shape of the best version

A reader would experience the book as one where bodies keep score. Pathwell carries the arm, the ribs, the neck, the cough that fades, and the chest pressure he keeps not naming, and his brother and Elizabeth keep asking about each of them. Elizabeth's shoulder and head are her costs. Stansbury's hand and ribs are his. Milo's ankle and lungs are a child's. The cookbook costs memory, and the healers' ordinary care sits underneath the magic as the honest counterweight. The jokes stay, because the characters are funny and the book knows it, but each joke lands on a body that has been checked. Chapters 11 and 12 already do this best, and the rest should read more like them.

## Confidence and gaps

- I'm confident about the missing neuro and chest checks, the dropped rib, neck and cough threads, and the "sore and hungry" line.
- I don't know whether the arm's damage is meant to be invisible. The text says both "not a mark" and "the place where the roots had gone in."
- I don't know whose concussion l.7040 refers to.
- Chapter 17 is where I was least engaged, because the restitution lists run long. The medical content there is thin, and that's where the costs fade.
