## Part 3: Jokes in the wrong beat

Most important first. I'm confident on 1-3, medium on 4-6.

1. **Ch 18, the confession followed by the geese.** Pathwell says, "He was still trying to get free... He was calling for help." Then: "I thought that if the creature took him, it would be finished." That's the darkest thing anyone admits in the book. Elizabeth gets "That's not what I did." and a walk into the stacks. Within about forty lines we're on a field guide where "every bird had been assigned an inaccurate personality," then "They have learned commerce." / "They steal buttons.", then the "Perfect" button. Each joke is good. Together they tell the reader the confession is handled, and it isn't. The ending's warmth borrows against a debt the scene never pays.
2. **Ch 11, Pathwell's first line after Elizabeth leaves hurt:** "I am adjusting my brand." The narrator joins in with "a cold pack that took both hands and an insult to activate." Pathwell has just broken her shoulder and destroyed originals. The scene recovers ("I was. Then I looked at the cases."), but the first beat of his guilt is a quip. Ch 16 shows you know this is his flaw. Here the narrator lets it pass.
3. **Ch 11, the narration on her injury:** "Elizabeth heard herself make a noise she would later deny. Her arm stopped belonging anywhere reasonable." This is the protagonist's first real wound, and the prose winks at it. The healer scene in Ch 12 earns its laughs ("Yes. That. Definitely that."). This one comes too early.
4. **Ch 3, "Will that be paper or plastic?" / "Surprise me."** It comes straight after "Pathwell screamed in a million voices." The handcart bit, a few lines later, is the earned release. Two releases stacked weaken both. Cut the first.
5. **Ch 15, "The healer's instruction returned with perfect timing."** This is the narrator being clever in the middle of a rescue from a fire. The line it introduces ("You are not using this arm today.") does the work without help.
6. **Ch 5, "Shotgun!" ... knocking her into the hood at the waist.** It's not a deflation so much as a mis-aimed laugh. Elizabeth has just been drenched by the tree, and the bit makes Pathwell careless with her body. Combined with the run of physical gags in Ch 4-5 (the dirt kiss, the hood, "cracking her head on the door frame"), early Elizabeth is a pratfall target. The book later makes her body matter (Ch 11-16). If that shift is deliberate, build it. If not, drop the slapstick.
7. **The narrator commenting on laughs** is a habit, not one moment: "ridiculous enough that on another day Elizabeth might have laughed" (Ch 7), "Nothing was funny." (Ch 7), "Elizabeth almost laughed. She didn't." (Ch 10), "She almost laughed." (Ch 12), "There was no joke in it." (Ch 14), "Elizabeth almost laughed." (Ch 16). Each one tells the reader how to take a beat the dialogue already set. "There was no joke in it." is the worst offender, because "I will." with no topper already says it.

Places I expected to flag but which work: "Same arrogance in a better coat." plus Shade glancing at his jacket (Ch 14) works because nobody says anything. Milo's "I could give it to somebody with one foot" right before "Shade's dead?" (Ch 16) works as laugh-then-cry. "He's probably terrible!" at the restitution hearing (Ch 16) works because "Elizabeth didn't laugh. She didn't mind that someone else had." That last one is exactly how to place a laugh inside grief.

## Part 4: Where a comic habit turns into heart

Ranked by how well the setup supports the turn.

1. **"He still owes me a hand." (Ch 16).** The setup is Shade's right hand being pulled open all book, plus Hearts in Ch 13 and "That hand was promising." It works because the pun is a child's, unintended, and lands on all three meanings at once.
2. **The terrible coffee, then "I considered asking him to leave on that basis alone," then crying (Ch 16).** The setup is nine appearances across the book, with Shade adopting it in Ch 13 ("Then it's yours."). It works because Mama Baga, the least jokey person in Camp, makes the joke, and the laugh "broke in the middle." It's the best laugh-to-cry turn in the book.
3. **"His sock had finally stayed up. Elizabeth hated that detail immediately." (Ch 15).** The setup is the fallen sock from Ch 4 on. It works because the runner completes at the moment it can't be enjoyed, and the character says so.
4. **"LIKED POCKETS." (Ch 16).** The setup is Ch 13's "Pockets." / "I changed my mind." It works because the archivist's dryness, which has been played for laughs all book, becomes the only funeral available.
5. **"You are not making that joke today." (Ch 14) and "You don't get to use being funny to decide when this becomes easier." (Ch 16).** The setup is Pathwell's patter from Ch 1. It works because the book stops one character's comedy as a moral act. Then Ch 17 has him hold back "This child is cheating." That's the payoff: a joke he chooses not to make.
6. **"She believed soup was an argument you could win." (Ch 4).** The setup is the Ch 3 flashback ("The pepper was the best part, Lizzy."; "Then you made them wrong.") and "She had forgotten she was good at parties." Her wit becomes how she lets go of the book.
7. **The dart (Ch 6).** The setup is the effortless bullseye thrown without looking. The miss with "The wall had been arrogant all day." shows us the shaking hand without anyone mentioning it.
8. **The welcome speech redone (Ch 17).** The setup is Ch 4's list with "Did I say runemasters?" It works because he does it for refugees, "with more spaewife in it," and asks before taking the grandmother's bag.
9. **"Oh, fine, you can have a dumpling." (Ch 2).** The setup comes three lines earlier with "I don't share." It's the smallest version of the book's move, and it's the moment I started trusting the writer.
10. **The peas (Ch 10).** The setup is "Peas." / "Is that a diagnosis?" The comic prop stays in his lap through the confession ("His thumb pressed a dent into the plastic bag.").
11. **HESS SAYS FOUR (Ch 17).** The setup is in Ch 4. It changes Pathwell's view of "mistakes" through a joke, and it leads to him undoing the Susquehanna correction.

## Part 5: Whose comedy is their own, and whose is the house style

**Their own.**
- **Pathwell.** His comedy comes from need, and the book even says so: "Pathwell filled silence because silence looked undefended." His lines are the food, the theatrical lists, "I tip historically," "I am an excellent driver," and the comedy of being wrong with total confidence. He's the clearest comic character in the book.
- **Milo.** He has kid logic and kid legalism: "It wasn't me." / "You were going to.", "Pathwell stupid?", "Strong case.", "I could give it to somebody with one foot." His jokes come from what he wants (to win, to be right, to matter). He's the funniest secondary character because his comedy is about getting what he wants, not just getting the last word.
- **Shade.** He starts curt, almost house style, with "Would you prefer applause?" He becomes himself through discovery: "Pockets." / "Your foot is purple." / "That hand was promising." Watching someone learn to joke is his arc, and it's done well.
- **The healer.** Clinical comedy built on consent: "people point at the wrong thing surprisingly often," "Briefly? Very.", "I will ask you very firmly not to." It's distinct because her jokes come from her job.

**Mixed.**
- **Elizabeth.** Her own comedy is early panic-listing, the Mrs. Pathwell improvisation (hostess competence), "Try some nouns.", "That seemed reasonable at the time." / "So did the ditch.", and worrying about the farmer. As the book goes on, she picks up house cadence: "Important distinction.", "Strong sales pitch.", "Define using.", and the one-word topper. Some borrowing is deliberate and flagged ("You came to a bar. I happen to be in it." / "This is spreading."; her "Perfect"). Much of it isn't. More importantly, the book tells us she's funny ("She had forgotten she was good at parties"; the landlord joke) long before it shows us.
- **Stansbury.** Most of his best material is his own: grieving for the car ("I have not reached the axle emotionally."), the collector's caution, "It's a series." His sentence shapes are house: "Survive it.", "Noted.", "Occupationally.", "It improves compliance."

**Borrowed from the house style.**
- **The shopkeeper and the archivist** run the same engine: the clipped bureaucrat with a dry topper. Shopkeeper: "I dislike paperwork.", "A month is not a character trait." / "Accounting.", "I dislike novelty." Archivist: "Specificity is expensive.", "That is generally the opposite of an archive.", "Categories are temporarily flexible." Swap their lines and I wouldn't notice. Each is good on its own. Together they're one character in two coats.
- **Mama Baga** is terse and aphoristic ("That is not a posture.", "Same arrogance in a better coat."). She's the best of the deadpans, but that's the register everyone else is pitched near, so she stands out less than she should.
- **The narrator** writes in a Pratchett-adjacent wry personification mode: lights "that weren't trying hard enough," the mailbox that "declined responsibility," the mounted fish "withholding judgment," detergent boxes "small enough to qualify as insults," the beetle children ("It's practicing."). These are well made, and they're the house. In tense beats they compete with the characters (Part 3).

The **one-word topper** is the house signature: "Branding." "Diversifying." "Historically." "Occupationally." "Evidence." "Corruption." "Authentic." "Accounting." "Immensely." "Profoundly." "Technically." "Noted." It's funny about a third of the time it appears. On the shelf this book wants to sit on (cozy, grief-literate fantasy comedy, next to Klune, Kingfisher, or a gentler Pratchett), the best books give each speaker a different way of being funny. Here too many people are funny in the same way.

## Part 6: Punch-up notes (the scene wants a laugh and doesn't have one)

I'm describing the gap, not writing the joke.

- **Ch 1-2, Elizabeth's own wit.** She's all reaction until Ch 4. We're told she tells a landlord joke good enough to make a stranger laugh. Either put that joke on the page or give her one line in the apartment or at the dumpling table that comes from her, not him. The reader needs to know she's funny before the book makes her grieve. Otherwise Ch 4's soup line arrives as a surprise rather than as recognition.
- **Ch 2, the pajamas.** The coat-over-pajamas reveal is buried in a clause in Ch 4 ("which Elizabeth had been trying not to think about since the lobby"). Plant the pajamas visibly at the curb in Ch 2 so the reader enjoys her dignity crisis through the dumplings and the bookstore. Then Mama Baga's clothes in Ch 4 pay off a joke rather than reveal one.
- **Ch 5-6, "Mrs. Pathwell."** The fake-marriage improv is Elizabeth's best early bit, and nobody reacts to it afterward. Stansbury overheard it. The bar is the obvious place for the brother to get a laugh out of it ("Mrs. Elizabeth" in Ch 6 gestures at it but isn't the payoff).
- **Wrong day of the week (Ch 2, Ch 5).** It's two beats short of three. A third, late, with Pathwell getting the day right, or Elizabeth getting it wrong now that she's living on Camp time, would close it and say something.
- **The Cadillac (after Ch 10).** The restitution hearing (Ch 16) and the museum session (Ch 17) are built for it. Stansbury has standing ("It's a series."). The car never comes up. It's the funniest runner left open.
- **"I am an excellent driver" (Ch 6, Ch 8).** Ch 18 has them walking. Either let him drive once somewhere, so the claim is tested, or cut the second instance.
- **Ch 9, the car song.** Shade sings, loses the words, hears her, and stops. It's a lovely quiet beat that wants one small spoken exchange so it's theirs and not just observed.
- **Ch 12, the diary donation.** It's the cookbook scene again, structurally: the archivist, name, family name, a card. The second time through wants a comic variation, Elizabeth knowing the procedure and getting ahead of the archivist, so the repeat reads as growth rather than recurrence.
- **Ch 17, Elizabeth at work.** Her ordinary world never gets a laugh after Ch 2: the coworkers who cut ELIZ, the meeting she missed. One beat back at the office would pay off the meeting and the cake together.
- **Ch 18, the opening phone call.** It's good ("At least four."). The walk to the laundromat wants one exchange where Elizabeth uses one of Pathwell's old lines on him and he notices. The book does this with "It has required practice." Make it land harder.

## Part 7: Ranked top ten (most important first)

1. **Your supporting cast shares one comic voice.** The shopkeeper, archivist, Mama Baga, Stansbury, and later Elizabeth all deliver the clipped deadpan with a one-word topper. Give each a different engine: the shopkeeper as an accountant who enjoys the misery, the archivist as a pedant who loves mistakes, Mama Baga in silences and actions more than lines, Stansbury in grievance and property. This is the biggest gap between this book and the published comedies it's close to. (High confidence.)
2. **The Ch 18 confession is buried under the ending's comedy.** Pathwell admitting he watched Shade call for help is the darkest moral beat in the book, and it's followed within a page by bird personalities, geese, and "Perfect." Either move it earlier (Ch 16 or 17) so the ending can be earned, or give it room and put the comedy only after Elizabeth has visibly decided something about it. (High.)
3. **Your runner craft is real. Finish the job.** Coffee, sock, Hearts/hand, pockets, and Hess are at a professional level. The landlord joke (never told), the Cadillac and axle, "excellent driver," the wrong day, ELIZ, and "Mrs. Pathwell" are left open. Pay each off or cut it. Open runners make the closed ones look accidental. (High.)
4. **Show that Elizabeth is funny before the book needs her to be.** The book says she's good at parties and tells a landlord joke, but she doesn't get a laugh of her own until Ch 4. Plant her comic signature (hostess competence, politeness, worrying about property owners) in Ch 1-2, and keep her from drifting into the house cadence later. (High.)
5. **Run a tic pass.** "X is not the same as Y" appears a dozen or more times. "You were going to" and its variants, about five. "Almost laughed," about six. "Apparently," everywhere. The one-word topper, constantly. Cut each by at least half, so the remaining ones register as character. (High.)
6. **Stop the narrator from commenting on laughs.** "There was no joke in it." "Nothing was funny." "Almost laughed." The dialogue already lands those beats. Trust it. (Medium-high.)
7. **Take the jokes out of the pain beats.** That means "paper or plastic" (Ch 3), "a noise she would later deny" (Ch 11), "I am adjusting my brand" and the "insult to activate" cold pack (Ch 11), and "perfect timing" (Ch 15). Each is small. Together they teach the reader not to trust your serious moments, which is the opposite of what Ch 14 and 16 are building. (Medium-high.)
8. **The magic mechanics are where the book goes dry.** The pruning ceremony (Ch 3), the van confession (Ch 10: "pruned ahead," "malformed pruning," "someone asked me for help"), and the rejoining frame (Ch 14: "two anchors, one return") are abstract. What carries Ch 14 is Shade's "No." Make the mechanics concrete in human terms, or shorter, and let character do the work. In Ch 10 especially, I still can't picture what Pathwell did. (Medium.)
9. **Ch 17 is a long tag.** The material is mostly excellent (HESS SAYS FOUR, The Lemon, the museum session, Hearts, the welcome speech), but after the climax it's a montage of several weeks before the final chapter. Cut it by about a quarter. Keep every beat where Pathwell is stopped from improving something. Cut the ones that restate it. (Medium.)
10. **Fix the small confusions and the in-joke.** "Big cat" / "grande gatto" (Ch 10, 16) needs an origin or needs to go. In Ch 5, "He lies, you know." then "I don't lie." isn't clear: did she say it aloud? Elizabeth's mom (Ch 6, "Red sky at night") appears once and never again. I was only half sure what Pathwell was tempted to do with the Merritt cookbook (Ch 18), which weakens "I may need to sit down." (Medium.)

## Part 8: The best version

### 1. What to protect

Don't lose any of these in revision.

- "Oh, fine, you can have a dumpling." (Ch 2), three lines after "I don't share."
- "She believed soup was an argument you could win." / "Sorry. That was for me." (Ch 4)
- "Elizabeth chose the ditch." and its echo "It was the crash that woke her." (Ch 8), plus "That seemed reasonable at the time." / "So did the ditch."
- "Drive." / "Not cruel. Worse. Useful." (Ch 7)
- "I have not reached the axle emotionally." (Ch 10)
- "A crappy cup of coffee." (Ch 10). The runner becomes the plot.
- Shade's Hearts game, all of Ch 13, especially "knew exactly why each bad decision had been bad about three seconds after he made it," "Your foot is purple.", and "That hand was promising."
- "That's terrible." / "I know." / "Then it's yours." (Ch 13)
- "You are not making that joke today." (Ch 14)
- "His sock had finally stayed up. Elizabeth hated that detail immediately." (Ch 15)
- "He still owes me a hand." / "LIKED POCKETS." / "Archive burned. Categories are temporarily flexible." / "Leave it." (Ch 16)
- "You don't get to use being funny to decide when this becomes easier." (Ch 16)
- "I considered asking him to leave on that basis alone." into the crying, and "Person is the word I have for that." (Ch 16)
- "HESS SAYS FOUR." / "a family argument preserved as infrastructure" / "Copy the smudge." (Ch 17)
- "He remembered the runemasters this time." (Ch 17)
- "RUTH LIED ABOUT THE LARD." / "SHE DID NOT. YOU USED THE WRONG CUP." / "NOT THAT GOOD." (Ch 18)
- The final reversal: "Are you ready?" "No." "Perfect." (Ch 18)
- The structural idea under all of it: runners become the material of magic and memory. The comedy isn't decoration. It's what the characters share, and losing it is the grief.

### 2. The changes, in order of impact

1. **Give each supporting character a different comic engine** (all chapters; large). Go through every line of the shopkeeper, archivist, Mama Baga, healer, and Stansbury and ask whether anyone else could say it. Where they could, rebuild the joke from that character's want. For the reader, Camp becomes a place full of people instead of a room full of one voice. That raises the emotional stakes of the fire, because each loss is specific.
2. **Re-stage the Shade confession** (Ch 15-18; medium). Either seed Pathwell's inaction visibly in Ch 15 and bring the confession into Ch 16 or 17, where the book has room to sit with it, or keep it in Ch 18 and make Elizabeth's response a real beat, a decision rather than a walk, before any comedy returns. For the reader, the final "Perfect" becomes a choice made with full knowledge rather than a warm note that skips the bill.
3. **Close or cut the open runners** (Ch 1, 2, 5, 6, 8, 10, 16-18; small each, medium overall). Tell the landlord joke or stop mentioning it. Give the Cadillac a last beat in restitution. Give "excellent driver" a test or remove it. Give the wrong-day runner a third beat. Connect ELIZ to the name runner. Let Stansbury or someone react to "Mrs. Pathwell." For the reader, the comedy feels designed, so the big payoffs read as inevitable rather than lucky.
4. **Plant Elizabeth's comic signature early** (Ch 1-3; small-medium). Show the party competence, the politeness, and the worry about other people's property on the page in Ch 1-2. Plant the pajamas. Later, check each of her dry lines against the house voice and keep the ones only she would say. For the reader, she becomes the book's comic center, not its straight man, and her grief lands harder because we saw her funny first.
5. **Tic pass** (whole book; small-medium, mostly mechanical). Search for "not the same," "almost laughed," "were going to," "Apparently," "Fair," and standalone one-word replies, and halve them. For the reader, the dialogue's rhythm stops being visible as a pattern.
6. **Clear jokes out of pain beats and stop the narrator from flagging laughs** (Ch 3, 7, 10, 11, 12, 14, 15, 16; small). Cut the specific lines listed in Part 3. For the reader, every serious moment becomes trustworthy, which makes every laugh afterward bigger.
7. **Make the magic exposition run on character** (Ch 3, 10, 14; medium). In Ch 10, give one concrete image of the old job and its human cost in place of the jargon. In Ch 14, trim the frame mechanics so that Shade's "No." and Mama Baga's "Stop." carry the scene. For the reader, the two most important plot scenes stop being the two least engaging.
8. **Trim Ch 17** (Ch 17; medium-small). Keep the rack, HESS, Susquehanna, the museum session, The Lemon, the welcome speech, and Hearts. Compress the connective summary. For the reader, the denouement keeps its jokes and loses its drag.
9. **Fix the confusions** (Ch 5, 6, 10, 16, 18; small). Origin or cut for "big cat," the "I don't lie" exchange, Elizabeth's mom, and clarity on the Merritt temptation.

### 3. The shape of the best version

A woman who has spent years carrying plates up thirteen stairs is pulled out of her too-quiet apartment by a man who eats dumplings while she cries, and from the first chapter she's funny too. She fights back, tells her own jokes, and keeps worrying about her landlord while the world goes strange. The jokes build from page one, and every one belongs to someone: the shopkeeper who enjoys a bill, the archivist who loves a mistake, the brother grieving for his car, the boy whose sock won't stay up. The reader starts collecting runners without noticing: bad coffee, socks, pockets, a card game. When the fire comes, each of those runners comes back with a different meaning, and the reader realizes the jokes were never filler. They were what these people shared, and now the losses are made of them.

Nobody winks during the worst moments, so when the laughs come back, through a child's accidental pun about a hand of Hearts, a ledger line that reads LIKED POCKETS, or a joke about bad coffee that turns into crying, they hit with the full weight of the book. Pathwell's confession gets the room it needs. Elizabeth decides something about him in front of us, and only after that does the book let itself be light again: a cookbook full of family arguments, geese that steal buttons, and a door she opens herself. "Are you ready?" "No." "Perfect." reads as a promise made with open eyes. A comparable comedy on the shelf would make me laugh and then make me cry, and this one already does both. The revision is about making it do both more reliably and with more voices.
