## Headline

No stoppers. The book ends well: it ends on a small, warm, funny errand, with the Ch1 triplet turned the right way round, and nothing the reader was promised is left in the middle of the floor except one object (the foam dagger, see Pass 1b). The Merritt put-back is the best beat in the chapter: a man picks up a book, is visibly tempted, and puts it back square to the edge of the shelf, and the page lets him do it in silence. The snags are small and mostly mechanical: a sentence at L88 that says the opposite of what the scene shows, "the same alley" at L109 (no alley exists), the narrator's four-item formula for his temptation at L291, two untagged lines that flip speaker (L354, L362), a muddled backward look at Ch4 (L348), and a short run of narrator commentary at L416-L438 that tells what the scene has already shown. The tics are down hard from the pre-pass text (the "Not... / No..." paragraphs are 3 now, from 27), but three families have moved in: three absence-lists (L41, L150, L424), five one-word "-ly" replies, and about fifteen "Name looked" openers. The records are stale (no P43 entry; the Ledger and Plan still describe the old text).

## Pass 1b: the questions the launching agent set

**The Merritt cookbook: what happens and why.** Elizabeth sees a green cloth family cookbook on the end of a shelf (L211-L217), and sees Pathwell see it (L219-L221, "Of course he was."). He asks "May I?" (L247), opens it, the margins make them both smile, then "he turned another page, and the smile faded" (L289). His eyes go to the ledger under the shopkeeper's arm and stay there. He rereads one margin note, closes the book, puts it back where it was, takes his hand off the cover (L295-L299). She says nothing. The shopkeeper says he may need to sit down; Pathwell says he is "sitting spiritually" (L303-L307). The why, from the page and my memory of Ch15 and Ch17: it is a book that resembles the one she lost, he can see a way to pay for it (the ledger), and he knows what that sort of fix has cost.

**Did I understand the temptation and the choice not to act?** The temptation, yes, but partly from Ch17's Henry Vale refusal rather than from this page: on this page nothing says that anyone needs the book, and the narrator supplies the word "need" in a formula (L291). The choice not to act, yes, completely; the put-back needs no explanation and "resemblance was enough" at L330 arrives afterwards as the reason, which is the right order. It is a plain, quiet read.

**Was it enough?** Yes. It is enough to cut the formula at L291 and keep the glance. After the cut a cold reader who has read Ch3, Ch15 and Ch17 still gets it from "the smile faded", the ledger glance and the silent return, and a reader who hasn't gets "he wanted it, and didn't take it". I would not add a line.

**"It was my fault": what it refers to, who comes to mind first.** First: Shade. The coffee line "Shade would have liked it." sits 14 lines earlier (L316), and "his empty hand" reads like the hand that kept opening toward Shade. Second, immediately after: the cookbook, because "resemblance" at L330 is the Merritt book standing in for Nana's, and so the whole replacement habit (Nana's cookbook burned; the page he spent). Third, Elizabeth's shoulder and the archive fire. I could not tell from the page which he means and I think that is the point; I held it open and nothing after it asked me to close it. "Resemblance" works for both readings (Shade was made from him and looked like him; the Merritt book looks like Nana's), which may be luck or may be the best word in the chapter.

**The ending.** Elizabeth asks "Are you ready?", Pathwell says "No.", Elizabeth says "Perfect." I read it that way on the first pass because L440 names her ("Elizabeth kept her hand on the handle.") immediately before the bare lines. For one beat I heard it in Ch1's order (his "Ready?", her "No.", his "Perfect.") and had to settle who says "No." It settled at once. It was never really unclear and it is not a stopper, but it is the one place in the book where who-says-what matters as much as what is said, and the page carries it on a single paragraph.

**Does the book end well? Is any promised object or thread left hanging?** It ends well: a door, cold evening air, geese that "remember me", a hand on a handle, three plain lines, nothing after. Threads: Nana's cookbook and diary are paid (L195-L209, the torn sugar-cookie page, the Merritt book); the museum and Camp are paid ("available through the proper route"); the account at zero and the front door are paid (L33, L172). Hanging, as I read cold: **the foam dagger**. Ch5 gives it, Ch7 uses it, Ch14 and Ch15 have her hand go to it and stay; it is not in Ch16, Ch17 or Ch18, except that "knife" sits in the list of things she was not told to bring (L41), which made me look for it and not find it. Whether Pathwell can still prune is the other open thread, and it is open on purpose (he chooses not to find out); it did not feel hanging, it felt like a man not touching a stove.
