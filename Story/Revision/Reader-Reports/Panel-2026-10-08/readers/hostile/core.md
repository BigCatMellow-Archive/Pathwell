# Core questions, as the hostile reader

1. What is this book about?
It is about grief that can be spent like money, and about the people who keep objects for others: archivists, shopkeepers, a cook's handwriting in the margins. A woman who has just moved into a city apartment learns that the memories stored in her family's things can be used as a currency, and that every use costs someone something real. Underneath the magic it asks what we owe the dead when we hand over what they left, and whether a man who has paid for his mistakes with other people's futures can ever be held to account for them.

2. The moment that worked best for me.
Chapter 4, the healing with Nana's cookbook. Elizabeth gives up the whole book, not just the page, and the narration says so plainly: "That was the choice. / Everything afterward was consequence." The healer's note, "MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH," and the flu memory that follows are specific, and the magic costs her something she cannot take back. It is the first time I believed the grief was paid for. Chapter 11's ledger line, "SAM HOME TODAY. THIN AS A FENCE RAIL. ATE THREE EGGS.", runs a close second.

3. The moment that worked least for me.
Chapter 5, the school sequence and the Stansbury business. The "Honey, you just wandered off" cover story, the foam swords, the "noodle" banter and the "Shotgun!" gag all run together in a way that makes the book's real question (is selling children's work any different from what Pathwell did at the bookshop?) fall through the floor. Elizabeth asks the right question and the chapter laughs over it.

4. Where I got confused or stopped believing it.
- Shade. I never understood how a failed working became a person, and in Chapter 10 Pathwell says he does not know either. I accepted that as honest, but the book then gave Shade very little room to be a person before he was taken.
- The Space Between, the "Rai Stone," "Lydian coins," "Bakhtak," and the folklore list in Chapter 4 read as nouns. I could not tell what the economy is for, or who sets its prices.
- "He still has it" at the end of Chapter 2 is ambiguous. I could not tell whether it means the book, the diary, or the page.
- Thumper's six-toed paw print in Chapter 12 has no setup that I could find.
- By Chapter 17 I stopped believing the happy aftermath. The yellow wagon and the sitcom beats felt like the book wanted me charmed before it would let me alone with the loss.

5. What I wanted and did not get.
- A clear account of the magic's cost at scale, beyond "it is paid in pieces of you."
- Shade given enough time to choose something Pathwell could not decide for him.
- Elizabeth's mother (mentioned once, in Chapter 6) given one line of weight, or cut.
- Nana's funeral, which Elizabeth tries to write about in Chapter 12 and gives up on. I wanted the page she could not finish.
- An ending that did not end on a greeting-card line. The door at the end of Chapter 18 is the better close, and I wanted the book to stop there.

6. One thing I would tell the author.
Stop following every loss with a joke. Let one sad thing sit for a full page before anyone speaks, and trust the reader to feel it. The cookbook, the ledger and the burning archive already prove the book can do this, so the wink is a choice, not a limit.
