# Hostile reader report

## (a) One-star review

Rating: one star out of five.

The best thing I can say about this book is that its central idea is real. A cookbook can heal a stranger, but only if its owner gives it away, and the book will not let her take it back: "That was the choice. / Everything afterward was consequence." (Ch. 4). I believed that, and it is the reason I read all eighteen chapters instead of stopping at four.

So why one star? Because the book keeps taking that idea back with a wink.

Chapter 1 has a stranger reading a woman's diary "like a magazine in a waiting room," and within three pages he is saying "Don't think," then "Ready?" "No." "Perfect." That exchange returns as the last line of Chapter 18. By then I had heard its rhythm so often I could finish it. The sibling banter in Chapters 5, 6, 10, 11 and 13 runs on the same engine: "Shotgun!", "noodle," "Branding," "Stagnation." It is pleasant the first time and a tic by Chapter 8.

The grief keeps arriving with a joke attached. In Chapter 10 a woman with a bleeding heel gets "The mailbox declined responsibility." In Chapter 16, the morning after a man has been taken by the blob in front of her, the archive ledger reads "LIKED POCKETS." Mama Baga has a zinger for every scene: "Don't become decorative." "When somebody tells you stop, develop a new hobby." I stopped trusting the sentiment because the book so rarely let it sit still.

The folklore is costume. Chapter 4 lists "charmers, blessers, wizards and witches... spaewives and the gwyllion... mambos... runemasters," and not one of those names changes how anyone behaves. Chapter 5 drops "Bakhtak" and never explains it. The Lydian coins, the Rai Stone, and "Thumper" (Chapter 12, a six-toed paw print, with no setup I could find) are nouns. Chapter 5 gives the magic a real rule, that the waste-eater is "unpleasant rather than murderous," and then the book drops the rule whenever it is inconvenient.

Pathwell keeps saying the same thing. He pruned, he paid, and in Chapter 11 he says "I can contain this." Elizabeth has to remind him in Chapter 14: "That's what you said at the museum." The book knows this. Knowing it is not the same as changing it, and by Chapter 14 I wanted him to be wrong in a new way.

The best chapters are the ones where the jokes stop. The diner in Chapter 9 is the author at full strength: "You were never the point." "Don't make that sentence larger than it is." And the ledger in Chapter 11, "SAM HOME TODAY. THIN AS A FENCE RAIL. ATE THREE EGGS.", is the best sentence in the book, followed at once by "Nobody made a joke after that." The book can do this. It should do it more often.

Shade is its best invention: a man who says "I remember things I did not live," and who is taken in Chapter 15 after roughly four chapters in which to become a person. "Shade smiled. It was small, and it was his." is a good line. I wanted the pages it would have taken to earn it.

This is a book with one great idea and no editor willing to tell its author to stop being charming. One star, because I am not persuaded it trusts its own grief.

## (b) The single change that would have won me over

Give Shade a scene of his own, alone with Elizabeth or with Milo and without Pathwell in the room, in which he decides something about his own future that Pathwell cannot decide for him. Put it in Chapter 13, before the frame in Chapter 14. Right now he gets a card game, a coffee lesson and a green jacket, and then he is gone. If the reader is meant to grieve him, the book has to spend the time to make his refusal cost something. The argument is already in Chapter 14 ("Do I remain myself?" / "I think the distinction can survive." / "Can." / "Not will."). It only needs Shade to win one of his own.

## (c) The one thing I would grudgingly admit works

The book keeps the loss lost. In Chapter 15, when the archive burns, the narration refuses the usual glow: "No spell. / No sacrifice. / No last message. / Just fire." Elizabeth's diary and Nana's cookbook go to ash with no magical payoff, and Chapter 17 holds the same line: "The information survived. The page didn't." That is the book's most honest decision, and it is the one I would defend against the rest of this review.
