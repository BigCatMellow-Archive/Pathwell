## Chapter 1

- Annoyed: A stranger is rifling through her boxes and reading her diary, and the book asks me to find that charming. The stranger is "Nice", the monster is "some sort of blob", and it is also "the behemoth" and "an ooze" in the same scene. The magic arrives with no setup, just "Don't think." Then a cookbook recipe is the key, and the cookbook's sentimental pull is delivered by one flashback that I was told to feel. "Because it was hers" is a non-answer dressed up as a line. He is also the man who "smiled" and asked "Ready?" and got "No." and said "Perfect." That exchange is a cute button, but I have seen it on a hundred covers.
- Grudging: The opening is fast and the room is specific. "Low tide, a long way from any sea." is a good smell, and "reading her diary like a magazine in a waiting room" is a sharp, bored image. "The cake, somehow, was fine." is the first line that made me smile. The party debris (ELIZ on the cake, the banner in the ooze) gives the room a history.
- Quotes: "reading her diary like a magazine in a waiting room" (Ch. 1). "Low tide, a long way from any sea." (Ch. 1). "The cake, somehow, was fine." (Ch. 1). "Because it was hers." (Ch. 1). "Ready?" / "No." / "Perfect." (Ch. 1).
- Craft notes: Tense slips ("the letters glowed"; "ooze searched through the gaps"). Fragments used as stage directions. Nothing has been lost yet.

## Chapter 2

- Annoyed: The "Tuesday" / "It's Thursday." / "Which makes it all the weirder." bit is a gag the book is pleased with, and I was not. The stranger's name is "Pathwell," and then he refuses a last name: "Don't need one. Saves everybody time." That is a quip doing the work a character should do. "Lizzy" is a nickname he hands her without asking, the way a bar owner would. The dumpling comedy ("I'm more peckish," "I don't share") is performed at length while her panic is in the next paragraph. The grief arrives as one lovely paragraph of exposition, and then she "just let it happen" and a stranger solves it with a dumpling. The lines "She heard her again. Lizzy." and "the heat of an oven" are the grandmother-magic again, so the mechanism now repeats: a smell, a voice, a memory she did not ask for. Ending on "He still has it." leaves "it" unclear to me. I would have to scroll up to know whether it is the book, the diary or the page.
- Grudging: "A life she'd packed into garbage bags and moved across the city because staying felt worse than leaving, but leaving didn't feel like anything at all." is the best sentence so far and it is about her, not the magic. The city-in-motion paragraph (siren, taxi splashing, laughing group) gives the night a real street. The street-level dislocation (the coffee shop gone, a park that should not be there) is the first image that feels like a world changing rather than a stunt.
- Quotes: "A life she'd packed into garbage bags and moved across the city because staying felt worse than leaving, but leaving didn't feel like anything at all." (Ch. 2). "They aren't trying to be anything they're not." (Ch. 2). "Don't need one. Saves everybody time." (Ch. 2). "He still has it." (Ch. 2).
- Craft notes: The stranger's dialogue is much more "lines" than "people" so far. He is never once frightened or hurt by the thing that broke her door, and nothing he says costs him anything.

## Chapter 3

- Annoyed: The whimsy is now stacked three deep in every paragraph: "part-time blood god," "We pay him in mice," "Love note. Cures most wounds," "Neither here nor there," "Surprise me," "Paper or plastic?" Each one is a small smile, and together they keep the room from ever being frightening. The cat draws blood and then the shopkeeper makes a joke about it. The bargaining over a "Rai Stone" and "thirty Lydian coins" is fun to read once, but I am not told what either is, or what "Camp Cunnan" is, or why anyone owes money to a bookshop. The price of the tome is paid in "potential," and then Pathwell jokes it away: "If I knew that, I'd make worse decisions with more confidence." The Latin ("Perdat quod esse poterat") is not glossed for the reader, so I cannot tell whether the sentence means what the scene needs. The handcart reveal is a straight comedy beat and it sits in the middle of the book's first real sacrifice.
- Grudging: The sacrifice itself is the first passage where something is actually taken and the cost is physical: "Pathwell screamed in a million voices, voices lost to time, burning out to become memories never made." That is a good, cruel image and I did not expect it. The shelves of "lives" (journals, letters, recipes on index cards "spotted with oil") is the best world idea so far, and the emotional spill-over where Elizabeth feels grief that is not hers, and her decision to "hold on to that", is the first time the magic costs her as a reader instead of a character. "Familiar things are useful" is a line I could have used as a thesis.
- Quotes: "Not books — lives." (Ch. 3). "None of that had been hers, and she held on to that." (Ch. 3). "Pathwell screamed in a million voices, voices lost to time, burning out to become memories never made." (Ch. 3). "If I knew that, I'd make worse decisions with more confidence." (Ch. 3). "These were never his to spend." (Ch. 3).
- Craft notes: The shelf-of-lives scene is the book's clearest argument so far that the magic is about grief and memory. Undercut risk: the sacrifice is followed within a page by a joke ("Surprise me.") that refuses to let it weigh anything.

## Chapter 4

- Annoyed: The camp introduction is a catalogue: "Dwelling of charmers, blessers, wizards and witches. Abode of the spaewives and the gwyllion... Haven to the mambos. Did I say runemasters?" That is a list of folklore names, Welsh, Norse, Vodou, Romani-coded fortune-telling, all parked in one sentence and used as a costume rack. None of them changes how anyone in the camp behaves, which is the exact thing I stop reading for. Mama Baga is the "kind-faced old woman with a lantern" who knows things before they are said ("He said you would say that," twice). The archivist is the wise-elder-with-a-ledger, and he is quotable on purpose: "Specificity is expensive." "That is generally the opposite of an archive." The children are there for charm: "It's practicing." "For what?" "Being dead." I laughed at that and then I noticed I was being handled. Pathwell's "Did I say runemasters?" shows the author enjoying the list more than the reader can. The sudden dressing-up scene (pajamas to pastel linen and a gold chain) is a wardrobe montage.
- Grudging: This is the first chapter where the magic costs something a reader can feel. Elizabeth gives up the whole cookbook, not just the page, and the book says so: "That was the choice. / Everything afterward was consequence." The healing is also quiet and specific: "Not burned. Spent." Nana's handwriting "changed depending on what she'd been doing when she wrote," and the note "MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH." is the best line in the book so far. The flu memory (seventeen, the bowl between both hands, Nana "pretending not to watch") is the emotional payoff I was waiting for, and it comes from the object, not from the narration. "Nobody looked at Elizabeth. Somehow that made the cookbook heavier." earns its place.
- Quotes: "That was the choice. / Everything afterward was consequence." (Ch. 4). "MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH." (Ch. 4). "She believed soup was an argument you could win." (Ch. 4). "Not burned. Spent." (Ch. 4). "Specificity is expensive." (Ch. 4). "Somehow that made the cookbook heavier." (Ch. 4).
- Craft notes: The healing-by-memory is the strongest mechanism in the book so far, because she has to surrender the object and cannot take it back. Against that, the folklore catalogue is the weakest passage I have read: names with no behavior.

## Chapter 5

- Annoyed: Pathwell says "It's Saturday," and a child corrects him to "Friday." It may be deliberate, but it reads as the book not checking its own calendar. The school sequence is a sitcom: the "Honey, you just wandered off" cover story, the walkie-talkie guard, "I'm the missus," the elbow. It is funny once and then it is the template for every scene that follows. The brothers' banter is "noodle" and "put your noodle away" and "Shotgun!" knocking her into the hood, which is sibling comedy I have already seen forty times. The foam-sword armoury and the "dragons printed on [the] shields" are an adult fantasy cast playing at being children. The aside "You would be forgiven for not stopping in the town..." breaks the close third person and I did not know who was talking. "Bakhtak" is dropped as a name and never explained.
- Grudging: Stansbury's explanation of the "waste" creature is the first piece of worldbuilding with a real rule: "Things like that clean up loose magical waste. Failed workings. Unsettled messes. If you don't stand between them and what they're after, they're unpleasant rather than murderous." The question "why it was already at your door" is a good hook, and Pathwell's "Her door." / "Was it?" is the most interesting exchange in the chapter. The children's-work energy idea (kids' drawings put up on the fridge and then into the attic, and "It would never be used otherwise") raises a real ethical question, and Elizabeth's answer ("It doesn't really feel any different from what you did at the bookstore") is the right question for a reader to ask. I wish the chapter had let it stay open. The "I don't lie" line lands because Stansbury has just told her that he does.
- Quotes: "Sometimes where you want to go and where you need to go aren't the same place." (Ch. 5). "Things like that clean up loose magical waste." (Ch. 5). "Her door." / "Was it?" (Ch. 5). "Selling off potential nobody was going to use." (Ch. 5). "He lies, you know. Don't listen to him when it comes to old stories." (Ch. 5).
- Craft notes: Ethics raised (selling children's work) and then left to banter. The chapter is at its best when the rule of the magic is stated plainly, and at its worst when it becomes a sitcom.

## Chapter 6

It is well written and it is the third time the book has used the same sibling-sniping rhythm, and I could feel it being pleased with itself. The bartender "could have sworn one of them came a hair before his hand did" is a hint that goes nowhere in this chapter; I will hold it against the book only if it is never paid. "The mounted fish went on withholding judgment" is a tic, the second mounted-fish joke in one chapter. Elizabeth's "My mom used to always tell me that" is the first mention of her mother, and it is dropped in one line. The "Mrs. Elizabeth" slip and the callback "apparently a posture" are cute, which is the book's default register.
- Grudging: The back-hall mirror is the best passage in the book so far. The magic is a pull "more the difference between pulling against a snag and moving with it," and the self-recognition is exact: "like hearing your own voice on a recording and recognizing every word without recognizing the man who said them." "His own face in the mirror suddenly felt less like evidence than like an accusation." is the first sentence that made me stop reading to think. The reveal that the thing came for him ("Something points in an unpleasant direction." / "Mine.") is restrained, and Elizabeth's decision to let him have "Not now. Not here. Not necessarily not her." is a good piece of character. The wet, heavy thud at the close repeats Chapter 1 word for word, which lands as an echo rather than a copy.
- Quotes: "like hearing your own voice on a recording and recognizing every word without recognizing the man who said them." (Ch. 6). "His own face in the mirror suddenly felt less like evidence than like an accusation." (Ch. 6). "Something points in an unpleasant direction." / "Mine." (Ch. 6). "Don't confuse restraint with virtue." (Ch. 6). "Wet. Heavy." (Ch. 6, echoing Ch. 1).
- Craft notes: The chapter turns the book from "who is Pathwell" to "what is coming for Pathwell." The mirror earns it. The bar banter does not.

It is the tenth or so time someone has said "Not here," and I am now reading the withholding as a device rather than as character. Stansbury's "I still do." / "Of course he did." closes a joke that was already closed.
- Grudging: The fight is staged clearly and the payoff is earned. ## Chapter 8

- Annoyed: The chapter opens "It was the crash that woke her," which is the first line of Chapter 1 repeated word for word. In Chapter 1 it was a literal waking. Here it is a car in a ditch, and the echo means nothing except that the author likes the sentence. Pathwell's "He'd gotten her name right. It didn't improve the sentence." and "I am an excellent driver" are running gags that are now worn. Shade's "Enough name." and "You have opinions about naming?" / "Several." / "Keep them." repeat the "Pathwell, last name? Don't need one" joke from Chapter 2, with the same cadence. The sedan appears on cue, "Perfect," Elizabeth says, and I did not believe the timing was accidental.
- Grudging: Elizabeth crashing the car on purpose is the best decision a character has made in the book, and I did not expect the author to let her be the one who does it. "Now we're stopped." is a line I laughed at, and "Very stopped," is right behind it. Shade is a real idea: a man who says "I remember things I did not live. Places I have never been... Habits that arrived before I had anything to be habitual about." The doubled-man setup gives the book a question about who Pathwell is, and Elizabeth's comparison ("Same ingredients. Different recipe.") is a sharp one that makes her angry in a way the story can use. "Too late to call it an accident." closes the chapter on a line that could be a hook for a reader who has not yet decided to quit.
- Quotes: "Elizabeth chose the ditch." (Ch. 8). "Now we're stopped." / "Very stopped." (Ch. 8). "I remember things I did not live." (Ch. 8). "Same ingredients. Different recipe." (Ch. 8). "I know old versions of your excuses." (Ch. 8). "Only then did she realize she was following another man who sounded certain." (Ch. 8). "Too late to call it an accident." (Ch. 8).

## Chapter 15

- Annoyed: "That was the choice. / Everything else burned afterward." is a line the book has already used in Chapter 4 ("That was the choice. / Everything afterward was consequence."). In Chapter 4 it was a turn. Here it is a refrain, and I now hear the author's hand on it. "A person could accumulate a surprising amount of life in a morning." and "Shade had never been particularly interested in deserving scenes." are the narrator stepping in to tell me how to feel about a man walking into a blob. "Elizabeth made a sound she had no intention of discussing later." is the same tic. Milo's one sock "somehow present" is still at it. And the echoed line "That hand was promising" is handled as an epitaph, which means it has to carry more weight than a card-game joke should. The dagger is raised, again, and again not used, and I am less sure each time that the book knows what it is holding.
- Grudging: The refusal of glow is the best decision in the book. "No spell. / No sacrifice. / No last message. / Just fire." takes the cookbook and the diary, the two objects the reader has been asked to love for fifteen chapters, and burns them with no magical reward, and I believed it. "She didn't regret the order." is a hard line that is also honest. Shade's death is handled plainly: "Shade smiled. It was small, and it was his." / "I was winning." / "Corrupt institution." The blob's behaviour, removing the spillage "as unclaimed waste" and then withdrawing "with the same patient indifference it had arrived with," follows the rule Stansbury set up in Chapter 5, which is the kind of follow-through I wanted from the middle of the book. Pathwell's "Will you stay?" / "No." is the most honest exchange between the two men.
- Quotes: "No spell. / No sacrifice. / No last message. / Just fire." (Ch. 15). "She didn't regret the order." (Ch. 15). "Will you stay?" / "No." (Ch. 15). "Shade smiled. It was small, and it was his." (Ch. 15). "Corrupt institution." (Ch. 15). "Then don't make this sound like a plan." / "It isn't." / "That was worse." (Ch. 15).
- Craft notes: The fire is the book finally trusting its own objects enough to let them go without a glow. The narrator's "Shade had never been particularly interested in deserving scenes" is the one place I wanted the author to step back.

## Chapter 16

- Annoyed: Camp's wise characters now have a zinger for everything, and the zingers come too fast to sound like grief. Mama Baga: "Don't become decorative." "When somebody tells you stop, develop a new hobby." "I considered asking him to leave on that basis alone." Milo's boot ("It was a good boot." / "The other one is still good." / "I could give it to somebody with one foot.") is sweet and is also a joke in the middle of a dead man's inventory. The archivist's "Archive burned. Categories are temporarily flexible." and the ledger line "LIKED POCKETS" are cute in the way that makes the death smaller. "Rare morning for those," from Pathwell is another quip, and Elizabeth shuts it down in the same paragraph, which tells me the author knows it. The scar-removal thread (Stansbury's burn, Pathwell's "ways to reduce what remains," "No.") is set up and left open at the end of the chapter, and I am not sure whether I am meant to watch for it or forget it.
- Grudging: This is the best chapter in the book for what it refuses to do. Elizabeth's "I need you not to make me responsible for whether you become a better person." is the sharpest sentence about apology I have read in a fantasy in some time, and "Don't make them one thing because one apology is easier." is right behind it. Mama Baga's cross-examination of Pathwell ("What do you owe?" / "Everything we lost." / "Wrong.") works because it refuses the grand gesture: "Then stop naming impossible payment as if saying a large enough number is accountability." The archivist's "UNKNOWN" on its own line is the best use of a single word in the book. Elizabeth's crying, once it comes, is specific: a list of what she has lost, and it ends with "For the fact that choosing Milo had been right and had still cost her things she wanted back." "Person is the word I have for that." closes Shade's account honestly.
- Quotes: "I need you not to make me responsible for whether you become a better person." (Ch. 16). "Don't make them one thing because one apology is easier." (Ch. 16). "Stop naming impossible payment as if saying a large enough number is accountability." (Ch. 16). "UNKNOWN." (Ch. 16). "For the fact that choosing Milo had been right and had still cost her things she wanted back." (Ch. 16). "Person is the word I have for that." (Ch. 16). "Loss didn't care much about paperwork." (Ch. 16).
- Craft notes: The restitution scene is the book's moral argument at its most concrete, and the best thing it does is refuse Pathwell the grand gesture. The epigram-machine Camp is the weakness I would most like cut.

## Chapter 17

- Annoyed: This is the aftermath, and it has turned into a sitcom montage. Pathwell and the rack, "Three inches?", "Copy the smudge," "I have been placed under unreasonable government," "Their government may be proportionate," "Color is also a dimension," "It resembles a diseased lemon," Stansbury's "revenge literature." Each one is pleasant. Together they start to read like the author needs the reader to be charmed before the book can leave the reader alone with the loss. The yellow wagon named "The Lemon" and the crooked label are the book making a bright thing out of the damage. Elizabeth's dead plant and her "long route home" are a list of gentle things, and "Adventure had been difficult to separate from being dragged somewhere. A diner at four in the morning could count..." is a paragraph of self-congratulating recap. The queen of spades turning up in Pathwell's hand is a neat gift to Milo's character, and it is also a little saccharine. The ending ("Not because she knew where she wanted to go yet. / Because she wanted to go.") is a greeting-card close, and after fifteen chapters of harder endings, I wanted the book to stop before it wrapped things up.
- Grudging: The restitution as a matter of rulers and provenance is the book's most adult idea, and it is funny in the right way. The curator's refusal of substitution is the thesis made practical: Pathwell offers Henry Vale's letters as a replacement and says "It is better than an empty—", stops, and then says "They are not Daniel's." / "Correct." The facsimile card "ORIGINAL DESTROYED IN COLLECTION INCIDENT." beside the reproduction of "SAM HOME TODAY. THIN AS A FENCE RAIL. ATE THREE EGGS." is the book's best use of its own object, and "The information survived. The page didn't." is the sentence I will remember. Elizabeth's witness statement, which "didn't soften the sequence" and records that "he used it anyway," is her honesty in its clearest form. "Pathwell had kept showing up when other people told him what needed doing. / Elizabeth didn't call that redemption. She called it evidence." is the best ending line in the book.
- Quotes: "The information survived. The page didn't." (Ch. 17). "After provenance review. Not as replacement." (Ch. 17). "She wrote that he used it anyway." (Ch. 17). "Elizabeth didn't call that redemption. She called it evidence." (Ch. 17). "Pathwell had kept showing up when other people told him what needed doing." (Ch. 17). "Because she wanted to go." (Ch. 17).
- Craft notes: The book's most honest move is the refusal to let Pathwell be redeemed by a gesture; the curator's "Not as replacement" is the rule the whole book runs on. The final page softens it again.

## Chapter 18

- Annoyed: The last chapter closes on "Are you ready?" / "No." / "Perfect.," the exchange from the first page of Chapter 1, and I saw the button coming from two chapters away. The exchanges are full of the book's reflex: "Important distinction." / "Historically." (a third use of the Chapter 12 bit), "I contain multitudes," "I hate when you know things about your own shop," "Occupationally," "They have learned commerce." / "They steal buttons." The five-routes negotiation is a comedy scene placed in the last quiet pages, and it cuts against the grief the chapter has just earned. "You are sitting spiritually." / "Do not ruin this." is the same quip-to-tears move as before. The mythology is still a rack of nouns: the Space Between, the shopkeeper's "Lydian coins," the "Rai Stone," Camp's "gwyllion" and "spaewives," none of which has been explained, and the book ends with a fifth route and a door that "remember[s]" Pathwell. I would have liked the book to tell me what the Space Between is for, or leave the door shut.
- Grudging: The Merritt family cookbook is the best object in the book, and its marginalia are the best comedy and the best argument together: "RUTH LIED ABOUT THE LARD." / "SHE DID NOT. YOU USED THE WRONG CUP." / "ALSO GOOD WHEN YOU HAVE BEEN FIRED." / "NOT THAT GOOD." A family's book is an argument carried on across generations, and the reader can see it. Pathwell closing the book and setting it back square to the edge of the shelf, then "It was my fault" with "No speech followed," is the book's best restraint. The ending is also the right structural choice: Elizabeth does not decide for him. "Do you want to come?" / "Yes." completes the thread from Chapter 7 ("Whose decision was that?") and Chapter 10 ("You don't get to make me the part of this you explain around").

"I kept thinking resemblance was enough." / "It was my fault." (Ch. 18). "She had asked him to bring her here. That didn't make the next step his." (Ch. 18). "Do you want to come?" / "Yes." (Ch. 18). "Are you ready?" / "No." / "Perfect." (Ch. 18).
- Craft notes: The Merritt cookbook and the choice handed back to Pathwell are the book's two best endings. The bookend to Chapter 1 is the joke I would cut, because the reader has already been told the book is going somewhere.
