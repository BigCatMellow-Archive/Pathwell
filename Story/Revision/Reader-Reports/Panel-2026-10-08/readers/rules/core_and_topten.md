# Rules reader: core answers and top ten (saved by the orchestrator from the reader's reply; the reader's harness refused the write)

## Core

1. **What is it about?** A man who sells his own future to get things done, and the people around him who pay for it without agreeing to the price. Magic here is a ledger: every working spends something, and the spending lands on whoever is nearby, including a woman who came in to get her life back. The book is about who sets the price, who lives with the result, and whether saying sorry changes anything.

2. **Best moment: Ch 14, the rejoining frame.** The rule that a working does what you mean and not what you wish is shown with mechanics and stakes on the page. Mama Baga's line, "You are giving it two incompatible answers," is the clearest rule statement in the book, and Elizabeth standing there with a dagger she doesn't use is the scene's real tension.

3. **Worst moment: Ch 11, the museum.** The destruction is spectacle. A list of objects, a plane, the loose charge, and collateral that nobody chose. The cost was already clear from Ch 10, so the scene adds damage, not decision. I lost track of who was where, and Shade's grabbing her arm reads as convenient.

4. **Where I got confused or stopped believing.** The flat was "marked" in Ch 1, but by Ch 6 and 7 the creature wants Pathwell, and the book never chooses between them. Ch 4's archivist counts two pages and Ch 12's card says one. Ch 5 says the creature is "unpleasant rather than murderous" unless something blocks it, and then Ch 15 takes Shade while he is walking away. Pathwell says "It's Saturday" and "I don't lie" in the same chapter.

5. **What I wanted and didn't get.** The client in Ch 10 who asked for the prune. Whether Elizabeth has any power of her own. Ch 1 has "You really don't know?" and nothing follows. What the tome bought in Ch 3, and what Shade became. Whether the cookbook's weight means anything.

6. **One thing I'd tell the author.** Answer one of the big withheld questions on the page, the client or what "You really don't know?" meant in Ch 1. The rules are good because the costs are real. The unanswered questions read as withholding, not mystery, and the reader stops trusting the rest.
