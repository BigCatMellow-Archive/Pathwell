# Core questions (six chapters read: 1, 4, 9, 12, 15, 18)

## 1. What is this book about?

It is about what we hand over, and what it costs, when the things that hold us (a grandmother's cookbook, a diary, a face you share with someone else) turn out to be useful to other people. A woman who has just moved to a new city learns that her inherited recipes can heal, and she keeps having to decide what she will give up and what she will carry. Under that is a question about being "enough": being someone's copy, someone's leverage, someone's loose end, and getting to be yourself anyway. It is also, in tone, a book about being looked after by strangers who are rude to you.

## 2. The moment that worked best

Chapter 4, the chicken-and-dumplings scene, from "Did she make anything when someone was sick?" through "That was the choice." Elizabeth finds the page, remembers herself at seventeen with the flu ("furious at being sick and more furious that Nana had been right about it"), and then makes herself hand over the whole book because she knows she will otherwise argue herself out of it. It worked because she is stopped by her own feelings and not by an outside rule, because the grandmother is in the details (the grease mark, "MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH."), and because the magic is quiet: "No flash. No burst of gold." and "Not burned. Spent." Close second: the last page of Chapter 15, ending "Shade did not either."

## 3. The moment that worked least

Chapter 1's opening action. The man going through her boxes and reading her diary, the sniffing, the door bowing, the "Don't think. What's your favorite recipe?" sequence, and the sentences that stumble through it ("He placed his hand flat on the page, the letters glowed a soft gold.", "Her heart skipped and without thinking, she jumped into her shoes, and she threw on a coat."). It is the first thing I read, and it is the roughest prose in the six chapters, by a distance. The cake and the banner at the top of the chapter are lovely. The fight that follows is blurry and hurried, and it does not sound like the book that Chapter 4 turns out to be. Runner-up: Chapter 4's fall in the woods ("The taste of dirt kissed her lips.").

## 4. Where I got confused or stopped believing it

- Chapter 1: she wakes, finds a stranger reading her diary, and within about fifteen lines is told her house is "marked, magically speaking," and puts on shoes. She does ask for an explanation, three times, and I believed the first two. By "Do you have shoes?" I stopped, because he is joking and she is complying and neither the fear nor the confusion has had time.
- Chapter 4, the wheel exchange ("This wheel is new." / "Newer," Pathwell amended): there is no line before it that "Newer" corrects. I think something is missing. I don't know.
- Chapters 9, 12 and 15 lean on things I never saw (the museum, the graffiti threshold, "Daniel Vale's letters," Thumper's paw print, the prune, the brass frame, "At the bar"). I don't count that against the book, since I read six of eighteen, but I could not follow why Shade and Pathwell share a face and a pull, and I can only say I didn't understand it. I don't know whether the other chapters explain it.
- Everyone in the later chapters talks the same way. When the healer, the archivist, Mama Baga and the shopkeeper all answer with "That is different." / "It is also extremely common." I stop hearing people and start hearing a house style (see lens_output.md, (b)).
- Chapter 12: the archivist accepts the diary gift in almost the same ritual as the cookbook in Chapter 4. I believed it as a mirror, and I did not believe it as something that happened to a different person on a different night.

## 5. What I wanted that I didn't get

- More food. The cover line promised a grandmother's cookbook. I got two recipes (sugar cookies, chicken and dumplings), both described mainly by what they feel like, and no cooking. The best food writing in the six chapters is the diner (burnt bacon "exactly how she'd have made it", Ch9) and the Merritt notes (Ch18). I wanted Elizabeth cooking something, with her hands.
- Elizabeth's own want. She wants to give things away, to follow Pathwell, to see the camp. I could not tell what she wants for herself, other than that she "hadn't thought about [her meeting] in an hour" (Ch4). Her job, her new apartment and the party are strong at the start and then barely return, apart from "Work would still contain email" (Ch18).
- Shade to have more than one chapter of his own. He is the most alive person in these six chapters, and in Chapter 15 he dies to a joke ("Corrupt institution.") and a clever line, and I felt the loss mostly because the last line did it for me.
- A scene where somebody is wrong in a way that costs something. Everyone in the later chapters is wise, dry and kind. The one real villain I see is Pathwell's past, which is told to me by other people.
- A reason the grandmother's voice is in the cookbook ("Because it was hers," Ch1) that goes beyond the rule.

## 6. One thing I'd tell the author

Let the characters stop being clever for a page. The jokes are good, and the book keeps making the same one, in the same two beats, with every mouth in turn; at the end of Chapter 18 I had a stack of deadpan retorts and could not tell you the shopkeeper from the archivist. Give each of them a way of speaking they would not share, and keep the dry reply for the two or three people who have earned it.
