# Reader report: Chapters 1, 4, 9, 12, 15, 18

I opened only the brief and the six chapter files in the text folder. Counts below come from my log and from searching the files for the words quoted. Where my log miscounted (the sock gag, "Probably."), the count here is the correct one and the log entry stands as written.

I read six chapters out of an eighteen-chapter book. Anything that refers to events outside these six I mark "I don't know."

---

## (a) Repetitions

I'd cut these first, roughly in order of how much they cost me.

**1. The "looked" tag. About 130 uses of "looked" in about 14,800 words.**
Per chapter: Ch1 3, Ch4 30, Ch9 21, Ch12 25, Ch15 27, Ch18 27. The structure "[Name] looked at/toward/down/up/back [at X]" accounts for about 112 of them. By chapter: 3, 24, 18, 23, 23, 21. This is how nearly every beat of reaction is delivered, and it is the main reason chapters 9 through 18 start to sound like one chapter. Examples:
- Ch9: "Shade looked at her in the mirror." / "Shade glanced at her again." / "Shade looked out the window." / "Shade looked down at the coffee." / "Shade looked up." / "Shade looked toward the window again."
- Ch15: "He looked at the blob, then at Pathwell, then at the loose pale threads in the dirt."
- Ch18: "Elizabeth looked at him." / "Elizabeth looked at Pathwell." / "Elizabeth looked at the shopkeeper." / "Elizabeth looked back at Pathwell."

Related: "[Name] waited." as a one-line paragraph. Ch4 x3, Ch9 x2, Ch12 x6, Ch15 x1, Ch18 x1. In Ch12 alone: "Mama Baga waited. Shade waited." / "The healer waited." / "The archivist waited." / "The boy waited." Also "nodded" 22 times, 11 of them in Ch4.

**2. The deadpan two-beat exchange. 30+ times across Ch4, 9, 12, 15, 18.**
Someone says a flat thing, the other answers with a dry correction or a one-word label. The best ones land. The shape has no variation, though, and every character uses it.
- Ch4: "'Everything is new when you are twelve.' / 'I'm eleven.' / 'That explains the filing.'" / "'Specificity is expensive.'" / "'That does not make you less annoying.' / 'It makes me correct.'"
- Ch9: "'You ask efficient questions.' / 'I've had an inefficient night.'" / "'Would you prefer applause?'" / "'That's an extremely low bar.' / 'Still a bar.'" / "'Convenient.' / 'Accurate.'"
- Ch12: "'Excellent.' ... 'Strong sales pitch.'" / "'Define using.'" / "'Important distinction.'" / "'Very official.' / ... 'That's bleak.'"
- Ch15: "'Pathwell stupid?' ... 'Worse. Mine.'" / "'Unfortunately attached.'" / "'Corrupt institution.'"
- Ch18: "'Cruel.' / 'Accounting.'" / "'Coward,' Elizabeth said. / 'Evidence-based decision making.'" / "'You are sitting spiritually.'" / "'Occupationally.'" / "'They have learned commerce.'" Ch18 has at least eight in 2,200 words.

**3. Stock replies that reappear as the same line.**
- "I know." as a standalone reply (and "I am aware." / "I said I know."): Ch4 x5, Ch9 x1, Ch12 x1, Ch15 x6. In Ch15 it is used once with real force ("'I know.' / The answer came out in Shade's voice and Pathwell's voice and her own.") and then five more times in the next scenes, mostly with Stansbury.
- "Probably." as a one-word answer or close to it: Ch9 x2 ("'Because of a prune.' / 'Probably.' / 'Probably?'"), Ch12 ("'You were going to.' / 'Probably.'"), Ch15 ("'More unpleasant?' / 'Probably.'"); narrated "Probably." in Ch9 ("Pathwell would have done that. Probably.") and "The shoulder was still in place, probably." in Ch15.
- "Good." as one-word reply: Ch1 x1, Ch9 x2, Ch12 x2, Ch15 x3, Ch18 x1. Ch12's healer ("'Good.' / 'I disagree with your use of the word.'") and Ch9's Shade ("'Bad?' 'Impressively.' 'Good.'") use it with a purpose. The others are filler.
- "Fair." / "Fair enough.": Ch9 x2, Ch18 x1. "Accurate.": Ch9, Ch15. "Of course." / "Of course he is.": Ch4, 9, 12, 18 (about 6). "Important distinction.": Ch12 and Ch18, word for word.
- "You were going to." Ch4 (the boy and the archivist) and Ch12 (healer), then the same machinery in Ch9 ("Don't start doing that.").

**4. The sock gag. 8 uses in three chapters.**
- Ch4: "One sock had surrendered halfway down his calf." and later "one sock still down."
- Ch12: "One sock had surrendered halfway down his calf." (the sentence copied exactly), then "The child with the failed sock", then "His sock had fallen again."
- Ch15: "His sock had finally stayed up. Elizabeth hated that detail immediately." / "one sock still somehow present on the left" / "one bare foot and one sock."

The Ch4 version made me smile, and the Ch15 payoff is clever. The Ch12 repeat, including the verbatim sentence, is the one that tipped it for me. I would cut the Ch12 uses and keep Ch4 and Ch15. Its partner, the boy who "disappeared" after delivering his line (Ch4: "The boy nodded solemnly and disappeared." / Ch12: "The boy disappeared between shelves."), is thinner, and "disappear/disappeared" is overused anyway (11 in five chapters, see (d)).

**5. The hand held out, then lowered.**
- Ch4: "Elizabeth's hand was still halfway raised. She lowered it."
- Ch12: "Elizabeth's hand stayed suspended after the diary left it. Empty. She lowered it."
- Ch15: "Elizabeth stood with her right hand empty."
- Ch18: "He looked at his empty hand." (Pathwell)

I think this is the intended one, and it works because it moves from person to person. It is also the same donation scene twice. The Ch12 diary scene replays the Ch4 cookbook scene beat for beat: "Family name?" / "She gave it." (verbatim both times), the archivist's forms, the two-handed receiving ("the way Mama Baga had taken the cookbook"), and the rack versus the reserve shelf. As an echo it is deliberate, and it is a lot of the same page.

**6. "That was the choice." The line, used twice, plus its neighbors.**
- Ch4: "That was the choice. / Everything afterward was consequence."
- Ch15: "That was the choice. / Everything else burned afterward."
- Ch15 then restates it: "She didn't regret the order."
This is a wink at Ch4, but Ch15 already did the work in the action. I would cut the second sentence of Ch15's version, or the final line.

**7. Fragment pairs: "Not X. Y." Seven or more uses.**
Ch4: "Not burned. Spent." / "Not hidden. Not displayed. Available." / "Not twelve. Seventeen, maybe." / "Not because he had her things. He didn't." Ch12: "Not gone. Different." / "Still being used. Still itself." Ch15: "Not everything. Something." And the list variant: Ch15 "No spell. / No sacrifice. / No last message. / Just fire." Used sparingly this is a good tool. Four in Ch4 is too many for one chapter. "Not burned. Spent." and the Ch15 "No spell" run are the two that earn it.

**8. Endings.**
- Chapter endings: Ch1 "'Ready?' / 'No.' / 'Perfect.'" Ch4 "Behind her, Camp Cunnan kept being a place without asking permission." Ch9 "She had a question now, and this time she intended to make him answer it." Ch12 "...and watched Camp wake up." Ch15 "The draw did not return. / Shade did not either." Ch18 "'Are you ready?' / 'No.' / 'Perfect.'"
- Ch1 and Ch18 end on the same three lines. That bookend is a decision, and I noticed it as a decision, so it passes. But "Perfect." was already the man's reply earlier in Ch1 ("'My Nana always made the best sugar cookies?' / 'Perfect.'"), so by the final page it has been used three times.
- Section ends within chapters are mostly a three-to-six-word flat declarative: Ch4 "Pathwell stayed by the wheel." / "Elizabeth followed." / Ch12 "Elizabeth drank." / "Elizabeth stood." / "The healer nodded." / Ch15 "She got Milo through the door." / "The blob had ignored all of it." / "She didn't regret the order." That is 12 or so. The rhythm is: a long paragraph of detail, then a short flat sentence to close. By Ch12 I could predict the close of each section.
- Ch15's last line is the exception. "Shade did not either" makes the sentence shape itself carry the loss.

**9. Smaller repeats.**
- "Mama Baga's mouth moved at one corner." Ch4 and Ch12, identical, each followed by a one-word instruction ("I will take it." / "Drink.").
- "as if / as though": about 11 across Ch4, 9, 12, 15, 18. The best are earned ("as if something outside had asked a question only his wrist could hear," Ch9; "as though museums were a recognized injury category," Ch12). The Ch4 ones ("as if presenting them to each other," "as though he were twelve and badly assembled," "as though this explained the man") crowd one chapter.
- "despite herself / before she meant to / faster than she meant to": Elizabeth smiles against her will. Ch4 x1, Ch15 x1, Ch18 x4 ("Elizabeth smiled despite herself." / "Elizabeth leaned closer despite herself." / "Elizabeth smiled before she meant to.").
- Catalogues of what is absent: Ch18 has three within 110 lines. "No mission, no instructions, no suggestion that she bring a coat, knife, heirloom, charged record, backup plan, or emotional support dumpling." / "their complete absence of blood, monsters, stolen property, handcarts, collapsed practitioners, or visibly urgent Camp orders" / "no debt, no Camp order, no blob, no one waiting at the other end". Each reminds us nothing is wrong; the third time I didn't need telling.
- "emotional weather ... breaking loose" / "emotional fallout kept breaking loose in fragments": two sentences, one chapter (Ch15).
- Shade's right hand opening and closing: about 6 in Ch9 and again in Ch12 and Ch15. A motif, deliberate, and it pays off in Ch15 ("Shade's right hand was still open toward him. Shade closed it himself."). I'd keep it. I'd cut the matching "His jaw tightened" (Ch9 x2) and the "Shade's [body part] [verbed]" tags around it ("Shade's hand stopped on the cup." / "Shade's fork stopped." / "Shade's fingers tightened around the cup.").
- Low tide: Ch1 x2, Ch15 x3. A smell motif; fine.

## (b) Voices

**Where characters sound alike.**
By Ch9 and increasingly by Ch12 to Ch18, almost everyone speaks in the same register: no contractions, short declaratives, a formal word where a casual one would do, and a dry correction at the end. I could swap speaker tags among these and not notice:
- Mama Baga, Ch4: "That is not a posture." / "I will take it." / "That does not make you less annoying."
- The archivist, Ch4: "Specificity is expensive." / "That is generally the opposite of an archive." Ch12: "He respects paperwork more consistently than people." / "It is also why we keep paperwork."
- The healer, Ch12: "You are not using this arm today." / "If you are about to ask whether carrying something counts, yes."
- Stansbury, Ch15: "I am aware." / "Different problem."
- The shopkeeper, Ch18: "I dislike novelty." / "Three weeks is not a character trait." / "It is also extremely common." / "Accounting."
- Shade, Ch9: "I did not know you existed." / "Don't make that sentence larger than it is." Ch15: "You really have learned nothing in the last six minutes."
- Pathwell, Ch18: "I am capable of growth." / "I was not going to." / "That is a surprisingly responsible version of the question."

Compare one line from each: "That is not a posture." (Mama Baga), "That is different." (Mama Baga), "It is also extremely common." (shopkeeper), "Do not spread that around." (Pathwell). I could not tell you from the sentence alone who said them. The shopkeeper and the archivist are close to interchangeable.

Elizabeth also picks up the register. Her best lines are hers ("Try some nouns." / "You people are committed to making trees unpleasant." / "Define using."), but by Ch12 and Ch18 her replies ("Excellent." / "Strong sales pitch." / "Important distinction." / "That's bleak.") run in the same two-beat groove as everyone else's.

**Who sounds most like themselves.**
- Pathwell in Ch4, performing: "Welcome, Elizabeth, to Camp Cunnan, home of the Cunning Folk. Dwelling of charmers, blessers, wizards and witches. Abode of the spaewives and the gwyllion." / "Did I say runemasters?" / "Home is such a permanent term. I prefer… layover." / "Elizabeth here would be figuratively, and quite literally, lost without you." This is a man who can't stop adding to a sentence. In Ch1 he does the same thing under pressure: "Understandable. But your house has now been marked, magically speaking." In Ch18 that voice is gone, which is the point, but also why Ch18 Pathwell reads like the shopkeeper.
- The children. Ch4: "'It's dead,' one said. / 'It's practicing,' said the other. / 'For what?' / The second child considered this. 'Being dead.'" And "'It wasn't me.' / 'No one accused you.' / 'You were going to.'" Ch15 Milo: "Pathwell stupid?" / "You still owe me a hand." / "You were not." Milo is the only speaker whose dialogue the adults' rhythm doesn't touch.
- Nana, on the page, with no narrator: "MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH." (Ch4). Ch18's Merritt notes are even better: "RUTH LIED ABOUT THE LARD." / "SHE DID NOT. YOU USED THE WRONG CUP." / "ALSO GOOD WHEN YOU HAVE BEEN FIRED." / "NOT THAT GOOD." Three different hands, each one different from the others. They sound more like separate people than the living characters do.
- Shade, Ch9 only, in the diner. "A room in Prague I have never entered. A woman named Celia who hated pears. Three ways to cheat at a card game I have never played." and "For him to stop deciding what I am before I get to." He has a voice because he is hesitating and correcting himself ("We used to— / He stopped. / 'He used to,' he corrected.").
- Elizabeth in Ch1, before she picks up the style: "My Nana always made the best sugar cookies?" and "They're… in a box in the kitchen?" Frightened, rising at the end of the sentence, not clever. I wanted more of it.

## (c) Paragraphs

### The three worst (worst first)

**Worst 1. Chapter 4.**

> She made her way around a large oak, and her foot caught in the undergrowth. She wiggled it free and took one step forward, just enough for her foot to meet a tree root, followed by another greeting with the ground. The taste of dirt kissed her lips.

Why: this is the one paragraph in the six chapters where the prose reaches for a metaphor and misses. "Another greeting with the ground" is a euphemism for falling, and "the taste of dirt kissed her lips" is the kind of line I strike in a margin. It's also the only romantic image in a book whose other jokes are dry. The surrounding Ch4 prose ("repaired by someone who believed replacement was a personal failure") is far more exact. I'd cut the paragraph down to "She caught her foot, wiggled it free, took one step, and found the ground."

**Worst 2. Chapter 1.**

> The door began to crack and splinter, an ooze searched through the gaps. Bringing a stench of low tide that churned her stomach.

Why: a comma splice ("crack and splinter, an ooze searched") followed by a sentence fragment that hangs off nothing ("Bringing a stench..."). "Churned her stomach" is a stock phrase; the chapter has already made the smell do that job twice ("Low tide, a long way from any sea."). Two sentences, each mis-built. Nearby: "He placed his hand flat on the page, the letters glowed a soft gold." and "Her heart skipped and without thinking, she jumped into her shoes, and she threw on a coat." Ch1 has more slips like this than the other five chapters combined; it's the roughest chapter.

**Worst 3. Chapter 18.**

> The shopkeeper looked up from his ledger when she entered, took in Elizabeth, Pathwell behind her, their complete absence of blood, monsters, stolen property, handcarts, collapsed practitioners, or visibly urgent Camp orders, and frowned.

Why: one sentence carrying a six-item list inside a sentence that already lists three things he does (looked up, took in, frowned). It leans on events from other chapters ("stolen property, handcarts, collapsed practitioners"); I recognize the handcart from Ch4 but not the others, and the joke only works if you do. It also doubles the catalogue-of-absences joke from forty lines earlier ("emotional support dumpling") and again at line 418 ("no debt, no Camp order, no blob..."). Same joke, three tellings. The gag is "he frowns because nothing is wrong," and the sentence buries that under the inventory.

(Runners-up I didn't count: Ch12's "The book belonged here now, and it no longer felt like a disappearance," which states the emotional point after the scene already showed it, and Ch18's "Pathwell's old answer almost arrived. She saw it. Then he smiled.")

### The three best

**Best 1. Chapter 1.**

> The party was still out here. Paper cups on every flat surface, a tray of cheese cubes going shiny, and half a sheet cake that now said only ELIZ, because her coworkers had started at the wrong end. A WELCOME ELIZABETH banner hung by one corner over the kitchen doorway. It had been a good party. Better than she'd expected for someone so new to the building that she still couldn't find the light switch in the hall. The guests had left hours ago, and she'd gone straight to bed. She had a meeting at nine, her first big one since she'd started.

Why: every sentence gives me something to see or something about her. "Cheese cubes going shiny," the cake saying ELIZ, the banner hanging from one corner, the light switch she can't find. The party tells me she's new, alone, and trying. And it sets up the cake that survives later ("The cake, somehow, was fine."), which is the best joke in the chapter and pays off a detail I'd already filed. The "meeting at nine" returns in Ch4 ("her meeting was still at nine. She noticed she hadn't thought about it in an hour"), also well used.

**Best 2. Chapter 9.**

> Shade drove with both hands on the wheel. Almost. Every few miles his right hand loosened and opened a little toward the driver's-side window, palm turning as if something outside had asked a question only his wrist could hear. Each time, he caught it. Sometimes he closed it against the wheel. Once he shoved it under his thigh.

Why: the one-word "Almost." makes the reader do a double take, the image of a hand answering a question "only his wrist could hear" is particular and a little eerie, and the paragraph escalates from catching the hand to closing it to shoving it under his thigh. It shows us the pull before any character explains it. Shorter and more precise than the explanation in the diner that follows.

**Best 3. Chapter 4.**

> A different memory this time. Not twelve. Seventeen, maybe, sitting at Nana's table with the flu, furious at being sick and more furious that Nana had been right about it. A bowl between both hands. Steam in her face. Nana pretending not to watch until Elizabeth took the first bite.

Why: this is the memory-magic scene at its most concrete: "furious at being sick and more furious that Nana had been right about it" is a true teenager, and "Nana pretending not to watch until Elizabeth took the first bite" gives me the grandmother in six words without a description of her. It follows the page note "MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH." and the fragments here ("Not twelve. Seventeen, maybe.") are doing real work: they show her correcting her own memory. The fragment pair that I list as overused in (a) is at its best here.

## (d) Bans

I'd ban these, in this order:

1. **"[Name] looked at [Name]" as a reaction beat.** About 112 of the 130-odd "looked" in the six chapters. If the author must keep a looking beat, keep it for Ch15's "Shade looked at Pathwell, and Pathwell was looking back. Shade wasn't asking for anything, or forgiving anything. He just looked." where it's the point. Everywhere else, give the glance something to see or cut it.
2. **"Probably." as a standalone answer or aside.** 6 uses (Ch9 x3, Ch12 x1, Ch15 x2), plus Ch1's "We should probably get going" x2 and "We should probably be anywhere but here." (Ch1 is a character tic; the later ones are the author's.) Keep Ch9's "Pathwell would have done that. Probably."
3. **"I know." as a standalone line.** About 13 (Ch4 x5, Ch9 x1, Ch12 x1, Ch15 x6). Keep Ch15's first one.
4. **"Good." as a one-word reply.** 9 (Ch1 x1, Ch9 x2, Ch12 x2, Ch15 x3, Ch18 x1). Keep the healer's in Ch12 ("I disagree with your use of the word.").
5. **"[Name] waited." as its own paragraph.** About 14 uses of "waited" in total, 6 in Ch12.
6. **"disappeared / disappear".** 11 uses in Ch4 x2, Ch12 x4, Ch15 x5. It is a flat verb, especially at "The low-tide smell disappeared into the trees" and "Her own ordinary handwriting disappeared without being used for anything" (the second is fine; the others add nothing).
7. **"Fair." / "Fair enough." and "Accurate."** 3 and 2 uses. Small, and they're the same move as the deadpan exchange in (a).
8. **The sock.** 8 uses. Keep Ch4's first mention and Ch15's "His sock had finally stayed up. Elizabeth hated that detail immediately." Cut the rest.
9. **"Nobody ... / No one ..." lists of absences** (Ch18, three sentences). Keep one.

I'm not banning "low tide" (a smell motif that comes back deliberately) or Shade's hand (the book's best repeated image).

## What I'd praise (after the cuts)

- Concrete, odd detail in the camp and archive: the notebook with "DO NOT TRUST THE SOUTH BANK AFTER THREE DAYS OF RAIN, NO MATTER WHAT HESS SAYS" and "Hess has been dead twenty-two years." / "He'd still argue." (Ch4); the geese that "have learned commerce" (Ch18).
- The handwritten notes in the cookbooks. They carry more voice than the dialogue does.
- Ch15's last page, where the prose goes plain and short at the right moment.
- The cake.

- Whether the Ch4 line "'This wheel is new.' / 'Newer,' Pathwell amended" has a missing line of Elizabeth's before it. As it stands, "Newer" corrects nothing I can see. Ch4, near the end of the cart scene.
- Whether the Ch15 line "That hand was promising." refers to a card game I haven't seen. It sits right after "Shade saying he didn't know yet what else he liked," and I couldn't place it.
