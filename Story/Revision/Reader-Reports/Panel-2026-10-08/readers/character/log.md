# Reading log: Shade

## Chapter 8
First appearance, in the last third of the chapter. Elizabeth crashes the Cadillac on purpose, then a dark sedan pulls up and a man who looks like Pathwell gets out: "He was the same height, and near enough the same face that the distance swallowed the differences." Gives his name as "Shade", then "First name?" / "Enough name."

Wants: to be rid of a pull toward Pathwell. "Something keeps telling me which way you are... I can ignore it for a while. Then it gets worse." He drove toward it, "Long enough to be tired of it." Also wants coffee, which is the first thing he says that I believed completely.
Does: keeps his right hand from reaching toward Pathwell (catches his own wrist, pockets it), refuses to let Pathwell near ("Don't."), and refuses him a ride ("Not in my car. Not you."). Leaves Elizabeth free to get in, doesn't recruit her.
Says: short, flat, dry. "Keep them." "Pick a word." "I know old versions of your excuses." Admits "I remember things I did not live" and "I don't know your current life."
View now: I like him. The hand fighting him is a good physical tell and it's shown, not explained. He's the quiet counterweight to Pathwell, and the "Same ingredients. Different recipe." line from Elizabeth is a bit on the nose, but his behavior backs it up. Mild worry: he's already doing the "mysterious man who knows pieces" thing, and I've been burned by that one being used to feed exposition later. Reserving judgment.

## Chapter 9
Driving to a diner, then the booth. This is Shade's best chapter so far.

Wants: "For him to stop deciding what I am before I get to." Also wants Pathwell made to answer for something, but through Elizabeth, not himself: "Because he'll listen to you longer than he'll listen to me." He admits she's "Partly" leverage and doesn't dress it up.
Does: drives with both hands "Almost"; taps the wheel twice before every turn; sings along to the radio and stops when he hears her; offers the front seat once and drops it; pays for both, "counted out the tip with irritating precision"; lets her steal his toast; does not offer to drive her back. Resists the hand: "No," he said quietly. "It was for the pull itself."
Says: "Observation. Not memory." (when he notices she looks less tired after the bad coffee, and flags that it isn't one of Pathwell's memories). "You ask efficient questions." "Would you prefer applause?" "You were never the point... Don't make that sentence larger than it is." On what he remembers of Pathwell: "That he had reached the point where being uncertain no longer mattered to him." Slips into "We used to—" and corrects to "He used to."
Memory fragments: "A room in Prague I have never entered. A woman named Celia who hated pears. Three ways to cheat at a card game I have never played." Good, specific, and "A staircase with a loose fourth step" is the one that got me.
View now: sold. He's careful about the line between what he knows and what he was handed, and that care is the character. The "met me on the road twenty-five minutes ago" bit is funny the first time and Elizabeth tells him to stop the second, which is the right move. I'd cut nothing here. One quibble: he hands Elizabeth a lot of Pathwell's backstory ("A problem he had decided belonged to him") that's a little tidy as exposition, but it's framed as fragments so I let it pass.

## Chapter 11
Museum in the early morning; Shade is waiting in the lot by his sedan. Pathwell has a key; they tour the cases; Shade needs the graffiti wall to open Camp; Pathwell tries to "separate the problem" after Shade stumbles and grabs Elizabeth's arm; the working wrecks the gallery and breaks Elizabeth's shoulder. Shade goes through to Camp first, Elizabeth follows.

Wants: "I'm trying to get somewhere this stops being the only direction my body cares about." When Elizabeth says Camp, "Something like recognition passed over Shade's face. 'Yes,' he said. 'Camp.'" So his goal gets set here, by her. I noticed the swap: he came to find Pathwell in ch. 8, and in ch. 9 his stated want was for Pathwell to stop deciding what he is. Now it's Camp, and he agrees on the spot.
Does: stands with both hands in his pockets, fighting the right one. Tells Pathwell to keep his distance. Steps on the squeaky floorboard on purpose ("Shade stepped on it on purpose, and the sound cracked through the quiet. Pathwell looked back."), which I liked: petty and exactly right. Recognizes the Daniel Vale letters ("He complained about socks."). Asks Elizabeth, not Pathwell, for permission to use the wall: "I need the wall." Stumbles when the pull yanks him, grabs her arm, lets go at once: "Sorry." Gets thrown into the barrier, bleeds from one palm. Afterwards says only "You said all right." and "Do you?" (about the destroyed letter). Opens the wall when Pathwell finally says where the mark is. Goes through first because she tells him to.
Says: "She wasn't yours to take from." "That makes it Pathwell." "You don't know what happens if I eat breakfast either." (Pathwell's "Breakfast has a substantial experimental record" is the setup, and Shade's "For you." is the better half.) "Careful with that excuse."
View now: still with him. The best Shade beat is not a line, it's that he never accuses Pathwell of the grab and never explains himself. "Sorry" and nothing else. Two issues. (1) Here the plot needs him to be the cause, and it does that by making the pull knock him over; that's the hand being used as a plot lever, and I'd rather he'd chosen something. He's a passenger in the disaster. (2) He seems to have no feelings about his own injury or about Elizabeth's shoulder on the page. He looks at her first, which is something, but I want one line from him. The chapter belongs to Pathwell and Elizabeth and Shade is the occasion for it.

## Chapter 12
Camp Cunnan. The healer sets Elizabeth's shoulder, Mama Baga feeds them, Elizabeth gives her diary to the archive. Shade is mostly furniture in this chapter, by the wagon door or the fire.

Wants: nothing stated. He wants to be where she is and not be in the way: "Shade remained by the wagon door, in a spot where he wasn't blocking anything." When asked if he's staying he looks at Elizabeth, not the healer, and she says "You can."
Does: watches the bucket ("Efficient system." / "Diversifying."); reaches toward her, stops before touching; waits with everyone else when she climbs the steps rather than helping; keeps old Pathwell knowledge out of the healer's work (the narrator tells me this, I didn't see it); lets himself be bandaged; drops his spoon in the stew; doesn't ask what she did with the diary.
Says: "Not especially." / "Hand." (to Mama Baga's "You hurt?" / "That was not the question."). "This is humiliating." "Say nothing." "You're preparing to." And when the boy asks "Are you Pathwell?": "No." Then, after: "I don't know what that means."
View now: warm and a bit thin. I like "This is humiliating" and the spoon, they make him human. But this chapter is where a quiet character risks becoming a prop, and I'd say he's close. He has one interior beat (the boy's "Good") and no reaction to it beyond puzzlement; I wanted him to mind it. Also, everyone in Camp has the same dry cadence (the healer's "I disagree with your use of the word" style comebacks, Elizabeth's, his "Diversifying."). His joke here is more like theirs than like the Shade of the diner. Small thing, but I noticed.
Anything I'd cut: the narrator's explaining sentence about what he "kept out of a treatment". Show me or don't.

## Chapter 13
The best Shade chapter. Mama Baga makes him change coats; he makes terrible coffee; plays Hearts with Milo (the sock boy), the archivist and an older woman; carries onions; the pull gets stronger and Pathwell opens a threshold from the museum side.

Wants: for the first time he says it out loud. Mama Baga asks "You want to leave?" He looks at the trees, the card table, the onions: "No." / "I want to stay here." Smaller wants before that: coffee, Hearts ("Again?" / "Yes."), and, as his joke answer to "What else do you like?": "Pockets." Then "I don't know yet."
Does: takes off the museum coat and puts it in the wash basket, picks a green canvas jacket with "too many pockets" over the coat he'd "preferred" (admits it: "No. I didn't."). Makes coffee with knowledge that isn't his and distrusts it ("He would start a motion, pause, and go on only after looking at what his hands were actually doing."). Plays badly, then plays the people instead of the rules. Pockets the queen of spades as "Evidence", then gives it back to Milo ("You'll need this."). Teases Milo about his purple foot. Carries onions. Gets the escaped onion with the hand fighting him "the entire way."
Says: "I have inherited worse problems." (then catches it: "Sorry... Making everything about that."). "I can choose badly." "My face is adjusting." "Corruption." / "Exactly. No one suspects him." On the invitation: "People keep doing that." "Asking." "Disorienting." On the pull: "Stronger." / "Probably." To Elizabeth about Pathwell: "He keeps acting like you're something he can hold onto." Last line, as the threshold opens and the cards are on the ground: "That hand was promising."
Moment that got me: Elizabeth says the coffee is terrible and he says "I know," and she stops, because Pathwell said exactly that to her. He says "Then I don't remember it." and later "He was right once." That's the whole character in four lines: he's not Pathwell and can't stop sounding like him, and he knows.
View now: this is the Shade I'd follow. "People keep doing that. ... Asking." is the line that answers the want from ch. 9, and the book earns it by having four different people in Camp ask him what he wants without telling him what he is (Mama Baga, the healer, Milo, the archivist). Cut: Milo being a precocious card shark and the sock gag are both doing the same job as the earlier turnip joke, i.e. the cute child who sees through everyone. I'd keep one. Also "bandaged left hand" is the first time I've been told which hand the cut is on and I'd forgotten it was a palm; not important.

## Chapter 14
Pathwell and Stansbury come through from the museum. Pathwell lays out brass pins and a loop. Shade recognizes it as a rejoining working that would fold him back into Pathwell. He refuses. Pathwell does it anyway; it fails, hits Pathwell, and the archive wagon catches fire with Milo inside.

Wants: to not be rejoined. Stated four ways: "No." / "Then no." / "Then leave it unresolved." / "I am the person living with it. No." Under it, what he said in ch. 13: "I want to stay here." He also says "Nothing changing is acceptable to me" and I'll admit I read that three times and I'm not sure what it means (that he's content with the status quo? that no change is acceptable?).
Does: spots what the pins are before anyone says ("Two anchors. One return."; "You're building a rejoining frame."); asks "What happens to me?" and then "Do I remain myself?"; hears "Can." / "Not will." and says "Then no."; does not tear the frame apart but "simply stood where he was and refused"; lets his arm hang open toward Pathwell ("His choice was in the rest of him, which stayed still"); tells Elizabeth she doesn't have to stay; steps in for Stansbury ("Don't make him the reason."); when the working starts, plants his feet and lets the pull extend his arm; says "I am not fighting you." / "Then whatever happens next is yours." Last paragraph: "Shade was still alive."
Says (the ones I'll remember): "There he is." (when Pathwell says remembering the shape isn't understanding it). "You still think the result gets to decide whether the choice was acceptable." "For who?" And his reply to Milo's "You would know": "It's fine... Mostly."
View now: strongest and most frustrating chapter for him. The strongest part is "Don't make him the reason": the one man who has the least reason to care about Stansbury's pride understands exactly what it would cost him, and says it. That's a person, not a device. "For who?" is the question the book had been circling and he's the only one who can ask it.
Frustrating: (1) The long speech ("You are going to use the existing connection as the return line. You're going to put the unresolved part back against its source...") is Shade as exposition engine, which is the thing I'm tired of. The line before it, "You're building a rejoining frame," already did the work. Cut the speech. (2) His refusal is entirely passive. He's within reach of the pins the whole time, the hand is pointed at the frame, and he says "I am not fighting you" and then stands there. I understand it's a principle (nothing he does would be his choice if the pull moves him), but I don't see why he doesn't just step out of the pull's range. Maybe it can't be done and the book didn't tell me. (3) "Nothing changing is acceptable to me" is the one line in the chapter that doesn't sound like him.
Also: the fire is what the chapter is really about, and Shade is a bystander for the last third.

## Chapter 15
The archive burns. Elizabeth gets Milo out (and leaves the diary and cookbook). Stansbury burns his hand saving what he can. A blob arrives. Shade volunteers to be the thing it follows, walks away from Pathwell, and is absorbed. The blob then eats the spill and leaves. The draw is gone and so is he.

Wants: not stated as a want. What he says is "I'd like to find out which problem it thinks is biggest." and, when Elizabeth asks what the plan is: "It isn't." (a plan). Before that, "I told Pathwell to leave it unresolved. I thought I'd be the one carrying it." I take that as: he feels the fire is his to answer for, and he looks at Elizabeth's dangling arm when he says it. The book doesn't say that, though. I'm guessing.
Does: watches the blob, works out it's "been trying to decide between us because it can't read the difference cleanly"; refuses Pathwell's "Stay where you are" ("You really have learned nothing in the last six minutes."); hears Pathwell's late "Will you stay?" and answers "No."; walks away, arm dragged back toward Pathwell; blob changes course to follow; looks at the queen of spades and at Milo; closes his own right hand as the blob takes him. The blob then cleans up the spill and goes.
Says, in order: "Interesting word." / "I'm not doing anything yet." / "By separating the signals." / "Unfortunately attached." / "Well." / "I was winning." (Milo: "You were not.") / "Corrupt institution."
View now: I'm sad and I'm not sure I'm persuaded. The last ten lines are good: "Shade had never been particularly interested in deserving scenes" is exactly right, and "I was winning." / "Corrupt institution." costs him nothing and says everything. "Will you stay?" / "No." is the best exchange between the two of them in his chapters, and Pathwell not arguing back is the first time Pathwell has done what Shade asked.
What I don't buy: his theory. The blob took him and then ate the spill anyway, which is what Elizabeth said it might do ("It could just take the fresh spill." / "Yes."). So I can't tell what he saved. If he saved Milo and Camp from a blob that would otherwise have wandered through, say that; I didn't see it. I also noticed that his death is what ends the draw, removes the unclaimed spill and takes the blobs away: the plot's mess is cleaned up by him. That's the thing I get nervous about. It is also what Pathwell's rejoining was supposed to do, so Shade got a worse version of the outcome he refused.
Answer to something I wondered in ch. 14: why he didn't step out of the pull's range. Here he walks twenty feet with the arm dragging. So he could, and standing there in ch. 14 was a choice. I'd have liked to have been told sooner.
Last line: "Shade did not either." A good last line. I don't know whether he comes back; I only have his chapters.
