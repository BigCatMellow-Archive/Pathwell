# Reading log

## Chapter 1
Leaned in at the cold open: the party debris is a nice touch, "half a sheet cake that now said only ELIZ" and later "The cake, somehow, was fine." Funny and specific. The stranger reading her diary "like a magazine in a waiting room" is a good first image of him. The cookbook magic (recipe glows, she hears Nana) is the best idea so far; "Because it was hers" made me sit up. Braced when the door "bowed in its frame". Slight skim in the fight: "Sounds of struggles, pounding, and kicking. A loud wet smack" is generic. A few typos/fragment oddities ("Bringing a stench of low tide..."). Mild wariness: she "doesn't know" and the man knows, and he "went still" in the hall after checking it. That is exactly the kind of convenient quiet I distrust. Also "Do you have shoes?" is a Rivers-of-London-ish tone, fine. Not yet a chosen-one smell, but "You really don't know?" is the classic opener.

## Chapter 2
Laughed at "I wouldn't say hungry, I'm more peckish" and "Just a really weird Tuesday for you." / "It's Thursday." / "Which makes it all the weirder." Pathwell is good company on the page; the no-last-name bit ("Don't need one. Saves everybody time.") is a bit cute but it works. Leaned in at the wrong-neighbourhood moment: "Just a shuttered laundromat she'd never seen before. Beyond that, a park that shouldn't have been there." That is the Neverwhere itch. The dry-cleaning ticket skipping into the gutter is a small, good, human beat. The crying paragraph ("A life she'd packed into garbage bags ... but leaving didn't feel like anything at all") is the first line that told me who she is; I liked it, though "garbage bags" contradicts the many "boxes" - noticed, didn't mind. Skimmed the panic-list speech lightly. Ending ("She heard her again. Lizzy.") lands: he nicked her Nana's page. Small snag: he calls her "Lizzy" from the start with no explanation (diary, I assume), and "dropped her books on the table" - I only recall the one cookbook and the diary. Pathwell goes oddly breezy exactly when the information would matter; I'm watching him.

## Chapter 3
Biggest lean-in so far. The bookstore as a place made of lives ("Not books - lives. Journals with cracked spines, letters bundled in ribbon, recipes on index cards spotted with oil.") and the emotions leaking off the shelves, with the warm journal spine, is the best worldbuilding yet. The awful coffee that helps ("Familiar things are useful.") is a lovely rule. The shopkeeper's deadpan billing ("Will that be paper or plastic?" / "Surprise me.") made me laugh, and "Pathwell looked genuinely wounded" over the handcart too. Elizabeth's single laugh and "Don't make anything of that." is a nice small character beat. Braced hard at the payment scene: "Pathwell screamed in a million voices, voices lost to time, burning out to become memories never made." Strong. "If I knew that, I'd make worse decisions with more confidence." is the line I'll remember from him. Mild eye-roll at the cat bloodletting being so quickly waved off ("blood god", "We pay him in mice") - fun but a bit sitcom, and the cut + scar line barely matters afterwards. The Rai Stone / Lydian coins joke is for people who read about money; I got it, but it's a nudge-nudge. The magic pays real costs here, which is what Rivers of London never quite does; I noticed and approved. Watching: "I've got potential to sell. Lots." / "You did." sets up that Pathwell is hiding something about himself and his own price. The deferral "I can answer all your questions" turned into a cat and a transaction - he has now dodged her three times.

## Chapter 4
Long, and the best chapter so far. Where I leaned in: the chicken-and-dumplings page, "MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH." and "She believed soup was an argument you could win." That is a real person in four lines. "Not burned. Spent." is exactly right and the page vanishing line by line hurt. The archive scene is the other high: "Specificity is expensive." and "That is generally the opposite of an archive." and the dead Hess still arguing about the creek ("He'd still argue.") made me laugh. The kids ("It's practicing." / "For what?" / "Being dead.") too. Elizabeth handing over the whole book, no flourish, then her hand "still halfway raised" like holding out for change, is the first time she did something rather than being dragged.
Skimmed: Pathwell's welcome speech ("Dwelling of charmers, blessers, wizards and witches. Abode of the spaewives and the gwyllion...") - a list for the sake of a list, and he even starts over ("Did I say runemasters?"). Also the camp description in the second half of the arrival ("Everything was used and kept") ran a bit long though "someone who believed replacement was a personal failure" is a good line. 
Didn't care for: "That was the choice. Everything afterward was consequence." - the narrator telling me a moment is big; the scene had already earned it. And a dialogue glitch: "He nodded. 'This wheel is new.'" followed by "'Newer,' Pathwell amended" - looks like a line of hers went missing.
Bracing / suspicion: Pathwell goes "still" again at the tree line, and the talk with Mama Baga reveals he knows something about why the thing came ("It was already at the door before you used the cookbook? Yes."). He is withholding about something and the narrator is letting me see that he is withholding, not what. That I can live with, since I'm told it's being held. Stansbury, the older brother he's "personally offended" by, is a good hook. I noticed the book goes out of its way to say "Not because he had her things. He didn't." Her motive to follow is curiosity about why it came, not fate. Good, no chosen-one yet.

## Chapter 5
Mixed. Laughed at "I'm going to run out of ways to say 'I don't know' if you keep asking the same question" and at Elizabeth improvising "I'm the missus ... Don't be rude, honey" with the elbow. That is her best moment of agency to date and she is funny. The maroon Cadillac, the foam "noodles" that make a tree dump its rain only on the others while Stansbury "stood there dry, with the look of a man who expected them to be impressed" - good. Elizabeth drawing the line between Stansbury selling kids' leftover magic and Pathwell selling potential is a smart observation from a reader's seat, and it was not handed to her by the narrator.
Leaned in: Stansbury's "Her door." / "Was it?" - and Elizabeth declining to ask. That is the "something is being held back" signal; I note it and I'm braced for it to be about her. I'll say now that if the twist is that the thing was after her specifically because of who she is, I'll be tired.
Skimmed: the portal sequence, a full paragraph of wind, circles, "twisting and spinning", bandana singed - effects prose that I read for outcome only. Also the school-hall wandering and the badge guard Ken, which was a long runway for a joke that was mostly the missus bit. "Elementary school" gag ("How'd you know it was an elementary school?" "It was a lucky guess") is a good small one though.
Didn't care for: the narrator suddenly says "You would be forgiven for not stopping in the town as you drove through" - second person, one paragraph, never again. Odd. The Thursday/Saturday/Friday day-of-week gag is a bit muddled; I couldn't tell if Pathwell was being dim or if time works strangely, and I'd rather know. "He lies, you know" / "I don't lie" is a good plant and I'll hold both of them to it.

## Chapter 6
The bar chapter is a rest stop and a good one. Laughed at "You came to a bar. I happen to be in it." / "This is spreading." and the brothers' fish bickering ("Seventeen years," / "You complained about the batter then too." / "Consistency." "Stagnation." "Branding."), and "I tip historically." / "It has meant something in several currencies." Also "The bartender took the receipt away before the argument could become archival." The brothers' unspoken closeness (moving the beer six inches back from the edge) is the best characterisation of the pair; show-don't-tell done well. Elizabeth saying no to the appletini is the cheap but effective beat of her coming into herself. 
Leaned in at the bartender's muddler: "She could have sworn one of them came a hair before his hand did." Small, creepy, unexplained; I'd like that picked up. Stansbury's "You didn't ask for them." and "Don't confuse restraint with virtue" are good.
The mirror scene: first time the narration goes inside Pathwell. It tells me what I was waiting to be told: the pull points at him, "damage was easier than mystery," and "His own face in the mirror suddenly felt less like evidence than like an accusation." That's honest narration - I'm glad the book showed it to me and did not hold it back for later; it eases my distrust of the quiet-narrator thing. But it also shows me the narration is willing to dip in and out of heads when it suits. I'll see if it stays honest.
Didn't care for: the opening paragraph's bar description ("neater than it should have been", seats worn "from customers too inebriated to do it for themselves") - slack. "It was a decent time. She let it be one." is nice. The ending "Wet. Heavy." echoes ch. 1's thud and works as a chapter-end sting, though I'm starting to see the wet thud as a recurring shape: hide, quiet, thud.

## Chapter 7
Braced and leaned in through the parking-lot fight; it's short, clear, and the physics are readable, which I can't say of ch. 5's portal. Elizabeth cutting Pathwell out with the foam dagger ("Something opened. Not flesh. A seam.") pays off Stansbury's throwaway "cuts separation into things that don't separate easily" - good setup and payoff. "Anything missing?" / "Several dignities." made me laugh, as did "I'm familiar with the face." "Cold ran through the sleeve of her coat where it touched the black edge" - nice.
The driving scene is the point of the chapter and it is where I got irritated in a useful way. Elizabeth's speech, "I become the driver", is the best thing she has said and earned. "Drive," Pathwell said. ... "Not cruel. Worse. Useful." is a sharp line. But then: "'She's right,' ... 'Yes.' Elizabeth waited. Nothing followed." The brothers admit they should tell her and still don't. This is the thing I distrust: the characters shielding a fact from her so there is something left to reveal. Because ch. 6 already showed me Pathwell's side, I'm not being tricked as a reader, only watching two men be stubborn on her behalf. I'll give it a chapter or two before I start counting it against the book. The prompts "Not me" / "Then tell me what is different." / "I can't yet." is fine, and Pathwell actually doesn't know yet, which is why this one does not feel like a cheat.
Didn't care: "Elizabeth laughed once. Both men stopped. Nothing was funny." Third time "laughed once" in this book (ch. 3, 4, 7). Verbal tic. "Of course he did." fine. The last line, "nobody was telling Elizabeth where to go," lands, though it's hollow because she has no destination.

## Chapter 8
Best chapter yet for me, and it is Elizabeth's. Crashing the Cadillac on purpose ("Elizabeth chose the ditch.") is the first time in the book a character solves a problem by being awkward instead of clever, and the aftermath is funny without winking: "My axle is in a drainage culvert. I'm evaluating several positions." and "You broke my car." / "Yes." / "I believe you." Also "Neither did you." / "That is exactly the problem." / "No. It's one of them." Stansbury's "I really am sorry." / "I know." is oddly touching.
Leaned in, hard, at Shade stepping out of the headlights: "He was the same height, and near enough the same face that the distance swallowed the differences." The tell with the right hand ("caught his own wrist with his other hand and pushed it down") is a great physical detail. "Same ingredients. Different recipe." - worked because it came from a woman who bakes in her head. "Enough name." is good. "I know old versions of your excuses." is very good.
I will say plainly: by here I had worked out what Shade is, because chapter 3 told me Pathwell sold "potential," chapter 4's "pruned" and chapter 6's "pull" were all on the page, and a man with the same face who has memories he never lived is not hard to read. I wasn't annoyed to be ahead; the book isn't pretending otherwise ("Nobody knows enough yet"), and Pathwell's "Current memories?" shows he's at least asking the right question. I'd rather have the book be ahead of me on what it means than behind me on what it is.
Didn't care for: a stray line in the middle of the crash, "It was the crash that woke her.", repeats the book's first sentence and isn't attached to anything; if it's a deliberate echo of ch. 1 I couldn't tell what it meant, and it jolts the otherwise tight action. The ending line "Only then did she realize she was following another man who sounded certain." is a nice honest note about her habit. I braced a little that she gets in a stranger's car at night on the strength of "pieces Pathwell wouldn't give her" - plausible given she's been abducted all week, but it's a heavy lift.

## Chapter 9
Another strong one, and the one that bought the most of my goodwill. The chosen-one door is opened and shut in two lines: "Maybe the universe had picked her out, for once." / "'You were never the point,' he said. ... 'Don't make that sentence larger than it is.'" I put the book down and said "thank you" out loud. That is the single best choice in the book so far from where I sit.
Leaned in: the diner coffee ("It was terrible, and it was the Space Between's coffee all over again.") and "Observation. Not memory." - Shade making the distinction between what he sees and what he is carrying is exactly the sort of narrator-honesty I have been looking for. "Try some nouns." and then the list ("A woman named Celia who hated pears. ... A staircase with a loose fourth step.") is a very good, very specific inventory of someone else's life. "I don't want to." as the reason not to go back to the one who pulls him: simple, real. "For him to stop deciding what I am before I get to." - the emotional core of Shade in one sentence.
Laughed: "'He met me on the road twenty-five minutes ago.' / 'Don't start doing that.'" And Shade tipping "with irritating precision", and Elizabeth stealing his last toast.
Didn't care for / minor skim: the first page of road-and-radio, the singing along bit, was a bit of mood for mood's sake, though "tapped the wheel twice with one finger before every turn" is a nice tell. The "Ask him what he chose." as a quest instruction feels like the book handing her a task, and Shade's "That's his answer" is again a character who knows part and refuses the rest. It's more honest than ch. 7 because he really does only have pieces, but I'm a little tired of everyone in this book having "pieces." 
Plausibility: she walks off from a diner on a night road toward a bar she can't reach by foot, with a waitress's directions on a receipt. I buy the gesture; I don't buy the distance. I'll see.

## Chapter 10
Long, three registers, and I'd have kept nearly all of it. Leaned in at the letter: "She wrote to the same man for six years and mailed none of them. He married somebody else in year two." and then the ink "simply loosened from the paper, as if the page had stopped being able to remember it." Stansbury's "It makes the cost mine to approve." is why I like him: he copies before he lets anything be spent. The grocery lists as a tracking tool ("Movement in sequence. Nothing more romantic than errands.") are the cleverest magic in the book, and "I can feel the shoe polish." / "Try to survive." made me laugh. "I have not reached the axle emotionally." and "Do not anthropomorphize the evidence." too.
Pathwell's confession in the van is what I was owed since ch. 7, and it arrives early, in plain speech, because she refuses to be explained around ("You don't get to make me the part of this you explain around."). That kills most of my distrust of the held-back fact. "It's the answer I use when I don't want to say the other one." and "'You are doing it again.' / 'Yes.'" - good. I have no more to ask about what he did; I do still want to know who "someone" was ("Someone asked me for help"). He doesn't say and she doesn't push - the one place the book still holds something back.
What I'd cut or tighten: Elizabeth's summing-up, "And paying for it yourself made you feel like the method was yours to choose." - it's the theme said aloud by the person who has known the man for two days, and "At the time, I would have called that responsibility" is the neat reply. I felt the machinery. Also the shared-memory finding ritual ran a bit long on mechanics (the 'catching' of threads, two grocery lists, direction fading at the crossroads). The slap exchange ("Leo, the next time I call you an idiot, you smack me right in the face." ... "Bank it.") I did not follow; I think it is a bit of foreknowledge I wasn't given. And "Leo", "big cat", "grande gatto" for Stansbury and Pathwell arrive with no explanation; I assume nicknames, I don't know why. I didn't care to be told.
Plausibility: Elizabeth walks a county road for an hour and a half and happens to be found because of a bad-coffee memory; they'd explicitly said it was luck. Fine.

## Chapter 11
The best chapter in the book for me, and the one that made me stop reading for a minute. The museum is the thing I came for: "Museums are arguments conducted very slowly." The accession card corrected three times ("REGIMENT UNKNOWN. / Then UNKNOWN crossed out. / Then 19TH VIRGINIA? ... NO. COAT FOUND IN HENRY VALE'S ATTIC, 1978."), Daniel Vale and the socks his sister knitted too narrow, "THIN AS A FENCE RAIL. ATE THREE EGGS." and "For her, perhaps that was the end of the war." / "Nobody made a joke after that." Then the book spends exactly that goodwill: "SAM HOME TODAY split down the middle." and "Relief hit the room, huge and impossible and pointed nowhere. Someone was home. Someone wasn't dead. Someone needed eggs." I did cry a bit and I'm annoyed about it.
The turn is earned and it is the book's best structural choice: Pathwell says "all right" and is lying ("For one second she believed him."), then does what he did before: "I can contain this." Elizabeth hears "the work van inside the sentence. Quickest. Cleanest. I can handle it." He has just confessed the pattern and he repeats it inside the hour. That's character, not plot. I'm no longer trying to guess what the twist is; the damage is the story. Her broken shoulder is a real cost, not a bruise she shakes off by the next scene.
Laughed: the blue-spot parking joke ("just because we're trying to find a bad guy doesn't mean we need to be one"), "Breakfast has a substantial experimental record." / "For you.", "She banned me twice.", "I am adjusting my brand." and the cannon aimed at the gift shop ("History has casualties.").
Where I'd trim: the paragraph of "ordinary museum visitors" walking round with Shade stepping on the squeaky floorboard on purpose is cute but the floor-board beat has no payoff. Elizabeth's "I don't need to be rescued." is on the nose in a book that has already shown it twice; the "You said all right." line from Shade is better. One mild logic bump: Pathwell took Stansbury's letter from the library "while he was upstairs" and I did not see it, and Shade's slip, which should have been ambiguous from Pathwell's side, is spelled out ("For half a second, from Pathwell's side of the room, it was simple and ugly") - I'd have got it without the narration.

## Chapter 12
A rest chapter, and I'd cut a third of it. The healer resetting the shoulder is a good scene on its own: "I need you to tell me when you're ready." / "Does being ready change anything?" / "No." / "Then why ask?" / "Because it's your shoulder." That is the best line in the chapter and the book's idea of care in six sentences. I also laughed at "You people are committed to making trees unpleasant." and Shade dropping his spoon in the stew ("Say nothing." / "I am enjoying the possibility."). "He respects paperwork more consistently than people." / "That's bleak." / "It is also why we keep paperwork." and the card "DONOR PERMISSION REQUIRED. PATHWELL IS NOT DONOR." - funny and a smart way to give her control.
What I'd cut: the diary donation is the cookbook donation in ch. 4 again: "Elizabeth's hand stayed suspended after the diary left it. Empty. She lowered it." is almost word for word "Elizabeth's hand was still halfway raised. She lowered it." I got it the first time. The "Store? / Loan? / Give" negotiation and the "Anything you want restricted?" beat mostly repeat what the ch. 4 archivist already taught me about how this works. A reader who is paying attention doesn't need to see it twice; if I were editing I'd keep only the new part, the PATHWELL IS NOT DONOR card, and the checklist "FIND NANA'S SPOON," and lose the rest.
Also: "Thumper was in there too: his six-toed paw print pressed into the center of a page" - a new named animal arrives in the middle of a paragraph and is never explained. I have no idea who or what Thumper is. Probably a dead pet. I didn't care to be told it in passing, because it is the one detail in the diary the narrator singles out and then drops.
Skimmed: the "healer wrapped his palm ... asking permission" paragraph; I knew they were kind by now. Not much happens. But the turnip boy with the sock is a running gag that's earning its keep ("Are you Pathwell?" / "No." / "Good.").

## Chapter 13
Shade's chapter, and it is where I started to love him. It is also a chapter with no plot until the last page, and I didn't mind, which is rare for me. Laughed: "I have inherited worse problems." followed by his own wince ("Sorry." / "For what?" / "Making everything about that." / "Then don't."), Milo's "You look like Pathwell. ... But you don't talk as much.", "'What else do you like?' ... 'Pockets.'", "'I changed my mind.' / 'That's a terrible answer.' / 'It is also true.'", and the purple foot that wasn't purple. The line that got me was "I want to stay here." / "Then stay." / "People keep doing that." / "Asking." / "Terrible habit." / "Disorienting." That is the whole of Shade's arc in four exchanges, and it echoes the healer's "Because it's your shoulder." Camp's culture, asking before touching, is coherent and it is the opposite of Pathwell's habit of deciding.
Leaned in at the coffee: "She took one sip. The coffee tasted like somebody had dissolved a burnt fence post in hot water and then concentrated it for shipping." / "That's terrible." / "I know." / Elizabeth stops. The echo of Pathwell's line in Shade's mouth is the kind of ghost the book should keep doing, and "Then I don't remember it." / "I know." is perfect restraint.
Braced at the end: the right hand "stayed there despite the strain in his wrist" and the pale seam between two trees. "That hand was promising," he said. Then the threshold opened." Good chapter ending; "Elizabeth heard the distant scrape of a shoe on museum wood before the opening fully formed" tells me exactly who's coming through.
What I'd trim: the Hearts game is five or six beats of the same joke (Shade is good at reading people, bad at the cards, Milo is smarter than everyone). Two would do. And "Shade carried a sack of onions for a woman at the cooking wagon, because she had asked for two hands and he technically had them." is a bit precious. And Mama Baga's "Take that off." coat business at the start; the coat being "doing more work than Elizabeth had realized" is a good observation I'd keep, the exchange around it I'd shorten.

## Chapter 14
I braced the whole way and I hated Pathwell for most of it, which I think is the intent, but this is where the book tips from "he has a flaw" toward "he is being made to repeat it for the plot." Everyone says no, in sequence: Shade ("No." / "I am the person living with it,"), Mama Baga ("Stop." four times), Stansbury ("You are not paying for this."), Elizabeth ("Let it fail"), and he does it anyway, saying "I can contain it" for the second time in a few hours, and Elizabeth names it: "That's what you said at the museum." After ch. 11, I didn't need the same beat at this length. The structure of five people asking in turn is mechanical, and Mama Baga's explanation of the working ("Your intent is split.") is the book telling me why it fails when the scene had already shown me.
What worked: Shade refusing without tearing the frame apart ("He simply stood where he was and refused.") and "Don't make him the reason." / "If this fails because Stansbury stood there, he'll spend the rest of his life explaining that part instead of the choice." - that is a shockingly adult line for Shade to say and I believed it. "Same arrogance in a better coat." made me laugh out loud, and Shade glancing at his green jacket as nobody laughs. "Cruel." / "In your handwriting." / "Now you're being punitive." - the curator's inventory banter as a last breath of normal. "You are confusing care with tyranny." / "Family tradition." / "Inherited badly." is a clean exchange.
Leaned in hard at the fire: the lantern, the diary "cover open, ordinary pages fanned against the boards," Nana's cookbook "still visible inside its support," and Milo shouting "Hey!" - more surprised than afraid. And the list of loosed impressions ("Someone laughing at a funeral and hating themselves for it. / Someone writing a daughter's name three times because once had not felt sufficient.") is exactly the Neverwhere-adjacent ache I read for.
Didn't care for: "At the time, it looked like fidgeting." - a narrator outside the scene winking forward. Also the loop's mechanics (three pins, waxed cord, loop "halfway between", "rejoining frame", "two anchors, one return") are fairly dense and I read them for outcome only. And the last six lines are staccato cliffhanger ("Shade was still alive. / The draw between them was still there. / The archive was burning.") - effective but it is the third chapter in a row ending on a short-line sting.

## Chapter 15
The emotional peak of the book for me, and I read the second half with my hand over my mouth. Elizabeth steps over her own diary ("Her body knew the book before her mind finished identifying it.") and past Nana's cookbook to reach Milo; the sock "finally stayed up. Elizabeth hated that detail immediately." The rescue is physical and unglamorous and costs her (tearing the sling with her teeth, using the bad shoulder as a fulcrum: "This next part is stupid." / "Pathwell stupid?" / "Worse. Mine."). The stakes are earned by chapters 4 and 12: I knew what the cookbook was. "Her own ordinary handwriting disappeared without being used for anything. / No spell. / No sacrifice. / No last message. / Just fire." is the best paragraph in the book. "She didn't regret the order." is one line too many saying what the scene says; I'd cut it.
Shade walking out: "You really have learned nothing in the last six minutes." and Pathwell finally asking instead of telling ("Will you stay?" / "No."), "'You still owe me a hand,' Milo called. / Nobody had told him not to." and "I was winning." / "You were not." / "Corrupt institution." The queen of spades is the object I'll remember. I did not want this and it is what the book had been building toward since ch. 9's "For him to stop deciding what I am before I get to": he gets to decide, and what he decides is to die for a child he has known for a morning. "A person could accumulate a surprising amount of life in a morning." Yes.
Where I'd push back: it is a bit convenient. Shade volunteers to "separate the signals," the blob follows him, absorbs him and the spillage, and the draw, the stalker, and the cleanup problem all disappear in one move. The book spent fourteen chapters making that problem hard and then solves it with the character I liked best, whose death costs the plot nothing. Also "I don't know what it will do" / "That seems likely" is a thin justification for the chosen method; I wanted one more beat of Elizabeth objecting (she does, then doesn't). And "That was the choice. Everything else burned afterward." repeats ch. 4's "That was the choice. Everything afterward was consequence." I see the echo and it works as a refrain, but it is the same sentence-shape, so it reads like a tic rather than a rhyme. Plain mechanical note: "Stansbury hadn't used the front door." etc. is good; Stansbury going back for "one more shelf" and getting burned is exactly him.
Didn't care for: the paragraph of Pathwell's "There is fresh spill." / "You can ask." - the book making a thematic point (asking vs deciding) just after having made it. I'll let it stand.

## Chapter 16
Aftermath, long, and mostly right, but it is where the book begins to explain itself. What I'd keep without touching: "SHADE - DIED AT CAMP. / GREEN JACKET, CAMP ISSUE - LOST WITH HIM. / LIKED POCKETS." and Mama Baga's "That is not an archive category." / "Archive burned. Categories are temporarily flexible." / "Fair." That is the funeral, and it needed nothing else. Milo: "He still owes me a hand." / "That counts as dying, right?" is the line I'll quote to people. "You could have grabbed them." / "Yes." / "But you got me." / "Yes." - no accusation, which "made it easier and harder." Right. "Loss didn't care much about paperwork." is the best line about the archive fire. Mama Baga on Shade's coffee ("I considered asking him to leave on that basis alone.") broke me properly, and the laugh breaking in the middle was earned.
Where I'd cut: (1) Elizabeth's crying list: "Elizabeth cried for Nana. / For the cookbook. / For the diary and every stupid grocery list inside it. ... For the fact that choosing Milo had been right and had still cost her things she wanted back." I had every one of those in my head already; the last line states the book's whole position and I'd end on the laugh breaking. (2) The restitution scene, "You will not prune to buy any of it." / "You will not decide that something is close enough to replace what burned." / "You will ask before reorganizing anything." - this is a contract read aloud, and Pathwell's "Agreed" five times is a bit too clean and a bit too fast. I'm not sure a man who has repeated one mistake three times in a day would sign off on a list of rules before lunch. If I were him I'd be sulking. (3) Elizabeth's apology scene: "I need you not to make me responsible for whether you become a better person." is a very good line; the next three sentences ("I am not going to tell you what lesson to learn so you can learn it at me...") are the author spelling out the same line in three more ways, and I'd cut them. (4) The last sentence of the chapter - "for once Pathwell would not be the person deciding what those things were." - tells me what the chapter was about. And "Person is the word I have for that." is a touch too neatly the answer to "Was he a person?" - I'd have let her ask and not got an answer.
Also: Stansbury refusing a scar fix ("Let it be.") is very good and tiny: the book's moral choice shown as a refusal of convenient magic. "You inhaled like an advertisement." Laughed.

## Chapter 17
A twenty-one-day montage. I liked most of it and it is the first chapter where I felt the book running out of story. Laughed or smiled at: "'Do you want to say you're doing well?' 'No.'" (I mean the version where she asks and he refuses), "This is not information. This is a family argument preserved as infrastructure."
