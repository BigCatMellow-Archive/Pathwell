# Core questions

## 1. What is this book about?

It's about what a person owes for the damage they do while trying to help, and who gets to decide the price. A man who treats every problem as his to fix, in the quickest way and at his own cost, keeps hurting the people around him, and the book makes him learn to ask first and to take instructions. Beside that, it's about how ordinary things (a recipe, a grocery list, a diary, a sock letter) carry the lives of the people who wrote them, and what it means to keep them or spend them. A woman new to a city, with nothing unpacked, finds out she can choose where she goes next.

## 2. The moment that worked best

Chapter 15, the fire. Elizabeth runs past her own diary and her grandmother's cookbook to get Milo out from under the shelf, and both books burn with no magic and no meaning: "Her own ordinary handwriting disappeared without being used for anything. / No spell. / No sacrifice. / No last message. / Just fire." Then "She didn't regret the order." It works because the book spent twelve chapters teaching me what those objects were worth, and then refused to make the loss mean anything extra. Shade's exit in the same chapter ("You still owe me a hand," / "Corrupt institution.") is a close second for the same reason.

## 3. The moment that worked least

The "That was the choice" lines in chapters 4 and 15, and, close behind, the chapter 16 wrap-up where Elizabeth cries and the text lists what she's crying for, ending "For the fact that choosing Milo had been right and had still cost her things she wanted back." In each case the scene had already done the work, and the line arrives to tell me what I felt. The second "That was the choice" is a copy of the first, so it also made me aware of the writing.

## 4. Where I got confused or stopped believing

- Chapter 8: the stand-alone line "It was the crash that woke her." in the middle of the car crash. It's the book's first sentence repeated. I couldn't tell if she's asleep, if it's an echo, or if it's a slip, and nothing later explained it.
- Chapter 9: Elizabeth walks out of the diner and sets off on foot for a bar the waitress described as "three turns" away, in the dark, with a sore shoulder. It took her an hour and I didn't believe it for a second. I let it ride because the chapter had earned it.
- Chapters 5 to 8: the brothers keep saying "Not here" in front of her, and the reasons for each delay are thinner than the last. I believed the first one.
- I never learned who "asked for help" before the bad prune, or why Pathwell was in her living room with her diary in chapter 1 and how a cleanup creature got to her door before the working broke. I don't know if I'm supposed to know by the end.
- Chapter 12: Thumper appears once with a six-toed paw print and isn't mentioned again. I don't know who he is.

## 5. What I wanted that I didn't get

The building. I picked this up for a book about an apartment building and its neighbors, and after chapter 2 they're gone. Her coworkers appear at the party, and nobody on her floor ever does. I wanted at least one neighbor to knock on a door in chapter 17 or 18, when she's unpacking and the door has been replaced.

I also wanted the question at the center of the book answered: who asked Pathwell for help, what the problem was, and why he thought pruning was the only way. Shade says "Ask him what he chose," and Pathwell says "Help." Elizabeth tells him "You are doing it again," he says "Yes," and I still don't get the answer. That is the one place where the book's honesty about withholding stops short of paying it off.

I'd also have liked more of the blob's story. The cleanup creatures are explained in chapter 5 and then used as a threat; what they are, who built them, and who benefits never comes up.

## 6. One thing I'd tell the author

Trust the scenes. When a chapter has already shown me what a choice cost (the sickbed in 4, the fire in 15, the apology in 16), don't write the sentence that explains it. The best lines in the book are the ones that don't say what they mean ("Specificity is expensive," "LIKED POCKETS," putting the cookbook back "square to the edge"), and the weakest are the ones that do.
