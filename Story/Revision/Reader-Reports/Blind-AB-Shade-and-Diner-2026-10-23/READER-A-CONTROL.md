# Reading log: Pathwell

## Chapter One

- **"half a sheet cake that now said only ELIZ, because her coworkers had started at the wrong end"** — Laughed, quietly. Good first detail. It tells me this is going to be funny without the book announcing it, and it makes her coworkers feel real and slightly careless in one line.
- **"reading her diary like a magazine in a waiting room"** — Delighted. Instantly know who he is: rude, unbothered, likable anyway.
- **"Low tide, a long way from any sea."** — First real unease. A nice, specific wrongness.
- **"Don't think. What's your favorite recipe?"** — Intrigued. Hooked on the idea that the magic runs on a grandmother's handwriting. That's a premise I want to understand.
- **"even through the crooked places where Nana's pen had hesitated"** — A little lump in the throat already. The hesitation in the handwriting is the kind of detail that makes a dead person present.
- **She grabs the book and tears the page** — Surprised, in a good way. She acts, and it costs something. Also felt a small sting: she's ruined Nana's book to protect it.
- **"She was twelve again..."** — Moved, mildly. It's a familiar move (smell triggers memory) but it's placed well, right in the middle of the danger.
- Slight confusion during the fight: I wasn't fully sure what happened with the glowing page and the blob, or how he won. The bathroom-door cutaway is a reasonable cheat, but the "rumbling... light... pop" ending felt vague.
- **"The cake, somehow, was fine."** — Laughed. Perfect beat.
- **"Because it was hers,"** — This landed. He stops being in a hurry for one line. Made me trust him a little.
- **"Ready?" "No." "Perfect."** — Grinned. Good closer, if slightly a type I've seen before.

End of chapter: Strong, quick opening. I like both of them already. The comedy is doing real work. Minor wobble in the action clarity.

## Overall

I liked this book a great deal, and more than I expected from the first chapter, which reads like a fun Doctor-Who-ish romp. It turns out to be about something real: who gets to decide what happens to someone, and what it costs when a charming, well-meaning person decides for you. The magic system, where ordinary handwritten things (a grandmother's recipe, a love note, a grocery list, an unsent letter) carry the feeling they were written with and are used up when spent, is the best idea in it, and the book never stops finding new uses for it. The comedy is constant and mostly lands: Stansbury, the archivist, Mama Baga and Milo all get laughs from me in nearly every scene, and the funniest lines are usually the ones doing emotional work too. My reservations are small: the early action is a bit hard to picture, the deadpan back-and-forth rhythm gets samey in the middle, and by Chapters 14 and 16 the book was spelling out its moral argument more than it needed to, with several characters lined up to say the right thing. I also finished unsure what Elizabeth made of Pathwell's last confession. The moments that stayed with me: "MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH."; Elizabeth crashing the Cadillac into the ditch on purpose; "SAM HOME TODAY. THIN AS A FENCE RAIL. ATE THREE EGGS."; Shade's "Pockets" and "That hand was promising"; Elizabeth stepping over her diary and running past Nana's cookbook to get to Milo; Milo saying "He still owes me a hand"; and the archivist writing "LIKED POCKETS" on the loss sheet, which is the line I'll remember longest.

## Follow-up

**1. Three favourite characters, ranked.**
1. **Shade.** He's the most moving thing in the book. He's dry and careful about what belongs to him ("Observation. Not memory."), and watching him become a person through bad coffee, Hearts and pockets is the best stretch of the novel.
2. **Stansbury.** He's the funniest ("I have not reached the axle emotionally"), and his love for his brother is the quiet backbone of the book. He's also the one who checks himself when told no, which makes him Pathwell's real counterweight.
3. **Elizabeth.** I cheered her on from the dry-cleaning ticket onward, and the ditch is when I fully loved her. Honourable mentions: Milo and the archivist, who were close behind.

**2. Did I care about Shade? When did it start?**
Yes. I was interested on the road ("as though he'd learned to stop before he walked into rooms"). The caring started in the car to the diner, when he's singing under his breath, loses the words in the second verse, hears her and stops, and says nothing about it. It locked in at the diner with "We used to—" / "He used to." By "Pockets" in Ch13 I was attached.

**3. When he was taken, what did I feel?**
Disbelief first, because I didn't think the book would really do it. Then a sick drop, then anger at Pathwell. Yes, it felt unfair. He had just said "I want to stay here," he had refused without fighting, and he was the one who paid for someone else's choice. He called for help and the man who caused it was lying right there. Something beyond the person was lost too: a life that had only just started being his own. The unfinished hand, the coffee that was "his," the things he hadn't decided he liked yet ("I don't know yet"). Also the open question of what he was, which he was finally getting to answer for himself. It felt like losing a future, which is grim, because that's exactly what Pathwell had been spending all book.

**4. Did the book give his loss enough room?**
Mostly yes. Ch16 gives it real room: Milo's questions, "He still owes me a hand," Mama Baga naming the death, "LIKED POCKETS," and the crying by the fire. The Hearts scene in Ch17 brought him back well. Two places felt thin:
- **Ch15.** The taking itself happens offstage, reported by a bystander ("It took him."). That's effective, but I never got a last moment with him.
- **Ch18.** Pathwell's confession that he watched Shade call for help and didn't call anyone is a huge revelation about that loss. The book moves past it quickly: Elizabeth goes back to the shelves, and an hour later they're off to see the geese. That's where it moved on too fast for me.

**5. Was there anything I wanted from or for Shade that I didn't get?**
Yes:
- I wanted him to finish the hand of Hearts.
- I wanted him free of the draw on his own terms, and some sign he would get to keep living at Camp.
- I wanted one real conversation where Pathwell actually listens to him, not argues with him or builds a frame around him.
- I wanted a goodbye, or a last exchange with Elizabeth before the end. The look he gives her before she goes to Milo is all we get, and I wanted one more word.
- I would have liked Stansbury to get to know him a little.

**6. "Then you were never the point." What did it mean for Elizabeth, and what did she lose?**
It meant the danger was never about her. She was swept along by Pathwell's mistaken certainty. What she lost was the justification for the whole night: the apartment, the meeting, Nana's sugar-cookie page. All of it was paid on the strength of his wrong assumption, plus whatever small comfort there is in being chosen. At the time it felt to me like a gut punch that was also a relief, because if she isn't the point, her life is hers again. I felt it more as a reframing than a big loss.

**7. Why did she leave the diner and go back toward Pathwell? Did it make sense?**
Shade gave her the right question ("Ask him what he chose"). She wanted the answer from the person responsible, and this time on her own terms, after a night of being driven around and talked over. She says she isn't doing it for Shade, and I believed that: it was for herself. Emotionally it made sense. Practically it felt a little odd. She walks for over an hour on a night road toward a bar with no real plan, and she leaves Shade alone in the booth just as I'd warmed to him. I felt a small pang leaving him there.

**8. Scores.**
- Shade's death: **8/10.** "That hand was promising" coming back as his epitaph, and Milo's "He still owes me a hand," are what pushed it that high.
- The diner: **7/10.** It's a quiet chapter that did a lot. The Prague / Celia / pears list and "For him to stop deciding what I am before I get to" both stuck. Some of the question-and-answer back-and-forth ran a bit long.
