# Reading log

## Chapter One

- **Opening, "reading her diary like a magazine in a waiting room."** Hooked fast. The image is funny and a little creepy at once, and the stranger's "Reading," with a page-turn is the first time I smiled. I liked him immediately, against my better judgement.
- **"half a sheet cake that now said only ELIZ, because her coworkers had started at the wrong end."** Small laugh. Nice detail that does double work: the party went fine, but nobody quite knows her yet.
- **"He laughed at her joke about the landlord, which was more than anyone from work had managed."** A little pang. She's lonely in a new place, and the book tells me so sideways. Good.
- **The thud at the door / "Friend of yours?"** Tension rose, and the dryness of his line kept it from tipping into horror-movie cliché. I was curious, not scared.
- **The cookbook and "Don't think. What's your favorite recipe?"** This is where I got interested in the magic. The idea that a recipe written in a grandmother's hand carries something real is lovely, and "even through the crooked places where Nana's pen had hesitated" got me; that's a specific, tender detail.
- **She tears the page out; the flash of being twelve.** Moved, a bit. The memory is brief and plain (sun, rain smell, Nana smiling) and the line "felt something she hadn't felt in years" is a touch generic, but the moment works because it's grabbed and lost in a second.
- **The fight.** Slightly confusing choreography; I wasn't sure what the light was doing until the blob hit it. She runs to the bathroom to be sick, which I appreciated: that's a real reaction, not a heroine reaction.
- **"The cake, somehow, was fine."** Laughed. Perfect beat.
- **"Because it was hers."** The best line in the chapter. He stops being in a hurry for one sentence and I believed there was a person under the patter. Then he's back to "Shoes. Now. I did say please." which I also enjoyed.
- **"Ready?" "No." "Perfect."** Classic, a bit familiar (Doctor Who energy is strong), but it landed.
- **End of chapter overall:** Strong opener. Warm, quick, funny, with a real ache underneath (Nana). The prose is plain and sometimes a little clumsy ("Her body moved back before she did" — I liked that, actually; "the behemoth's weight" felt off-register), but the voice is confident. I want to keep going.

## Overall reaction

I liked this book a great deal, and more as it went on. It starts as a charming, quick, slightly familiar urban-fantasy romp (a strange, flippant man drags a lonely woman out of her apartment), and quietly becomes a book about consent, loss, and the difference between an original and a copy, without ever stopping being funny. The magic system, where written feeling is power and using a page spends it, is the best thing about it, because every spell costs someone's real life: Nana's recipe, an unsent letter, a quarantine note, Daniel Vale's socks. The dialogue is consistently sharp. Stansbury, Mama Baga, the archivist and Milo all earned real laughs from me in very few lines, and the quiet "hanging out" chapters (the bar, the diner, the stew, the Hearts game) are where the book is strongest. My reservations: the middle chapters hold back the central mystery a beat or two too long; Pathwell's confession in the van is morally right but concrete-light; the museum and the brass frame follow nearly the same pattern; several characters say the moral out loud when the objects have already said it; and the final-chapter revelation about Shade is so heavy it needed more room. A few small snags too: who Thumper was, the "He lies / I don't lie" moment, "Leo" and "big cat." What stayed with me: Elizabeth handing over the whole cookbook ("If I keep holding the rest..."); the brothers arguing over fish and "Branding"; Elizabeth choosing the ditch; Shade's list of nouns ("a woman named Celia who hated pears"); SAM HOME TODAY. ATE THREE EGGS, and then watching it tear; Shade making terrible coffee and making it his; "Pockets"; Elizabeth stepping over the diary and running past the cookbook to reach Milo; Milo saying "He still owes me a hand"; the loss sheet reading LIKED POCKETS and REPAIR REQUESTED; Mama Baga's "Person is the word I have for that"; and Pathwell putting the Merritt cookbook back square to the edge. Shade, who's in the book for about six chapters, is the character I'll remember longest.

## Follow-up

**1. Three favourite characters, ranked.**
1. **Shade.** He shows up late, with a flat, dry wit and no charm to lean on, and I watched him become a person through small choices: the coffee, the jacket, losing at Hearts, "Pockets." He's also the only character who says no plainly and keeps saying it. He's the one I'll remember.
2. **Stansbury.** He's the funniest character in the book ("My axle is in a drainage culvert. I'm evaluating several positions."), and the deadpan covers a lot of quiet care: checking Pathwell's pupils, giving Elizabeth the dagger, choosing to keep his scar. He's also the one who actually listens when he's told he doesn't need to save everything.
3. **Elizabeth.** She grew on me. The ditch is where I started really liking her. She's polite to the point of self-erasure early on and ends up refusing to be anyone's lesson. (Milo and Mama Baga were very close behind.)

**2. Did I care about Shade? When did it start?**
Yes. He was intriguing on the road ("I remember things I did not live"), but I started caring in the car in chapter nine. An old song comes on the radio, he's singing under his breath, he hears her humming, and he stops and says nothing about it. I fell for him properly at "Your foot is purple." / His foot was not purple.

**3. When Shade was taken.**
In the moment I felt a drop, then grief. It's slightly odd that it happens out of sight while Elizabeth is inside, so I learned it from "It took him." That made it feel worse, not lighter. Then the queen of spades still face up on the table finished me off.
It felt unfair, yes. He had said no, he didn't fight, and he even told Elizabeth to go to Milo, and he was eaten by the fallout of someone else overriding him. He paid for Pathwell's choice exactly as he'd feared.
Beyond the person, what was lost was the future he'd only just started: the life he was choosing in real time. The book had set up a question, whether something made out of discarded possibility could become its own self. He was answering it yes, a little more each scene, and the answer got cut off mid-sentence. "That hand was promising" is exactly what was lost.

**4. Did the book give his loss enough room?**
Mostly yes, in chapter sixteen. Milo's questions, the loss sheet (LIKED POCKETS, REPAIR REQUESTED), Mama Baga's "Person is the word I have for that," and Elizabeth's crying are generous and very good.
Two places felt too quick:
- **The death itself in chapter fifteen.** It shares a chapter with the archive fire, the diary and the cookbook, so it gets crowded. It's one beat among several losses.
- **Pathwell's confession in chapter eighteen** that he saw Shade calling for help and chose not to call anyone. That's huge, and it gets about a page. Elizabeth goes back into the shelves, and soon we're on to geese. That is where the book moved on too quickly for me.

**5. What I wanted from or for Shade that I didn't get.**
- To finish the hand of Hearts he was winning.
- To get his sleeves let out and wear the jacket he chose.
- A goodbye of any kind with Elizabeth. They never get a last exchange.
- Some moment of being free of the draw while still alive, even briefly.
- One real conversation with Stansbury, who only ever knew him in fragments.
- Pathwell actually listening to him once, before it was too late.

**6. "Then you were never the point."**
For Elizabeth it took away the story she'd been telling herself: that the night was about her, that she'd been chosen or marked or mattered to the magic, and that this made it worth leaving her old life. The book is honest that "she had taken all of it personally, because she had wanted to."
What she lost was the comfort of being central. I felt a sting of sympathetic embarrassment and some sadness (the second egg with the skinned-over yolk did that). There was also relief, because it freed her: everything she did afterwards was her own choice rather than a role she'd been handed.

**7. Why she left the diner and went back.**
In my words: Shade handed her the real question, what did Pathwell choose, and only Pathwell could answer it. She wanted the truth from the person who owed it to her, and she wanted to get it under her own power: walking, not driven, and not as Shade's leverage ("I'm not doing it for you."). Having found out she wasn't the point, she wanted to stand in the conversation as herself.
It made emotional sense. Practically, walking alone at night for miles with no money made me wince. And there was a small strain in her leaving the one person who'd been straight with her to go back to the one who'd withheld. I accepted it, but it felt partly like the plot needed her back.

**8. Scores.**
- Shade's death: **8/10.**
- The diner: **7/10.** It landed as a quiet, honest turn for her, more ache than blow.
