Chapter Thirteen

By full morning, Elizabeth was still by the central fire when Mama Baga stopped beside Shade, pinched the sleeve of his dark coat between two fingers, and held it up to the light. Three tiny pieces of museum glass glittered in the wool.

"Take that off."

Shade looked down at the coat. "I was planning to."

"You have been planning to for forty minutes."

"I've been eating."

"You finished twenty minutes ago," Mama Baga said, with a glance at the empty bowl in his hands.

Shade looked toward Elizabeth. "Is everyone here like this?"

"Worse," Elizabeth said.

Mama Baga pointed toward a line strung between two wagons, where several shirts, two pairs of pants, a patched red shawl and three coats hung in various states of repair. "Clean clothes. Take something that fits. Put yours in the basket."

Shade stood. The movement pulled his right hand open toward the far side of Camp, and he closed it against his thigh before anyone said anything. Then he took off the coat, and without it the resemblance to Pathwell changed. The face, the shoulders and the hands were still there, but the coat had been doing more work than Elizabeth had realized. He held it for a moment. One cuff was torn from the museum, the collar had a dark smear where he had hit the barrier, and a button near the waist hung by two threads. He put it in the wash basket.

"If you want it repaired, leave a note," Mama Baga said.

"I don't."

Mama Baga waited. Shade chose a green canvas jacket with patched elbows and too many pockets and put it on. The sleeves were half an inch short.

"That one belonged to a man with unfortunate arms," Mama Baga said.

Shade flexed his wrists. "I have inherited worse problems."

Mama Baga's eyes lifted to his face, and Shade seemed to hear the sentence after he'd said it. "Sorry."

"For what?"

"Making everything about that."

Mama Baga shrugged. "Then don't." She walked away.

"That looked painful," Elizabeth said.

"The jacket?"

"Being given an answer that short."

"I preferred the coat."

"No, you didn't."

Shade looked down at the green sleeves. "No," he admitted. "I didn't." He put both hands into the pockets, then took them out. "Too many."

"You chose it."

"I can choose badly."

"Apparently."

That made him smile.


The one-sock boy arrived carrying a wooden coffee grinder almost as large as his head, and put it in Shade's hands. "Mama says if you keep asking for coffee, make coffee."

"I asked once."

"She heard you."

The boy started away. "Wait," Shade said.

He stopped.

Shade lifted the grinder. "Beans?"

"Tin with the blue lid. Not the red one."

"What's in the red one?"

"Pepper." The boy looked at him a moment longer. "You look like Pathwell."

Shade's face went flat.

"But you don't talk as much," the boy said, and left.

Elizabeth covered her mouth with her good hand. Shade stood with the grinder under one arm. "Do you want coffee?"

"I want to know whether you can make coffee."

"That is a different question."

"Exactly."

Shade went to find the blue tin.


He could make coffee, more or less. He worked the grinder with his bandaged left hand, because the right one couldn't be trusted to stay on the handle. He knew how fine the beans should be and how much water belonged in the battered enamel pot. He knew to wet the grounds first and wait, and how long to leave it near the edge of the fire instead of directly over the flame. The knowledge came easily enough that Elizabeth could see him distrust it. He would start a motion, pause, and go on only after checking what his hands were actually doing.

The first batch came out black enough to reflect nothing. Shade poured some into a tin cup and tasted it. Elizabeth waited.

"Good."

"That was not your face."

"My face is adjusting."

"To poison?"

Shade took another drink. "I like it."

Elizabeth held out her hand. "Let me see."

"You have already suffered enough."

"Cup."

He gave it to her, and she took one sip. The coffee tasted like somebody had dissolved a burnt fence post in hot water and then concentrated it for shipping.

Elizabeth handed the cup back. "That's terrible."

Shade took it. "I know."

Elizabeth stopped.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"That was clearly something."

"Pathwell said exactly that to me once," Elizabeth said. "About terrible coffee."

Shade's expression changed by a fraction. "When?"

"Space Between. After you already existed."

He nodded. "Then I don't remember it."

"I know."

He took another drink. "He was right once."

"About what?"

"Knowing the coffee was terrible."

"Yours is worse."

"I disagree."

"Then it's yours."

Shade drank again. "Good."


The boy came back ten minutes later with his sock fallen again, carrying a deck of cards with one corner rounded off from age. "We need one."

Shade looked around. "One what?"

"Person."

"For what?"

"Cards." The boy sighed. "We have three people. We need four. Are you playing?"

At the card table near the edge of the fire, an older woman in a knitted cap was already shuffling. The archivist sat across from her with one pencil still behind his ear, and an empty stool waited between them.

"What game?" Shade asked.

"Hearts."

Shade's expression changed, not quite into recognition. It was more like seeing a room through a window. "I know Hearts."

The boy pointed at the stool. "Then sit."

Shade did. Elizabeth followed more slowly and took a chair near the table where she could keep her sling supported, and the boy climbed onto the fourth stool.

"Milo," the archivist said. "Sock."

The boy looked down. "It's fine."

"It is under your heel."

Milo pulled it up. Elizabeth finally had a name for him.

Milo looked at her. "What's yours?"

"Elizabeth."

He nodded and went back to the cards. The older woman dealt. Shade picked up his hand, then stopped.

"I know the rules."

"You said that," Milo said.

"I don't remember playing."

The archivist arranged his hand. "Then today you play."

Shade looked at him. The archivist looked back, and the game began.


Shade was bad at Hearts. Not disastrously; worse than that, because he knew exactly why each bad decision had been bad about three seconds after he made it. The older woman won the hand with the expression of someone who had been winning card games since before any of them were a logistical concern. They dealt again. Shade adjusted quickly, too quickly. On the third hand he tried to dump a high heart on the archivist and walked directly into a trap Milo had been setting for six rounds.

The queen of spades landed in front of him. He stared at it. "This child is cheating."

"No," the archivist said.

"Thank you," Milo said.

"He's just better than you," the archivist added, and Milo leaned back with both hands behind his head.

"You see how they treat guests," Shade told Elizabeth.

"Are you one?"

Shade looked at the green jacket, then at the cards. "I don't know what I am here."

Milo dealt. "You're losing."

Shade picked up his hand. "Apparently that."

Elizabeth laughed. Her shoulder objected, and she did it anyway.

Somewhere in the fourth game, Elizabeth fell asleep in her chair. When she woke, the sling had slipped, someone had tucked Mama Baga's blanket back over her shoulder, and Shade was still losing.

By the fifth game he had stopped playing from remembered rules and started playing the people at the table. He watched Milo's eyebrows. He learned that the archivist scratched the side of his nose when holding the queen, and that the older woman never looked at anyone when she had a dangerous hand because she did not need to, and that Milo talked more when bluffing, which meant Milo talked almost constantly. Shade lost again, by fewer points.

The archivist gathered the cards. "Again?"

Shade seemed surprised by the question. "Yes."

The older woman began shuffling.


Later, when Milo had been sent on another errand and the archivist had gone back to the wagon, Shade stayed at the table turning one of the cards between his fingers. The queen of spades.

"Souvenir?" Elizabeth asked.

"I want to know how he did it." He turned the card over. "Six rounds. He set it up for six rounds, and I watched him do it."

"Milo is a child."

"Yes," Shade said, with something close to respect.

Elizabeth shifted the blanket around her sling. Shade noticed. "Pain?"

"Yes."

"Do you need the healer?"

"No. Same pain."

He nodded and left it there.

"You liked that," Elizabeth said.

"Being humiliated by a child?"

"Playing."

Shade turned the card over. The back was red, worn pale along the edges. "Yes."

"What else do you like?"

He frowned. "Coffee."

"Your coffee is an industrial solvent."

"Still coffee."

"Anything else?"

He looked around Camp, not toward the tree line: the green jacket, the card table, Milo's abandoned stool, a woman shaking flour from a cloth, a dog asleep under the cooking wagon. "Pockets," he said.

Elizabeth stared. "Pockets."

Shade put the queen into one of the green jacket's many pockets. "I changed my mind."

"That's a terrible answer."

"It is also true." After a moment he added, "I don't know yet."

"You could stay here," Elizabeth said. "Mama Baga wouldn't mind. Milo needs someone to beat at cards. When Pathwell comes, you don't have to talk to him. I could—"

"Elizabeth."

She stopped.

"That's your plan," Shade said. "Let me find mine."

She didn't say anything else. After a while he said, "He keeps acting like you're something he can hold onto."

Elizabeth thought of the parking lot at the museum. "I know."


Camp kept moving. Shade carried a sack of onions for a woman at the cooking wagon, because she had asked for two hands and he technically had them. Milo reappeared with a strip of cloth tied around his calf to keep the sock up.

"Fixed it," he announced.

Shade studied the knot. "That's going to cut off your circulation."

"No it isn't."

"Your foot is purple."

Milo looked down. His foot was not purple. He looked back up. Shade's mouth twitched.

"You are a bad person," Milo said.

"I've been told worse."

"Cards later?"

Shade glanced at the onion sack, then at Milo. "Yes." He took the queen of spades out of the green jacket and held it out. "You'll need this."

Milo put it back into the deck and ran off.

The right hand opened again, harder this time, and the onion sack dropped. Three onions escaped and rolled beneath a wagon. Shade gripped his right wrist with his left hand. Elizabeth stood too quickly. Pain pulled at her shoulder, and she ignored it.

"Shade."

"I'm fine."

Elizabeth waited.

Shade exhaled. "No." He turned toward the far side of Camp. "Stronger."

"Closer?"

"Probably."

He closed his hand. It opened again, and he closed it with both hands this time.

Down by the tree line, someone shouted.

A girl of maybe nine came up the stream path at a run, without her bucket. Elizabeth had seen her at breakfast, carrying water two-handed and spilling most of it. She was holding her right arm out from her body as if it belonged to somebody else. From the fingertips to the wrist her hand had gone the gray of old dishwater, and the smell reached Elizabeth before the girl did.

Low tide.

"It was in the water," the girl said, to nobody in particular. "Not in it. Under it. It went right past me."

The healer was already there, and then Mama Baga. "Can you feel this?" the healer asked.

"No."

"This?"

"A bit."

"Good," the healer said, and meant it, and walked the girl toward the wagon instead of running, because running would have frightened her.

Nobody looked at Shade. That was worse than if they had. Shade looked at the tree line, and then at his own hand, still open toward a man who wasn't here. "It wasn't after her," he said quietly. "She was in the way."

When Mama Baga came back down the wagon steps, she stopped twenty feet from him. Her bracelets were still. "You want to leave?" she asked.

Shade looked genuinely surprised. "Would it help?"

"I didn't ask what would help."

Shade glanced toward the trees again, then at the card table, then at the onions on the ground. "No."

Mama Baga waited.

"I want to stay here."

"Then stay." She bent, picked up one of the escaped onions, and put it back in the sack.

"People keep doing that," Shade told Elizabeth.

"What?"

"Asking."

"Terrible habit," Elizabeth said.

"Disorienting."

Mama Baga picked up the second onion. "There's another one under the wagon."

Shade crouched to retrieve it. The right hand fought him the entire way.

He got the onion anyway.


Within the hour the girl from the stream was sitting on the healer's steps opening and closing her fingers, and the gray had gone back to pink everywhere but under the nails. Her name was Wren. Someone had brought her bucket up from the stream. Nobody had wanted to go down for it alone.

They played one more hand of Hearts because Milo insisted. The archivist complained that he had work, and the older woman ignored him and dealt. Elizabeth stayed beside the table with her sling supported and watched Shade collect thirteen cards one-handed. He was winning the hand.

Milo narrowed his eyes. "You have the queen."

Shade said nothing.

"You scratch your thumb when you have it."

"I do not."

"You just did."

The archivist looked delighted.

"This place is hostile," Shade told Elizabeth.

"You're still here."

He went back to his cards. "Yes."

The right hand snapped open. His left jerked with it, and his cards scattered across the dirt. At the same moment, somewhere beyond the wagons, a seam of pale light appeared between two trees.

Milo looked at the fallen cards. "Do those count as played?"

The older woman leaned over the table. "Absolutely not."

Shade stared at the seam of light. His right hand opened toward it. He closed it once, and it opened again immediately and stayed there despite the strain in his wrist. The pale line widened, and Elizabeth heard the distant scrape of a shoe on museum wood before the opening fully formed.

Shade looked down once at the cards around his boots. The queen of spades lay face-up beside an onion that had somehow escaped again.

"That hand was promising," he said.

Then the threshold opened.
