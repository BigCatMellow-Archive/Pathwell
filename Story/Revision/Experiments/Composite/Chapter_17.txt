Chapter Seventeen

By the third morning, Pathwell had learned that restitution contained more rulers than magic. The new drying rack stood beside the remains of the archive under a patched canvas awning. It was six feet long, four feet high, and—according to Pathwell—structurally superior to the one the archivist had described.

The archivist walked around it once, and then twice. Pathwell followed half a step behind, hands clasped behind his back, like a man showing off a house he'd built himself. Which he had.

Elizabeth sat nearby with her left arm still in the sling and a mug of Camp coffee balanced on one knee. Milo leaned on his broom-handle crutch and watched with the concentration of someone hoping an adult was about to be corrected publicly.

The archivist pointed at the bottom rail. "Too low."

"By how much?" Pathwell asked.

"Three inches."

"It's stronger there."

"It is also exactly where the wet boxes need to slide underneath."

Pathwell opened his mouth, closed it, and looked at the rack again. "Three inches?"

"Three."

Pathwell picked up the screwdriver, and Milo whispered, "He almost did it."

Elizabeth drank her coffee. "I know."

Pathwell looked over. "I can hear both of you."

"Good," Milo said.

The archivist pointed to the opposite end. "And that brace goes outside."

Pathwell stared at him. "That's objectively worse."

"Outside."

A long silence. Then Pathwell unscrewed it, and Elizabeth looked down at her coffee so he wouldn't see her smile.


The first week was mostly things Pathwell couldn't make interesting. The blue road notebook spent three days beneath blotting paper while the archivist and two Camp women reconstructed copied route pages from it. Pathwell was allowed to help only after the archivist gave him clean hands, a pencil, and exactly one section.

"Copy what it says," the archivist told him.

"I understand transcription."

"Including mistakes."

"Some of these are obviously mistakes."

"Especially the obvious ones."

Pathwell bent over the page.

DO NOT TRUST THE SOUTH BANK AFTER THREE DAYS OF RAIN, NO MATTER WHAT HESS SAYS.

Below it someone had written:

HESS SAYS FOUR.

Pathwell tapped the pencil against the table. "This isn't information. This is a family argument preserved as infrastructure."

The archivist didn't look up. "Copy it."

Pathwell copied it. His right hand shook on the downstrokes, and he went back over two of the letters, slowly, until they matched.

Elizabeth had gone back to her apartment by then. She came by Camp that afternoon because she wanted to, which meant asking Pathwell to open the way, and she found him copying HESS SAYS FOUR in exactly the same uneven capitalization as the original.

"You missed a smudge," she said.

Pathwell looked up. "The smudge isn't textual information."

The archivist, from the next table, said, "Copy the smudge."

"I've been placed under unreasonable government," Pathwell told Elizabeth.

"You set fire to their archive."

"Their government may be proportionate."

He copied the smudge.

Elizabeth stayed for lunch. Afterward the archivist put a stack of finished copies in front of her and the blue notebook beside it, and she checked them one-handed at the next table, line against line. In the third copy she found a place where Pathwell had quietly fixed somebody's spelling of Susquehanna.

She turned the page around and showed him. He studied it for a while. "Nobody's spelled it like that since the canal went in," he said. Then he took a clean sheet and copied the page again, mistake included.

Nobody asked when she was coming back.


Her apartment took longer to become hers than the archive took to become a construction site. That annoyed her. She had expected catastrophe to make ordinary things feel trivial. Instead ordinary things had remained aggressively themselves. The electric company still wanted an account number. There were still three boxes marked KITCHEN even though only one contained anything that belonged in a kitchen. Her dry cleaning was permanently gone, because the ticket had disappeared down a storm drain days ago and the shop had required identification, a description, and twenty minutes of suspicious discussion. Her work email contained forty-seven messages and only six that mattered, and she answered those six, the first one to her manager. She said it was a family emergency. She mailed the farmer a check for his fence, with an apology she rewrote three times. She didn't offer Stansbury anything for the Cadillac. He had told her she couldn't, and she suspected he was enjoying the grievance.

She picked Nana's mixing spoon up off the counter, because it was a spoon, and put it in the kitchen drawer. Then she moved it to the crock beside the stove, because the drawer felt wrong. Then she moved it back.

The choice was ridiculous.

It was still hers.

On Sunday she made chicken and dumplings from memory, because there was nowhere else left to make them from. She stirred with Nana's spoon. The dumplings came out heavy. She put in too much pepper, on purpose, and ate two bowls standing at the counter, and then she wrote the recipe on an index card in her own handwriting, before any of it could get away from her, and put the card in the drawer with the spoon.

CHICKEN AND DUMPLINGS — NANA'S

FROM MEMORY

DUMPLINGS: HEAVY.

PEPPER: MORE.

Her right hand still reached, now and then, for the coat pocket where the diary used to ride against her hip. There was nothing there. By the end of the first week it happened less often, though not never. Some days her hand went to the gold chain at her throat instead. She unpacked another box.


The current curator emailed Elizabeth directly. Pathwell hadn't asked her to come. The museum wanted a witness statement from someone who had been in the room and wasn't Pathwell, so she sat at a side table in the damaged gallery with a pen and a lined pad, writing it one-handed, while the museum made Pathwell write everything down. This was Stansbury's favorite part.

The curator had set up a folding table and placed three stacks of paper in front of Pathwell. One was labeled PHYSICAL DAMAGE. One was labeled COLLECTION LOSS. One was labeled INCIDENT ACCOUNT.

Pathwell looked at the third stack. "That's optimistic about how much I've got to say."

"In your own handwriting, please," the curator said.

Stansbury, his burned right hand wrapped in a lighter bandage now, sat against the far wall doing his prescribed finger stretches. "I like her."

"You're not part of this meeting."

"I was injured in the incident this meeting is about. I have standing."

Elizabeth didn't look up from her pad. "You were burned at a different incident."

"It's a series," Stansbury said.

The curator pointed at the first stack. "Cases. Barriers. Flooring. Mounts. Conservation assessment. Climate-control inspection, because your discharge knocked two sensors out of calibration."

Pathwell nodded. "Paid."

"That's paid. It isn't finished."

He stopped. "No. It isn't."

She moved to the second stack. "Daniel Vale letters. Household notebook. Medical ledger. Three accession cards by Margaret Bell. One civilian photograph. Two textile labels. All originals. All irrecoverable. You've sent us scans, photographs and old catalog entries since."

"Every copy I could find."

"We're grateful for them," the curator said. "They aren't the letters."

"I found a letter packet from the same family." He took it out of his coat. "Henry Vale, 1882 through 1886. Similar provenance. Same family line."

The curator's face didn't change. "No."

"Same family," Pathwell said. "Same period. Genuine originals. It's better than an empty—"

He stopped. His eyes went to the blank space where Daniel Vale's letters had been, and then to the curator. "No," he said.

The curator waited. At the side table, Elizabeth's pen had stopped.

Pathwell put the packet back in his coat. "They're not Daniel's."

"No, they're not."

"May I donate them separately?"

The curator considered him. "After a provenance review. And not as a replacement."

"Understood."

Stansbury's fingers stopped halfway through a stretch. Pathwell saw. "Don't look impressed."

"I am deciding whether the concussion was permanent."

"Ouch."

"Continue writing," Stansbury said.

"I regret bringing you," Pathwell said.

"I drove."

"There are taxis."

"You are not allowed to optimize transportation either."

"Is this useful?" the curator asked.

"Rarely," Pathwell and Stansbury said together.

Elizabeth finished her statement. She didn't soften the sequence. She wrote that Shade slipped. She wrote that she told Pathwell she was fine. She wrote that she told him not to use the letter. She wrote that he used it anyway.

The curator read the first page and said, "Thank you." That was all. Elizabeth liked the museum better immediately.

On her way out she passed the empty Daniel Vale case and remembered a man who had kept every pair of socks. The letters stayed missing.

At the household-notebook display, the original was gone. A reproduction sat where it had been, clearly marked FACSIMILE, and beside it the museum had added a small temporary card:

ORIGINAL DESTROYED IN COLLECTION INCIDENT.

The reproduction still read:

SAM HOME TODAY. THIN AS A FENCE RAIL. ATE THREE EGGS.

The information survived. The page didn't.

Elizabeth stood there until that stopped feeling like something she needed to solve. Then she left the museum and bought lunch because she was hungry.


Milo's ankle improved enough that the broom handle went back to being a broom handle. His surviving boot stayed by his cot until Mama Baga produced a mismatched replacement from somewhere in Camp. One was brown and one was black, and Milo considered this an upgrade. Stansbury's hand healed more slowly. The dressing grew smaller and the scar grew clearer: a red uneven band across the back of his right hand that disappeared beneath the wrist. He did the stretches the healer gave him, and Pathwell never mentioned changing it again.


On the twelfth day, Camp needed a replacement archive wagon. Pathwell found four. The archivist rejected three from photographs. The fourth was structurally sound, dry, ugly, and painted a shade of yellow that made everyone standing beside it look ill.

"Perfect," the archivist said.

Pathwell stared at him. "You can't mean that."

"I can."

"It resembles a diseased lemon."

"It fits the cabinets."

"Color is also a dimension."

"Not one on my list."

Pathwell looked toward Mama Baga. She shrugged. "His archive."

Pathwell turned back to the wagon. Elizabeth could see an entire better plan forming in him: new paint, better suspension, a reinforced axle, possibly a roof modification no one had requested. Then he was on his back in the dirt underneath it, knocking on the axle with one knuckle as if it might answer.

He slid out, sat up, and rubbed both hands over his face. "Do you want it painted?" he asked the archivist.

"No."

"Suspension checked?"

"Yes."

"Axle reinforced?"

"If the wheelwright says it needs it."

"All right," Pathwell said.

The yellow wagon arrived two days later. Nobody repainted it. Milo named it The Lemon. The archivist objected, and the name survived. Pathwell was the first adult to use it.


Elizabeth spent less time at Camp during the second week. She went back to work. Nobody had held the meeting she'd missed without her. It had been rescheduled for the week she came back, and it turned out to be about the birthday calendar. Somebody asked whether she'd liked the cake. Elizabeth said yes. The half that said ELIZ had gone in her trash the night she got home, and she didn't mention that. She bought groceries. One evening she looked up whether the firm in Chicago still existed. It did. She didn't call them. She didn't close the tab, either. She learned that one of the plants in her apartment had survived being ignored through all of this and the other had not, and she threw out the dead one.

She took a long route home one evening because the weather was good. Nothing magical happened. She liked the walk anyway.

She decided it counted. Her standards for adventure had been set by a diner with terrible coffee, a museum before opening, and one ditch she had chosen herself, though she was willing to file the ditch under poor execution.


At the end of the second week, Milo needed a fourth player again. Elizabeth had come to Camp for dinner. The older woman in the knitted cap was already seated. The archivist had a new deck, still stiff from the box.

Milo pointed at Pathwell. "You."

Pathwell was carrying a box of blank paper toward The Lemon. He stopped. "Me what?"

"Cards."

"I'm working."

The archivist nodded at the box. "Put it inside. Then cards."

"This feels coordinated," Pathwell said to Elizabeth.

"You're popular."

"Unlikely."

He put the box exactly where the archivist pointed, and sat. Mama Baga put a bowl of stew in front of him without asking. He ate it in about four bites, the way he'd eaten the dumplings that first night, and Elizabeth realized she hadn't seen him eat anything since the fire.

Milo dealt. Pathwell picked up his cards and fanned them so wide that the woman in the knitted cap leaned politely away. "Hearts."

"Yes."

"I know Hearts."

Milo narrowed his eyes. "Shade said that."

The table changed. Pathwell studied his cards. "Did he."

"He was bad," Milo said.

Elizabeth said, "Very."

Milo pointed at her. "You weren't playing."

"I observed."

"That doesn't count."

Pathwell arranged his hand. "For once, Milo and I agree on evidentiary standards."

Milo dealt the next card harder than necessary, and the queen of spades appeared in Pathwell's hand. Pathwell looked at it, and then at Milo.

Milo looked innocent enough to convict himself.

Pathwell opened his mouth, and Elizabeth heard the sentence coming in somebody else's voice. This child is cheating.

He closed his mouth. "No," he said.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You're weird."

"Thank you."

They played. Pathwell lost by twenty-six points, and Milo was delighted. "Shade was better."

Pathwell looked at him. "Then I've got work to do."

Milo gathered the cards. "You owe me a boot."

Pathwell looked at Milo's feet, one brown, one black. "Yes."

"I don't want a boot. I got one." Milo squared the deck. "When I need a fourth, you come. Even if you're working."

Pathwell looked at the archivist, who didn't help him. "All right."

Milo held up the deck. "Again?"

Pathwell glanced toward The Lemon, then at Mama Baga. "Am I done for the evening?"

Mama Baga looked offended. "I am not your supervisor."

The archivist said, "You are done."

"Again," Pathwell said.

Elizabeth stayed for the second game because she wanted to see whether he could lose worse. He could.


Early in the third week, a family came into Camp from the north road: two adults, a grandmother, three children and a goat, all of them wearing the look Elizabeth remembered from the inside of her own face.

She was there because Milo had demanded a rematch. She saw Mama Baga start toward them, stop, and turn around.

"Pathwell," Mama Baga said. "Welcome them."

Pathwell put down the box he was carrying and looked at her once, to be sure. She waited. He went.

"Welcome to Camp Cunnan, home of the Cunning Folk," he said, and swept one arm at the wagons the way he had swept it the first night. "Dwelling of charmers, blessers, wizards and witches. Abode of the spaewives and the gwyllion."

He did the whole thing. He remembered the runemasters this time. The smallest child laughed at "spaewives," and he said it again for her, slower, with more spaewife in it.

When he finished, he asked the grandmother whether he could take her bag. She let him, and looked past him at Mama Baga. "Is he always like this?"

"Worse," Mama Baga said, and went to see about the goat.


Elizabeth went to see the museum reopen the damaged gallery on the eighteenth day. A temporary sign at the entrance said it was reopened, not restored.

Two repaired cases stood where broken ones had been. One remained intentionally empty; the curator had refused to fill every absence. The Daniel Vale display used facsimiles and a photograph of the original letters from before their destruction. The household notebook reproduction remained clearly labeled. Margaret Bell's surviving accession cards had been remounted beside scans of the destroyed ones.

Pathwell had paid for the glass, mounts, conservation work, environmental recalibration, and temporary exhibit printing. He had also spent two full days carrying cases, moving barriers exactly where museum staff had marked the floor, and writing an incident account so detailed that Stansbury claimed it qualified as revenge literature.

The curator didn't thank him for causing the work. She did thank him for completing the assigned work.


On the nineteenth day, Stansbury came to Camp with a letter that had arrived at his house. The envelope was addressed to Pathwell in blue ballpoint, in a hand that pressed hard enough to dent the paper.

Pathwell read it standing beside The Lemon, then read it again, and held it out to Elizabeth.

"She said you could," he said. "I wrote her about you. I asked."

Elizabeth took it.

Mr. Pathwell,

I got your letter. I read it twice, which is once more than I meant to.

Don't come here. Don't send money. Don't do anything about Tess. If she wants to write to me she has the address. If she doesn't, that's hers to decide. It always was.

You asked what you could do. Nothing. That's what I'm asking for.

You can show this to your friend. I'd like somebody to know I said it.

Ada Kell

Elizabeth handed it back. Pathwell folded it along its creases and put it in the inside pocket of his coat.


On the twenty-first day, Camp held no ceremony. The Lemon simply became the archive. Blank paper went into one cabinet. Saved originals went into another. The blue road notebook returned to a padded support shelf. The surviving family letters received a new cloth wrap.

The cardboard loss sheet from the morning of the fire went into the first drawer. The archivist had copied it, smudges included, and then filed the original anyway. Near the bottom, in his own handwriting, it still said LIKED POCKETS.

The current route copies went into a box that slid under the rack Pathwell had originally braced three inches too low. It slid out cleanly now. The archivist tested it twice.

Pathwell stood nearby holding a box of accession slips. "Satisfied?"

"No."

Pathwell checked the box under the rack. "What now?"

"Label crooked."

Pathwell stared at the label. It was crooked by perhaps one degree. He put down the accession slips, peeled the label off, and applied a new one. "Better?"

The archivist inspected it. "Yes."

Pathwell nodded, and that was all.

Stansbury still had a scar. Elizabeth still had a shoulder that hurt when rain moved in.

She looked toward the far end of Camp, where the road bent between the trees.

Elizabeth smiled.
