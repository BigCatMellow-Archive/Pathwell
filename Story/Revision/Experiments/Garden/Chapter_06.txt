Chapter Six

It was nicer inside than out. Somebody loved it, in the way people love a place by never changing anything in it. The booths were red vinyl, mended with red tape that didn't quite match. There was a pool table with a cue-shaped dent in the wall beside it, and a dartboard so old it was more cork than board, and a television up in the corner showing a baseball game to nobody with the sound turned off. Over the register hung a stuffed bass on a plaque with a little brass plate under it that said ONE THAT DIDN'T GET AWAY.

The bartender was reading a paperback with the cover torn off and didn't put it down to look at them.

Stansbury was already on a stool. Pathwell was already at the dartboard. Elizabeth took the stool between where they'd both be, if either of them ever sat down.

"What can I get you?"

"Oh." She looked at the bottles behind him. She didn't know what she wanted. She couldn't remember the last time she'd ordered something for herself that wasn't the same thing as last time. "I'm not sure."

"She'll have a—" Pathwell began, from the dartboard.

"No," said Elizabeth.

Pathwell stopped with a dart in the air.

"I haven't said anything yet."

"I know." She turned back to the bartender. "Do you have coffee?"

The bartender looked at her as if nobody had asked him that in some time. "It's been on since this morning."

"Good," said Elizabeth. "That one."

He poured it. It came out thick and black and smelling burnt, and when she tasted it, it was every bit as awful as she'd hoped, and her shoulders came down a full inch.

Pathwell watched her drink it. Then he went back to the darts without saying anything, and she was almost sure he was smiling at the board.

Stansbury looked at her coffee, and then at her, and then down at the bar with the very corner of his mouth tucked in.

"Beer," he said to the bartender. "And whatever he's having, put it on his tab."

"I don't have a tab," said Pathwell.

"Start one."




Nobody bled, or pruned, or was followed by anything. Stansbury got to have his drink sitting down.

He ordered fries. Pathwell said that he didn't want any, and then he wandered over from the dartboard every few minutes and took one, and Stansbury moved the basket six inches further away each time without looking up, and Pathwell reached six inches further. A man at the pool table was losing a game to himself, and taking it hard.

She watched the brothers instead of the game. They argued about everything, the fries and the darts and which team was winning the baseball game neither of them was watching, and neither of them ever raised his voice, and neither of them ever won. It sounded like something they'd been doing so long it had worn smooth, like a step.

Pathwell put three darts into the same thin slice of the board, one, two, three, and yawned.

He threw again, and the dart hit the wall a foot to the left of the board and stuck there in the wood paneling, quivering.

Everybody in the bar looked at it.

Elizabeth looked at his hand. He was flexing the fingers, once, and then again, as though counting them.

He didn't say anything. He went and worked the dart out of the wall, and put all three back in the cup on the bar, and didn't throw again.

Stansbury watched him cross the room. Then he turned on his stool, just a little, toward her.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Okay."

"You came from Camp with him. On your own."

"Yes."

"He doesn't still have something of yours?"

She thought about it. "Two dollars and sixty-five cents."

Stansbury stared at her.

Then he laughed. It came out of him like it had been stuck for a while, a real laugh, loud enough that the man at the pool table looked over, and he put his hand flat on the bar while he did it.

"Good," he said, when he could. "Good. Make him pay you back."

"I intend to."

He wiped his eyes with a knuckle and picked up his beer.

"Most people who meet him come away with opinions," he said. "So do I. About him, and about what you ought to do."

"Are you going to tell me?"

"No."

"Why not?"

He turned his glass a quarter turn on the bar. "Because you're the first person in a long time he hasn't had to talk into anything," he said. "I'd like to see how that goes."




Some time after that, she went looking for the restroom.

It was down a narrow back hall past the kitchen door, lit by one yellow bulb. There was a dead pay phone on the wall, with its cord cut. Beside it, a long mirror, gone dark and spotty around the edges where the silver behind the glass had given up.

Pathwell was standing in front of the mirror.

He hadn't heard her. He was turning, very slowly, at the shoulders. A little to the left. Then a little to the right, and stopping, like a man trying to find a draft. Left again. Right. He was frowning.

She looked at the mirror.

His reflection was frowning too. It turned left when he turned left, and right when he turned right, exactly. Everything was exactly right.

Except his right hand.

His right hand, the real one, hung at his side in a fist, and the knuckles were white. In the mirror, the hand was open. The fingers were spread a little, the palm turned out, toward him, the way you'd hold out your hand to someone you wanted to come with you.

She blinked.

The reflection's hand was a fist.

She hadn't made a sound. But he turned anyway, and saw her there, and for a second he looked at her the way he'd looked at her front door, at four in the morning, when something had been on the other side of it.

"Did you see something?" he said.

She could have said no. It would have been easy, and it would have been kind.

"Your hand," she said. "In the mirror. It was open."

He looked down at his fist. He opened it, slowly. Closed it again.

He looked back at the mirror for a long time. It showed him a tired man under a yellow bulb, in a coat that needed mending, and nothing else.

"Huh," he said, very quietly.

He went past her, back up the hall, and didn't touch her on the way, though the hall was narrow enough that he had to turn sideways not to.




He was back on his stool when she came out, turning a beer mat over and over on the bar with his left hand. His right hand was in his coat pocket.

Stansbury was watching him.

"What?" said Stansbury.

Pathwell looked at Elizabeth, then at the baseball game. "Later."

Stansbury's eyes went past him down the hall, and came back, and his face changed so little you'd have needed to know him a hundred years to see it.

Elizabeth sat down.

"At the school," she said, "your brother said he didn't like the first part."

"He didn't."

"Do you like it now?"

For a second she thought he was going to make a joke. She could see it coming, the way you can see a bus. Then it didn't come.

"It's starting to point somewhere I don't like," he said.

"Where?"

He looked at his hand, in his pocket, through his coat.

"Mine," he said.

Stansbury put his beer down very carefully, as though it might go off.

Behind them, the man at the pool table finally beat himself, and said so.

"What does that mean?" said Elizabeth.

"I don't know yet."

"Tell me," said Stansbury.

Pathwell looked at Elizabeth. Then at his brother. "Not here."

He'd said not here. He hadn't said not in front of her. She decided that counted for something.




Stansbury paid. Two beers, one coffee, one basket of fries, mostly eaten by someone who hadn't wanted any.

"Wait," said Elizabeth.

They both looked at her.

She held out her hand to Pathwell, palm up.

He looked at it. Then, slowly, as though it were a much bigger thing than it was, he reached into his coat pocket and came out with a small handful of coins, and counted them into her palm, one at a time. Two dollars. Two quarters. A dime. A nickel.

"Two sixty-five," he said.

"Thank you."

She turned to the bartender. "How much for the coffee?"

He looked at her. He looked at the coins. "Dollar and a quarter."

She counted it out onto the bar, a dollar and a quarter, and then, after a moment, she put the rest down next to it.

"That's too much," said the bartender.

"It was a good coffee," said Elizabeth.

Stansbury looked at the coins on the bar, and then at his brother, who was looking at them too, as though he'd never seen money before and was finding it more interesting than he'd expected.




Outside, the evening had come down. The sky over the fields had gone orange and then red, long streaks of it, with a bank of cloud standing up tall at the edge of the world and throwing shadows across the corn for miles.

Elizabeth stopped at the bottom of the steps.

"Red sky at night," she said. "My mom used to say that. Means it'll be nice tomorrow."

"Does it work?" said Pathwell.

"Never once."

"But she kept saying it."

"Every time."

He looked at the sky as if he'd been given something. "Good for her."

Then he looked past the Cadillac, to the far side of the lot, where the gravel ended and the tall grass started, and his face went still.

Stansbury followed his look. Then he dug his keys out of his pocket, and after a moment, he held them out. Not to his brother. To her.

"Why me?" said Elizabeth.

"Because I'd like to sit next to him."

He said it plainly, the way he'd said everything, and he didn't look at Pathwell when he said it.

"You can drive?"

She took the keys. "Yes."

"I'm a superb driver," said Pathwell, to the sky.

"You've never had a license," said Stansbury.

"I've never needed one. That's how superb."

The keys were warm from his pocket. Behind them, the screen door banged shut, and the lot went quiet, so quiet she could hear the insects starting up in the grass.

Then, from the far side of the Cadillac, something hit the gravel.

It was wet, and it was heavy.
