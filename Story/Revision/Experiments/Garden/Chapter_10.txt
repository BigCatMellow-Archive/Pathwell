Chapter Ten

She'd been walking a little more than an hour. There wasn't a car in either direction. The fields went off black on both sides to where the trees started, and the only light was the stars, which had come all the way out now and were showing off.

She walked on the left, facing traffic, because her father had told her to when she was eight, and apparently that had stuck when a great many more important things hadn't.

The order slip was in her pocket. The dagger was in the blazer, against her ribs. Her shoes had been bought to stand in an office in, and they had started, a mile ago, to say so.

She thought about Shade's notebook. Elizabeth owes me breakfast. The careful straight letters at the bottom of the page.

She thought about the bar, and Pathwell counting coins into her hand one at a time, as if it had been something much heavier than two dollars and sixty-five cents. The way he'd watched her put them down on the bar and leave them there.

She didn't know what that had been. She thought about it anyway, for a long time, walking.

And she thought about what Shade had said. He knows what he did. Ask him.




Stansbury's house was two miles from the ditch, which had not helped the Cadillac at all.

They walked it, the two of them, each with an arm round his own ribs, so that from a little way off they looked like a pair of old men coming home from a wedding.

"She has no money," said Pathwell.

"I'm aware."

"She has one shoe that's rubbing. I saw her walk."

"You saw her walk away from you. Yes."

They went on a bit.

"The hand," said Stansbury. "That thing that pulls you. Which way is it going?"

Pathwell didn't need to check. "After the car. After him."

"Not after her."

"No."

"Then you leave it alone," said Stansbury. "I mean it. I know you. You're standing there thinking, well, she's in his car, so if I follow him I'm following her. And that's true for about another hour, and after that it isn't, and you won't know when it stopped."

Pathwell didn't say anything, which was as good as saying yes.

"So what's the plan?" said Stansbury.

"I'll think of one."

"You always say that."

"I always do."

"No. You always say it, and then something happens near you, and afterward you call it the plan." Stansbury hitched the box of swords up under his arm. "That isn't a method. That's weather."

A dog barked somewhere a long way off, at nothing, and kept it up for a while, and stopped.

"You've got letters," said Pathwell suddenly. "Haven't you. With longing in them."

"I have a great many letters with longing in them. It's practically a genre."

"Unsent."

Stansbury walked a little way before he answered.

"I might," he said, in the voice of a man who knew exactly which shelf.




Stansbury's house was small and square and white, with a lawn mown in straight stripes and a front door that wasn't locked. Nothing in that town was locked. Everybody knew everybody, so everybody knew better.

Inside, everything had a place. There was a hook for each coat. There was a little dish by the door for keys, and Stansbury reached into his pocket out of habit to put the car keys in it, and then remembered, and stood looking at the empty dish for a moment like a man at a graveside.

He opened the freezer and threw something at his brother without turning round.

Pathwell caught it against his chest and made a noise. "Peas."

"For your ribs."

"What are you having?"

"Corn," said Stansbury, and took it out, and held it to his side.

"You have two bags of frozen vegetables set aside for exactly this."

"I have a brother," said Stansbury.

The garage had been an archive for much longer than it had been a garage. There were steel shelves from the floor to the roof, and on them, rows and rows of gray boxes, every one labeled on the end in Stansbury's square handwriting, with a name, and dates, and what was inside, and a little colored dot that meant something to nobody but him. There wasn't a speck of dust anywhere. It smelled of cardboard and cold concrete and nothing else.

Pathwell stood in the doorway and looked at it with real fear.

"Don't touch anything."

"I wasn't going to."

"Your hand was moving."

Pathwell put his hands behind his back.

Stansbury went down the shelves without looking at any labels, the way you find a light switch in your own house in the dark, and came back with a flat gray box. VARGAS, M. 1958–1964. CORRESPONDENCE, UNSENT. ORIGINALS. COPIED. A green dot.

He opened it on the workbench. The letters were laid flat between sheets of tissue. He lifted them one after another with the tip of a finger until he got to one folded in thirds, with no envelope, the ink faded to the color of tea.

Pathwell stood back from it. He could feel it from where he was. It came across the bench at him the way the smell of bread comes out of a bakery. Somebody at a window, a long time ago, waiting.

"How many?"

"Seventy-one. One a month for six years, and she never posted any of them. He'd married somebody else by the second spring. She knew. She went on writing." Stansbury put the lid back on the rest. "Her grand-nephew sold the whole lot at an estate sale. Two dollars a box. They were in with the Christmas decorations."

"And you copied them."

"Every word. Before I bought them. I always do."

Pathwell looked at the letter. "If I use it, it's gone."

"I know."

"The copy keeps the words."

"Not the thing," said Stansbury.

They looked at it together for a while.

"That's why I copy them," said Stansbury. "You asked me once. You thought it was fussiness." He nudged the letter an inch toward his brother. "It's so that if it has to be spent, the words are somewhere. And so that I'm the one who decides it's worth spending. Not whoever happens to be standing nearest with his hands out."

He pulled a stool from under the bench and pointed at it. Pathwell sat.

"You know how this one goes. It wants something real between the two of you. Something you both had. You hold it out, and if she happens to be holding the same thing at the same time, it catches. Then I'll give you something to steer with. Lists. I keep lists."

"Where?"

"Filed," said Stansbury, offended, and went up the two steps into the house to fetch them.

The bench had a drawer, and the drawer was a little open. Inside were a few things waiting to be filed. On top was a single sheet, in a neat, frightened hand. Pathwell's eyes went down the first few lines without his asking them to. A mother, to a son, about a fever in the house. Don't come home this month. I want you so much. So don't come.

He folded it once along its crease and slid it inside his coat, without quite deciding to, the way a man takes a mint from the bowl by the register.

Then he put two fingers on the Vargas letter, on the fold, and shut his eyes.

It warmed. The first line of it began to let go of the paper, gently, like a guest who knows it's time to leave.

The longing came up through his fingers. A kitchen window in summer. A road. A particular someone who wasn't going to come down it, and the exact shape of the gap where he would have been. Not anyone. Him. Pathwell held the shape of it and didn't try to look inside. It wasn't his to read.

Under his hand, the letter went. Then the page went after it.

Now. Something they'd both had.

He tried the dumplings. The red dragon with its leg out, and her face over the tray, wet. He held it out into the dark.

Nothing.

He tried the cart, stuck on the root, and her shoulder in the back of it. Nothing. Mama Baga's hands on the handle. Nothing. The Space Between, the bad coffee, her face. That's terrible. It slid, and didn't hold.

He went through the night like going through his own pockets. The longing waited.

The bar. The pot that had been on since the morning. Her hand, held out, palm up.

He'd counted them into her hand. Two dollars. Two quarters. A dime. A nickel. And it had felt, for no reason he could have explained to anybody, more like paying something than anything he'd done in fifty years.

It caught.

He felt it take, like a key going round in a lock that's been stiff a long time.

"Stan!"

Above him, a drawer stopped. Stansbury's head came round the door. "What?"

"It's caught."

"Already?" Stansbury came down the steps with two slips of paper. "What on?"

Pathwell opened his eyes. "Two dollars and sixty-five cents."

His brother looked at him for a long moment.

"Well," Stansbury said. "Of course it did."

"She's thinking about it. Right now. Wherever she is."

"She doesn't feel this?"

"Not a thing."

"And you can't reach her through it. Or steer her. Or—"

"No. It's a thread, Stan. That's all. It's not a leash."

"Good," said Stansbury, and Pathwell understood, finally, that his brother had been checking the stove all night, over and over, and that the stove was him. "List."

The first one was on the back of an old electric bill, in pencil worn down to a stub.

BREAD
STAMPS
SHOES (RESOLED) — PICK UP
BANK BEFORE 3
BULBS (2)

There was almost nothing in it. That was why it was good. Somebody had written it to get from one place to the next and then the next and then home, and there was nothing in it but that, a plain forward lean, like a person leaving the house with their coat already half on.

He put his fingers on it. The pencil darkened and then faded, and the going-somewhere came loose with nothing to hold on to, and went looking, and found the thin thread of the coins, and leaned along it.

His head turned. He didn't turn it. He just knew, the way whoever wrote the list had known the bank came after the shoes.

"East," he said.

Stansbury was already reaching for his lanyard.




She reached the water tower and got the order slip out and read it by starlight. The waitress had underlined the second left twice, and drawn the water tower, so there could be no mistake. She took the second left.

It was a narrower road. A ditch on each side and the telephone wires sagging from pole to pole.

Half a mile on, a dog came out of a farm gate. It was big and yellow and old, and it didn't bark. It just fell in beside her, on the road side, as if it had been waiting all night for somebody to walk with, and they went along together, the two of them, not saying anything.

At the edge of the next field it stopped, at some line she couldn't see, and sat down.

She looked back at it. It looked at her.

"Thanks," she said.

It thumped its tail once on the road, and stayed where it was, and watched her go on without it.




Stansbury's lanyard had a key for every door in the school, and one for the school's activity bus.

It was short and yellow, and it said EAGLES down the side in blue, and inside it had eleven rows of very small seats. Stansbury drove it with total seriousness. Pathwell sat in the front seat holding the peas to his neck and the second list on his knee, and every time they hit a bump the whole bus rang like a dinner bell, and something that had been left on it after a field trip came rolling down the aisle from the back.

It was a juice box.

"Is this allowed?" said Pathwell.

"I'm authorized to move the vehicle."

"For what?"

"School purposes."

Pathwell looked round at the eleven rows of tiny seats. "And this is a school purpose."

"Somebody," said Stansbury, "is about to learn something."

At the first crossroads, the lean went soft and then went out.

"Stop."

Stansbury stopped. Pathwell turned over the second list.

SOAP
STRING
COFFEE
PEARS
LAMP OIL

He looked at his brother.

"Don't," said Stansbury.

"I didn't say a word."

"You were about to say 'coffee' to me in a meaningful voice."

Pathwell put his fingers on the list, and the words went one by one from the top, soap, string, coffee, and the next-errand feeling came loose, and found the thread again, and leaned.

"Left," he said. "Soon. There's something tall."

"Water tower."

"That'll do."




She heard it a long way off: a high, rattling engine, like a lawnmower that had been given ideas. She got over onto the shoulder. Headlights came up behind her and slowed, and went past on the far side of the road, and she saw the side of it go by, yellow, and EAGLES in blue.

It stopped, with a hiss. The front doors folded open.

Pathwell came down the steps. He had a bag of something frozen against his neck, and he set it down on the bus's bumper and then had to keep his hand on the bus a moment before he could walk.

He looked worse than she did. She hadn't known that was possible.

"Hi," he said.

"Hi."

She looked at the bus. Behind the enormous flat wheel, Stansbury raised one hand to her, gravely, like an admiral from a bridge.

"How did you find me?"

He looked back at the bus. Then he made himself look at her.

"A letter that should have been sent," he said. "Two grocery lists. And two dollars and sixty-five cents."

She stared at him.

"Did you put that in my head?"

"No." Fast. Then slower. "It doesn't go that way. It can only catch on something you're already holding. I had to keep holding things up until I found the one you had. I held up a lot of things. That was the one."

She thought about that for a while, standing there in the road.

"I checked," Stansbury called from the driver's seat. "Several times."

"He did," said Pathwell.

She looked at Pathwell. He was doing the thing he did, watching her face for the shape of what was coming so he could be there first, with an answer ready.

She didn't give him a shape.

"Tell me what you did," she said.

He went quiet.

"Shade says you know. I'm not asking if you think it's a good time. I'm not asking if you think I can take it. I'm asking you what you did."

His hands went into his pockets. "Lizzy—"

"Don't."

He stopped.

"Elizabeth," he said.

"I'm not the part of this that gets explained around," she said. "Not anymore."

He looked at her a long time.

"Okay," he said.

She went past him and climbed up into the bus and sat down in the second row, in a seat built for a seven-year-old, with her knees nearly up to her chin. Stansbury watched her in his big mirror, and his face did something that, on anyone else, would have been a smile.

Pathwell came up the steps and sat across the aisle from her with the peas.

"Camp," he said, to his brother.

"No," said Elizabeth.

Stansbury turned the engine off.

"Not later," she said. "Not when you've thought of a nicer way to put it. Now."

The bus ticked. Outside the windows there was nothing but stars and one sagging wire.

Pathwell sat for a long time with his hands around the bag of peas. When he started, he didn't look at her. He looked at the back of the little seat in front of him, which had initials scratched in it, B.K. + T.R., and somebody had tried to scratch the T.R. out.

And he told it.

He told it all the way through, without stopping, in an even, careful voice, the way you might read out something you'd written down a long time ago and gone over and over since. There'd been a woman called Ada Kell. An old woman with bad hands, in a kitchen with a yellow oilcloth on the table, cherries on it, and she'd held a cup of tea between her hands the whole time he'd been there and never drunk from it. Her girl had gone. Tess. At nineteen, and three years without a word. Ada wanted to know if she was alive. And if she was, Ada wanted her told, if it could be managed, that her mother wasn't angry. That was all she asked for. That was all.

He'd found Tess in a day. Waiting tables two states over, doing fine, better than fine. He'd told her what her mother said. Every word. She'd listened to all of it, and she hadn't wanted to come home.

So he'd gone back to Ada's kitchen. And he'd sat down at that table across from her, and looked at her, at her hands round the cup. And she was old. And Tess might come round in ten years, or she might not, and Ada might have ten years, or she might not.

"And I did the sum," he said. "For both of them. Sitting there. I just did it."

Nobody had asked him to. That was the part he said slowest. Ada hadn't asked him for anything but news. Tess hadn't asked him for anything at all. He hadn't asked either of them if they'd like him to do anything more. He'd known the quickest way through, he'd thought, and it was his own to pay, so he'd paid it.

He'd made a working. A pull, toward home. The kind of working that would take what he had in place of money, if he let it, and he'd let it. That was the prune. That was all a prune was. Paying with what you might have been.

And Tess had got on a bus the next morning, and couldn't have told anyone why. She'd stayed three days. Then she'd gone again, and that time she'd never written, not once, not ever.

"Before that," said Pathwell, to the scratched initials, "Ada could hope. That one day her girl might come home because she wanted to." He was quiet a moment. "I spent that. For her. She didn't ask me to."

The bus was very quiet. Stansbury sat with both hands on the big wheel and looked straight out through the windshield at nothing.

"And it didn't all go where I sent it," said Pathwell. "Some did. Not all. There was more left over than there should have been. A pull, with nowhere left to pull to. I looked for it. I couldn't find all of it. I couldn't put it back." He looked at his own hands, at last. "And then there was Shade."

Elizabeth sat with it.

She'd thought, when he started, that she'd have a hundred questions. She found she only had a few.

"Shade remembers that table," she said. "The cup."

His hands stopped moving on the bag. "Does he."

"Did you make him?"

"What I chose made the mess he came out of." He said it slowly, setting each word down where he wanted it. "That much is mine. How a mess turns into a man, I don't know. I don't think anybody does."

"Would you do it again?"

"I wouldn't know how."

"That's not what I asked you."

He looked across the aisle at her, finally.

"No," he said. "I wouldn't."

"And my door. The first one."

"Came for me. Or for him, coming after me. Not for you." He didn't look away. "It was never for you. I told you your home was marked because I'd already decided what was happening before I'd looked. The same as Ada's table. I decided, and then I went and looked for reasons."

She thought of the diner. For him to stop deciding what I am, before I get the chance.

"He thinks you'll decide for him too," she said. "What he is. What happens to him."

"I know."

"Knowing's not the same as not doing it."

"No," said Pathwell. "It isn't."

"Because you paid," she said slowly, working it out as she went, "you thought that made it yours to pick. That's how it works, with you. You pay, so you choose."

He didn't say anything.

She waited. Stansbury waited.

"At the time," said Pathwell, "I'd have called that being responsible."

"And now?"

"I don't know what I'd call it now."

"Yes, you do," said Elizabeth. "You know enough. What happened after, the part that turned into him, you didn't choose that. But you chose the rest of it."

He looked down at the peas. Then back up at her.

"Yes," he said.

She let her breath go. It felt like putting down something she'd been carrying since four o'clock that morning.

Stansbury turned round in the driver's seat. "So. Where?"

Pathwell turned toward him to answer, the way he always had.

"Camp," said Elizabeth.

They both looked at her. She'd thought Pathwell would get there first. He didn't.

"Why Camp?" said Stansbury.

She looked out at the dark.

"Because Mama Baga isn't yours," she said. "Either of yours. And Camp isn't anybody's. And nobody there has asked me for one thing I didn't want to give."

Stansbury looked at her in the mirror a while. Then he nodded, once.

"Camp."

"There's no door to Camp from out here," said Pathwell. "Not one I'd trust."

"The ticket stubs."

"They take you where you need to go, not where you'd like. Last time it was under some bleachers."

She waited.

He heard himself, and stopped, and began again more carefully. "The nearest one I'd trust is in Marlow. At the museum. There's a wall there. If it's still got enough in it, it'll hold a door."

Stansbury started the engine. The whole bus rang.

"You know it?" said Elizabeth.

"Unfortunately," said Stansbury. "He has a key."

"Why does he have a key?"

"Ask him."

She looked across the aisle. Pathwell was looking out of his window.

"Later," she said. "I'll ask him later."

The bus turned east, rattling, and nobody corrected her.
