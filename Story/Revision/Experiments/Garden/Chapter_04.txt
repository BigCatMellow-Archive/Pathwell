Chapter Four

The bookstore's back door opened onto a dirt road in the dark, which Elizabeth decided not to think about.

Pathwell pulled the handcart. He pulled it with his left hand, and kept his right tucked against his chest, and every time a wheel found a rut the whole cart lurched and he muttered at the wheel. Elizabeth walked beside it with one hand on her books.

The road went down between trees. It was cold, the clean kind of cold that comes before dawn, and it smelled like leaves and water and, a little way off, woodsmoke.

At the bottom of the hill the cart hit a root and stopped dead, and Pathwell kept going for a step without it and had to come back.

He looked at the root. The root looked back.

Elizabeth went around behind the cart and put her shoulder into it. He pulled and she pushed, and the cart rolled up and over, and the crate with the twine slid an inch and settled.

He looked back at her, and seemed about to say something, and didn't.

"You're welcome," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"I know."

The smoke got stronger. Then there was lantern light through the trees, and a fiddle somewhere, playing something that couldn't make up its mind whether to be a dance, and the road opened out into a clearing full of wagons.

They stood in a ring, painted once, a long time ago, in colors that had since become suggestions. In the middle was a big fire, and smaller fires off it wherever people had wanted to sit. Washing hung between two wagons. Someone was playing a fiddle badly, and someone else was singing along worse, on purpose. Two children were asleep on a pile of coats by the fire, and a third was very much awake, and on a log, poking the coals with a stick to see what happened, which was sparks.

It was four-thirty in the morning and nobody here seemed to have heard.

A woman came out of the nearest wagon before the cart had stopped. She was old and small and brown as a walnut, with a red shawl around her shoulders and a scarf over her hair, and she came straight across the ring to Pathwell, and took the handle of the cart out of his hand.

She didn't ask. She just took it.

Then she took his right wrist in her other hand and held it up into the lantern light and looked at it, at the arm, shaking, without a mark on it.

"Mama," Pathwell said. "Hello."

"Sit down."

"I'm fine."

"Sit down before you fall down and I have to step over you."

He sat on the edge of the cart. Elizabeth had never seen him do what he was told before, and found she had opinions about it.

People were coming. Three or four of them, and then more, from the wagons and from the fire, and nobody asked what was on the cart, or who it was for, or why. They just started taking things off it. A woman lifted the stack of good blank paper and held it against her chest with her eyes shut, like it was a cat she'd lost.

"We were nearly out," she said, to nobody. "We were writing on the backs of things."

A boy came past with a basket of turnips, eleven or so, all elbows, with one sock fallen down around his ankle. He put the turnips on the ground, hitched the sock up with his other heel, failed, and joined in anyway.

Elizabeth took her hand off her books and picked up a bundle of letters.

Nobody asked her to. There was a cart, and things on it, and people carrying them, and she knew how to do that.

"Not those," said the turnip boy. "Those go to the archive."

"Where's the archive?"

He pointed. She went. When she came back, he was waiting with another bundle and gave it to her without a word, as if they'd done this before.

The old woman watched her go and come back, go and come back. Then she bent and got her shoulder under the crate with the twine, the one Pathwell had made such a production of, and stood up with it, and walked off with it toward a wagon, and didn't seem to notice she was carrying anything.

When she came back without it, she stood in front of Pathwell with her hands folded.

"Nobody asked you for this."

"You needed it."

"We did." She looked toward the woman with the paper. "That is not the same thing."

"It's close."

"Next time, ask."

"Next time I'll ask."

"You said that last time."

The old woman looked up at the sky for a moment, as if it might take her side, the way people do when they've been having the same argument for forty years and have decided, again, not to win it tonight.

Then she looked at Elizabeth.

"And this?"

"Elizabeth," Pathwell said. "She came with the books."

"The books came with me," said Elizabeth.

The old woman's mouth moved at one corner.

"Mama Baga," she said, and held out her hand, and Elizabeth shook it. It was dry and hard and very warm. "You are cold. Inside."

"I'm fine, really, I can—"

"Inside," said Mama Baga, and Elizabeth took her books off the cart and went inside.




The wagon was warmer than anywhere she'd been all night. It smelled of tea and woodsmoke and wool, and of something green and dried that hung in bundles from the roof, and there were jars along every shelf with labels on them she couldn't read, in a writing that might not have been letters. Quilts were piled everywhere. At the far end a blanket hung on a line, partway across, like a door somebody hadn't finished shutting.

"Sit."

Elizabeth sat on a little bench under the window, with her knees nearly up to her chin. Mama Baga put a cup of tea in her hands. It was hot and dark and sweet and very good, and it was such a relief to be handed something that wasn't terrible that her eyes stung.

She drank it. Her books were on her knees, the diary and the cookbook, one on the other.

Behind the blanket, somebody breathed in, sharp and shallow.

Elizabeth looked up.

There was a cot back there. A young woman lay on it under two blankets, about Elizabeth's age, maybe younger. Her face had no color in it at all. One arm lay on top of the blankets, and the fingers were curled, and they didn't move.

Beside the cot, a thin woman with her sleeves pushed up past the elbows sat on a stool with two fingers on the young woman's wrist. There was a basin of water at her feet with a cloth in it.

"What happened?" Elizabeth said.

"Wagon came down off the north grade," said Mama Baga. "She was between it and a tree."

The healer took her fingers away from the girl's wrist and put the back of her hand to her cheek, and kept it there a while.

"I can hold her where she is," she said. "That's all. What I had that would help, I've used. There's nothing left in the wagon that's right for this."

Mama Baga nodded.

Elizabeth looked at the basin. The water in it had stopped steaming a long time ago. Before she knew she was going to, she was up, with her books under her arm, and had the basin, and was asking where the water was. Mama Baga pointed at a kettle on a little iron stove. Elizabeth emptied the basin out the door and filled it again and brought it back, and put it down by the healer's foot, and wrung out the cloth and handed it to her.

The healer looked at her, and took it.

"Thank you."

Elizabeth sat back down on the little bench. The cookbook was on her knees again. She hadn't noticed taking it out from under her arm.

The young woman breathed in, a little, and out.

Nobody looked at the cookbook. Not the healer, not Mama Baga. Nobody said anything about it at all, and that was somehow louder than if they had.

There were no rubber bands on it anymore. They were somewhere on her living room floor, with the marker. Elizabeth opened it.

It fell open where it always did, where the spine had been bent back a thousand times. You could tell how Nana had felt on the day by how she'd written. Neat and upright on a good day. Leaning hard to the right when she was in a hurry. And always, everywhere, the margins full of everything she'd thought of after.

She turned past the pie. Past the Jell-O. Past the place where the sugar cookies had been.

Chicken and dumplings.

There was a grease spot by the title the size of a thumbprint, and under the part about the broth, in pencil, pressed in hard:

MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH.

Elizabeth was nine, and had chickenpox, and was lying on Nana's couch under the afghan with the scratchy fringe, in socks with oven mitts taped over her hands so she couldn't scratch. Nana brought the bowl in on a tray and sat on the coffee table and held the spoon, because you can't hold a spoon in an oven mitt. She didn't say anything about eating. She just waited, with the spoon, until Elizabeth opened her mouth.

The page was paper. Just paper, a little soft at the corners.

Elizabeth shut the book.

"No," she said.

Nobody moved.

She opened it again.

"Sorry," she said, quieter. "That one was for me."

She held the cookbook out to the healer, open to the page.

"Here."

The healer looked at it, and at her. "I only need the page."

"Take the book."

"I don't need the book."

"She wrote more if they're sick," Elizabeth said. Her voice was steady, which surprised her. "She never sent anybody home with half a pot in her life. Take the book."

Mama Baga was up off her stool and had it first. She took it in both hands, underneath, the way you'd take a baby somebody was handing you, and held it a moment.

Elizabeth's hands stayed up in the air a second after it had gone, still holding the shape of it.

Mama Baga gave it to the healer, and it was out of Elizabeth's reach.

That was the choice.

Everything afterward was consequence.

The healer laid the cookbook open on the blanket beside the girl. She read the page through once, and then again, slower, moving her lips. Then she put one hand flat on the paper and the other very lightly on the girl's breastbone.

The pencil warmed.

There was no flash this time, no gold. The page just started to give off heat, the way a kitchen does in winter when something's been on the stove all afternoon. Then the smell came. Broth, and black pepper, and flour on the back of a hand, and the soft, heavy smell of the dumplings when you first take the lid off.

Behind her, Mama Baga breathed in.

The girl's shoulders came down off her ears. Her chest rose, and fell, and rose again, and the second time it went deeper.

On the page, Nana's pencil went dark, and then faint, and then it went away a line at a time, from the top down, and the paper went with it. Not burned. Just used.

The girl took a breath that lifted the blankets. Her fingers uncurled on top of them, slowly, one at a time.

The healer kept her hands where they were for a long while after the page was gone. Then she sat back.

"She'll sleep," she said.

"And then?"

"And then she wakes up and wants to know who's paying for the wagon."

Elizabeth let her breath out. It came out nearly as a laugh.

The wagon smelled of chicken and dumplings for a little longer. Then it smelled like a wagon again.

The healer closed the cookbook. There was a gap in it now where two pages had been. She held it a moment on her knee, and Elizabeth's hand came up off her lap to take it back, all on its own, the way your hands go out when somebody passes you a plate.

Then she remembered what she'd said.

The healer looked at Mama Baga. "Archive?"

Mama Baga looked at Elizabeth.

Elizabeth put her hand back in her lap.

"Archive," she said.




Mama Baga didn't say anything about it. She looked at Elizabeth for a while instead, and then at her coat, and then down at the pajama bottoms coming out underneath it. They had sheep on them.

She went to a trunk under the quilts and came back with her arms full.

"Try these. Take what you like."

"Oh, no, I couldn't—"

"You could."

She hung a quilt off a hook to make a corner, and put the clothes in Elizabeth's arms, and that was the end of the discussion.

When Elizabeth came out, she had on a pair of loose linen trousers the color of weak tea, a pale yellow blouse with tiny buttons, and a short blazer that had belonged to someone with longer arms. She'd tied her hair back with a green bandana. Her pajamas were rolled up under her arm.

Mama Baga looked her over, and then looked again, with her head on one side.

"Something is missing."

She reached up behind her own neck and undid a thin gold chain, and held it out.

"I can't take that," said Elizabeth. "It's yours."

"Hush." Mama Baga stepped in close and put it around Elizabeth's neck and did up the clasp herself, with her small hard fingers, and stepped back. "It is not a loan."

Elizabeth put her hand up to it. It was still warm.

Mama Baga reached up and tucked a loose piece of hair back under the bandana. "There," she said. "Now you look like you came here on purpose."

Elizabeth looked down at herself. Nothing went with anything. She found she didn't mind.




The healer had wrapped the cookbook in a square of clean muslin and put it on the stool by the door. Elizabeth picked it up on her way out. Nobody stopped her.

"I'll take it," she said. "I know where it is."

The archive was a long wagon on the far side of the ring, close enough to the fire to be warm and far enough from the sleeping wagons that nobody had to lie awake beside a hundred years of other people's letters. The door was open. Inside, it was all drawers and shelves, floor to roof, and on them were boxes and ledgers and bundles of letters and loose pages pressed between boards. The bundles she'd carried in from the cart were stacked by the door, waiting.

At a narrow desk, a man was writing. He was somewhere past sixty, long and bent, the shape of a question mark, as if he'd spent so long reading small print that he'd stopped bothering to straighten up between pages.

"You brought the letters," he said, without looking up. "Thank you. Milo dropped two."

From somewhere behind a shelf, the turnip boy said, "I did not."

"Milo dropped two," the archivist said again, and went on writing.

Elizabeth put the cookbook on the corner of the desk.

He finished his line. Then he looked at the muslin, and at her, and put down his pen.

"Donated?"

"Yes."

He took a card from a drawer.

"Your name."

"Elizabeth."

He wrote it.

"And the writer?"

"My grandmother."

He waited, pen up. When nothing else came, he nodded and wrote, in small square capitals, HER GRANDMOTHER, and seemed satisfied with that.

He undid the muslin. He opened the cover and turned the pages, all the way through, carefully, the way a doctor goes down a spine, and stopped at the two gaps and made two small marks on his card. Then he went on to the end.

At the end, he stopped. He tipped the book toward the lamp, and tipped it a little further.

"These last ones have no ink," he said.

"The marker ran out."

"Hm." He tilted it again, and she watched him read it, the pressed-in shapes of Nana's last recipes, word by word, in the slant of the lamp. "She didn't stop."

"No," said Elizabeth. "She didn't."

He wrote something on the card. She couldn't see what. Then he wrapped the cookbook again in fresh paper, folding the corners in square, and wrote on the paper too, and handed it to the turnip boy.

"Reserve shelf, Milo."

Milo carried it three steps across the wagon, holding it in both hands, and put it on a shelf about level with his chin, near the front, where somebody his height could reach it.

Elizabeth watched it sit there.

"What happens to it now?"

"It stays here until somebody needs it."

"And if nobody does?"

"Then it stays here." He looked at her over the desk, for the first time with something like interest. "People think an archive is where things go when everyone's finished with them." He dipped his pen. "It's where they go to wait."

A man put his head in at the door. "Olan wants to cross at the south bank this morning. He says it's fine."

The archivist didn't answer. He reached up without looking, took down a narrow blue notebook with its corners worn white, opened it to a page marked with a strip of red cloth, and turned it around on the desk.

The handwriting changed three times going down the page, three different people over who knew how many years, and in the margin, sideways, in somebody's angry capitals: DO NOT TRUST THE SOUTH BANK AFTER THREE DAYS OF RAIN, NO MATTER WHAT HESS SAYS.

The man read it. "Who's Hess?"

"Nobody, for twenty years." The archivist held the notebook out. "Show Olan."

The man took it and went. Milo came out from behind the shelf, watched him go, and looked at the archivist.

"He's going to say Hess was wrong."

"He's going to say it from the north bank," said the archivist, and went back to his card.

Elizabeth laughed, quietly, and then she went out, and left the cookbook where it was.




Pathwell had gone from the cart. She found him at the edge of the ring, where the lantern light ran out and the trees started, standing very still and looking into the dark. He'd gone still the way he had in her doorway, before he'd told her the apartment was marked.

Mama Baga was beside him with her arms folded.

"It was already at the door," Mama Baga said, "before you used the cookbook?"

"Yes."

"And after the working broke?"

"It went for the waste. That part made sense."

"And the first part does not."

He didn't say anything.

"Stansbury," said Mama Baga.

He let out a long breath through his nose. "I know."

"He should have been the first place you went."

"I had things to bring."

"You had things you decided to bring."

"They were writing on the backs of things, Mama."

"Then they would have written on the backs of things a little longer," said Mama Baga, "and you would have both your arms."

She reached up and straightened his collar, both sides, with two sharp tugs, as though he were twelve and had dressed himself in the dark.

"There," she said.

Elizabeth came up beside them. "Who's Stansbury?"

Pathwell looked at Mama Baga. Mama Baga looked at Pathwell.

"My brother," he said.

"Do you two get along?"

"We're brothers," said Pathwell.

"No," said Mama Baga, pleasantly.

"Thank you, Mama."

"Go and see your brother."

He looked once more into the trees, and then he turned to Elizabeth. "You can stay here," he said. "You'd be all right here. Better than all right."

She looked back at the ring. The fire had burned down to orange. Somebody had found Milo's turnips and was peeling them, badly, with a pocketknife. The fiddle had found its tune. The man with the blue notebook was standing by a wagon, holding it open under somebody's nose, and the somebody was presumably Olan. The archive door was open, and the lamp was on.

For a minute she could see it. Staying. Back in the city it would be getting light by now, and she'd have to be up in two hours for work, and she found she was thinking about it the way you think about the weather somewhere else.

Then she looked at Pathwell.

"You still have my two dollars and sixty-five cents," she said.

He opened his mouth. He shut it again.

Behind them, Mama Baga laughed, one short bark, and went back toward the fire.

"And something came to my door," she said. "Before you did anything. I want to know why."

Pathwell looked at Elizabeth for a long moment.

"All right," he said.

Pathwell started down the path into the trees, and Elizabeth went after him, with her hand on the gold chain, and her diary in her blazer pocket, and nothing else to carry.
