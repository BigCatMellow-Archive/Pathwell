Chapter Seventeen

By the third day, Pathwell had found out that making things right involved a great many more tape measures than he'd expected.

He'd built a cabinet. It stood under a patched canvas awning next to the black ribs of the old archive, and it was oak, and it was square, and it had eight drawers, and every one of the drawers ran in and out on its runners with a sound like a contented cat. He was, Elizabeth could see from across the ring, very proud of it.

The archivist walked all the way round it. Then he pulled out the top drawer, all the way, and looked into it.

"Too deep," he said.

"It holds more."

"It holds more at the back," said the archivist, "where nobody will look for forty years, and where a thing will slide down behind the others and be lost until somebody pulls the whole cabinet away from the wall. Shallow. Two inches. So you can see everything in it at once."

"Two inches? It'll take three times as many drawers."

"Yes."

Pathwell looked at the cabinet. He looked at the archivist. He looked, for a moment, as if he were about to explain to him about cabinets.

Then he went and got the saw.

Milo was sitting on an upturned bucket nearby with his foot strapped up on another bucket, watching with the deep contentment of a child who is about to see a grown man be wrong in public. Elizabeth sat beside him with her coffee.

"He nearly argued," Milo whispered.

"I know."

"I can hear both of you," said Pathwell, over the saw.

"Good," said Milo.




The first week was mostly work that nobody could make interesting, and Pathwell found that very difficult.

The blue road notebook spent three days pressed between blotting paper, drying, while the archivist and two of the Camp women copied the routes out of it onto fresh paper. Pathwell was allowed to help after the third day, once he'd washed his hands to the archivist's satisfaction and been given one pencil and one page.

"Copy what it says," said the archivist.

"I know how to copy."

"Mistakes too."

"Some of these are very clearly mistakes."

"The clear ones especially."

Pathwell looked down at the page he'd been given.

DO NOT TRUST THE SOUTH BANK AFTER THREE DAYS OF RAIN, NO MATTER WHAT HESS SAYS.

And under it, in another hand, very small:

HESS SAYS FOUR.

Pathwell put down the pencil. "This isn't information. This is two dead men arguing on a page."

"Copy it," said the archivist, without looking up.

He copied it out. The pencil wobbled on the long strokes, because his hand still did, and when he'd finished he looked at the H and the S and went back over them, carefully, until they leaned the same way as the dead man's.

She'd gone home on the second day, to her brown door. On the fifth she came back, because she wanted to, and because getting to Camp meant asking Pathwell to open the way for her, and she wanted to see whether he'd do it without turning it into a favor.

He did it without making it into a favor.

She found him at the copying table, writing out HESS SAYS FOUR, letter for letter, small, cramped, exactly as it was.

She looked over his shoulder. "You missed the smudge."

He looked up at her. "The smudge is not part of the text."

"Copy the smudge," said the archivist, from the next table.

"I am living," Pathwell told her, "under a very harsh government."

"You burned down their library."

He thought about that. "It's a proportionate government."

He copied the smudge.

She stayed for lunch. After it, the archivist gave her a stack of the finished copies and the blue notebook to check them against, and she went through them one-handed at the end of the table, line by line. In the fourth one, somebody had changed 4 MILES TO THE FORD into 6 MILES TO THE FORD.

She turned the page round and pushed it across to Pathwell.

"It's six," said Pathwell. "It's six miles. I've walked it. Twice. In the rain."

"Then write that underneath," said the archivist, from the next table, without looking up. "In your own hand. With your name. Don't change his four."

Pathwell opened his mouth. Then he shut it, and took a clean sheet, and copied the page again, with the four in it, and underneath, small, in his own handwriting: SIX, I THINK. P. And then, after a moment, he crossed out I THINK, and then he wrote it in again.




It took longer for her apartment to feel like hers than it took Camp to build a new archive, and she resented that. She'd always thought that after something huge, the little things would stop counting. They didn't. They stayed exactly as little as they'd always been, and went on counting.

The landlord had put in a new front door. It was brown and heavy and completely without personality, and the lock stuck when it was damp. He'd said nothing at all about the old door, or the smell, or what had been all over the walls, except that the insurance had covered it, and that in his experience, these things happened to everybody once.

She hadn't asked him what he meant by that. She thought she might, one day.

The power company wanted an account number she didn't have. There were three boxes labeled KITCHEN and only one of them was kitchen. The dry cleaner, when she finally went in, wanted photo identification and a description of the gray suit and a long, suspicious conversation, before agreeing there had ever been a gray suit in the first place.

She cleaned. She'd thought it would take days. It took one, because the blob, whatever else it was, turned out to have left behind nothing but a smell and a stain on the ceiling shaped a little like a sleeping dog, and both of them washed off.

On her knees with a bucket, reaching under the couch with the brush, she touched something hard and round, and pulled it out.

A blue marker. The cap still on.

She sat back on her heels and looked at it.

She took the cap off. She put it to the back of her hand and drew a line, and there was no line. It had been dry for years. She'd always known that. She drew another line anyway, pressing, and a white scratch came up on her skin and faded.

She capped it again and put it in her pocket.

That afternoon she found a drawer for the spoon.

It was the drawer to the left of the stove, the one she'd been keeping the takeout menus in, and she took the menus out and put them in the trash and put Nana's wooden spoon in it, by itself, and looked at it.

Then she took it out again and stood it in a jar on the counter by the stove, where she'd see it.

Then she put it back in the drawer.

It was a silly decision, and she made it three times.

It was still hers.

She put the blue marker in the drawer beside it.

On Sunday she made chicken and dumplings. There wasn't a book to make them from anymore, so she made them out of what was in her hands and her nose, standing at the stove, stirring with Nana's spoon. The dumplings were heavy. She put in too much pepper, and didn't take any of it back. And there was far too much of it, a whole pot, the way there'd always been.

She ate a bowl standing up. Then she filled a second bowl, and covered it with a plate, and carried it carefully down two flights of stairs and knocked on the landlord's door. He opened it in his slippers and looked at the bowl, and then at her, as though nobody had ever in his life brought him anything that wasn't a complaint. "It's chicken," said Elizabeth. "There's too much. There always is." He took it. He said, "The radiator's been very quiet lately," for no reason she could see, and then looked embarrassed, and shut the door, and she went back upstairs laughing.

Then, before any of it could leave her, she got an index card and a pen out of the box marked DESK and wrote the recipe down. In her own handwriting, which leaned to the right, like Nana's, which she'd never noticed before. She wrote MORE IF THEY'RE SICK at the bottom, under the broth. She looked at it a while. Then she added, smaller: THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH. And then, because she was Nana's granddaughter, and somebody had to: WATCH THE PEPPER, LIZZY.

She put the card in her coat pocket, and kept it there.

Her hand still went to her right side sometimes, to the pocket where the diary used to ride, and found it empty. It happened less, by the second week. Some days it went up to the gold chain at her throat instead, and found that there.

She unpacked another box.




She went back to work on the Monday after.

Nobody had noticed she'd been gone, quite. Somebody had marked her down as sick. Somebody else had covered for her in the meeting. When she asked, carefully, what the meeting at nine had been about, a woman named Diane from the third floor told her it had been about whether the office should get cake for new people as a general policy, since the last party had gone so well.

Elizabeth went into the bathroom and laughed until she had to sit down on the closed lid of the toilet, with her good hand over her mouth.

She answered six emails out of forty-seven. The rest could wait.

She mailed a check to the farmer for his fence post, with a letter she rewrote four times. She did not send anything to Stansbury for the Cadillac. When she offered, at Camp, he looked at her very kindly and said it was on his brother's tab, and that he was enjoying keeping that tab more than he'd ever enjoyed owning the car.




The museum asked her for a statement.

Not Pathwell. The museum. The curator wrote to her directly, in a careful, old-fashioned hand on museum letterhead, and asked if she would be willing to describe what she'd seen in the back gallery, as someone who had been present and was not Pathwell.

So Elizabeth sat at a little side table in the damaged room with a pad of lined paper, writing it out one-handed, while across the room Pathwell sat at a folding table in front of three stacks of forms and the curator.

The curator was very old and very small and very upright, in a cardigan the color of oatmeal, and she looked at Pathwell the way a schoolteacher looks at a boy who has been sent to her for the fourth time.

"I remember you," she said.

"I was hoping you wouldn't."

"You slept in my tent."

"It was a very good tent."

"It was," said the curator. "That's why it's in a museum." She tapped the first stack of paper. "Damages. Cases, mounts, the rail, the floorboards, the conservation assessment, the cleaning. Second: what was lost. Third, your account of what happened." She slid a pen across the table. "Handwritten."

Stansbury was in a chair against the wall, his right hand in a lighter bandage now, doing the finger exercises the healer had given him, one finger at a time. "I like her," he said.

"You weren't invited," said Pathwell.

"I'm a witness. I was hurt."

"Not here," said Elizabeth, without looking up from her pad. "You got hurt somewhere else."

"It's a series," said Stansbury.

Pathwell had picked up the pen. He was halfway down the first page. He stopped, and capped the pen, and put it down on the table where the curator could see it, and looked at her.

"The cases are paid for," he said.

"Paid isn't done."

He thought about it. "No," he said. "It isn't."

The curator moved to the second pile. "The nurse's book. Torn in two along the binding. The halves are with the conservator. She'll be able to stitch it, she thinks, and it'll be readable, and it will never be the same book. The quilt. Torn down the middle through one square. The square says Ada Lowe, in blue thread. The textile conservator thinks she can mend the tear so that you'd have to know it was there to see it." She looked at him over her glasses. "You'll know it's there."

"Yes."

"That's not something you can pay for."

"No."

He reached into his coat. Elizabeth watched him do it from across the room, and her pen stopped.

He took out a small notebook, very old, bound in brown cloth, about the size of a deck of cards.

"Another nurse," he said. "Same war. Another hospital. She kept a list too. Of the men she wrote for. I found it. It's genuine."

The curator didn't touch it.

"Same war," said Pathwell. "Same kind of record. It would fill the case, while the other one's—"

He stopped.

He looked down at the little brown book in his own hand. Then across the room, at the empty case where the first notebook had been.

"No," he said. "It isn't hers. It's someone else's."

"That's right," said the curator.

He held it a moment longer. "Could I give it to you anyway? Not instead. Just as itself."

The curator considered him. "After we've looked into where it came from," she said. "And not instead of anything."

"Not instead of anything."

He put it on the table between them and took his hand off it.

Stansbury had stopped in the middle of an exercise. Pathwell saw him. "Don't look at me like that."

"I'm checking whether you hit your head harder than I thought."

"Write," said the curator.

Elizabeth went back to her statement. She didn't make anything softer than it had been. She wrote that Shade had fallen. That she'd caught him. That she'd said, out loud, that she was fine, and that she'd said, out loud, no. That Pathwell had used the letter anyway.

The curator read the first page, and then the second, and said, "Thank you."

That was all. Nobody asked Elizabeth to decide what Pathwell ought to do about it, or how bad he ought to feel, or to sit nearer to him than the little side table she'd been given. She liked the museum a great deal, after that.

On her way out, she stopped in the front room, at the case with the socks in it. Five people. Stansbury had been right. She'd looked.




Milo's ankle mended faster than he would have liked, and the broom handle had to go back to sweeping. Mama Baga found him a new left boot from somewhere. It had been Hamid's, from when Hamid's feet were younger, and it was two sizes too big, and Milo stuffed the toe with a sock and wore it everywhere, and told anyone who asked that it had been in a fire.

Stansbury's hand took longer. The dressings got smaller, and under them the scar came up, red and uneven and shiny, across the back of the hand and under the cuff. Every morning and every evening he sat on the steps of Mama Baga's wagon and did the healer's exercises, slowly, one finger at a time, without saying anything to anyone.

Pathwell didn't mention the scar again. Not once.




On the twelfth day Camp needed a new wagon for the archive.

Pathwell found four. The archivist turned down three of them from the photographs. The fourth was sound and dry and the right size inside for the new cabinets, and it was painted, all over, inside and out, the bright, chalky pink of a pencil eraser.

"Yes," said the archivist. "That one."

"You can't be serious."

"I am."

"It looks like a stick of gum."

"It fits the drawers."

"Color is also a measurement."

"Not one I'm using," said the archivist.

Pathwell looked at Mama Baga. Mama Baga shrugged. "His wagon."

Pathwell turned back to the pink wagon, and Elizabeth watched a whole improved plan arrive in his face, all at once. Paint. New springs. A better axle. A skylight, possibly, that nobody had asked for.

He climbed up the little ladder at the back, onto the roof of the pink wagon, and stood in the middle of it, and jumped, once, quite hard. The wagon took it without a creak. He stood up there a moment, looking down at all of them.

He slid back out and sat up and rubbed his face with both hands.

"Do you want it painted?" he asked the archivist.

"No."

"Springs checked?"

"Yes."

"The axle?"

"If the wheelwright says it needs it."

"All right," said Pathwell.

It came two days later and nobody painted it. Milo named it The Eraser. The archivist objected, at length. The name stuck. Pathwell was the first grown-up to use it.




On the day the new archive was filled, there wasn't any ceremony. The Eraser simply became the archive.

Blank paper into one cabinet. The saved originals into another, in the new shallow drawers, two inches deep, so that you could see everything at once. The blue road notebook on its own padded shelf. The family letters in a new cloth.

The piece of cardboard from the morning of the fire went into the top drawer, first, before anything else. The archivist had made a fair copy of it, smudges and all, and then he'd filed the cardboard too, because it was the one that had been there. Near the bottom, in pencil, it still said SHADE. DIED AT CAMP. And under that, QUEEN OF SPADES. RECOVERED.

Elizabeth stood in the doorway of The Eraser with Shade's notebook in her good hand.

She'd been carrying it for twelve days. In her blazer, then in her coat. She'd read nothing else in it but the two pages. She'd shown nobody.

"He asked me to keep it," she said to the archivist. "So it would never be Pathwell's."

The archivist looked at the notebook, and then at her, and waited, the way he waited.

"He showed people his pages," she said. "He showed them to me. At a diner, the first night. He turned it round on the table so I could read it." She looked down at it. "I think he'd want it somewhere it could be read. By whoever asks. I think he'd want somebody to know he was here."

The archivist held out both his hands.

She gave it to him.

He took a card from the new drawer and wrote on it, in his small square capitals, and propped it in front of the notebook on the shelf by the door, at about the height of Milo's chin.

SHADE.
HIS OWN.
ANYONE MAY ASK.
NOT PATHWELL'S.

"Should the last line be there?" said Elizabeth.

"He asked for it," said the archivist. "I'm only writing down what he asked for."

Pathwell was standing at the foot of the steps, with a box of index cards in his arms. He'd heard. She saw him read the card from where he stood.

He didn't say anything. After a moment he put the box down where the archivist pointed, and went to get the next one.




By the end of the second week, Milo was short a player for Hearts again.

The woman in the knitted cap was already shuffling. The archivist had a new deck, the cards still stiff and slippery from the box.

Milo pointed at Pathwell. "You."

Pathwell was carrying a crate of blank paper toward The Eraser. He stopped. "Me what?"

"Cards."

"I'm working."

The archivist looked at the crate. "Put it inside. Then sit."

Pathwell carried it up into The Eraser and put it down on the exact spot the archivist had chalked on the floor for it. Then he came back and sat on the empty stool. Mama Baga put a bowl in front of him and walked off before he could say whether he wanted it. He looked at it. Then he ate it, all of it, fast, bent over it, and Elizabeth realized she couldn't remember the last time she'd seen him eat anything since the fire, and that he couldn't either.

Milo dealt.

Pathwell picked up his cards. He held them the way he held everything, too loosely, so that the woman in the knitted cap could see two of them and very kindly didn't say so. "Hearts."

"Yes."

"I know Hearts."

"Shade said that," said Milo.

The table went quiet.

Pathwell looked down at his cards. "Did he."

"He was bad," said Milo.

"Terrible," said Elizabeth.

Milo looked at the cards in his hand for a while. Then he said, "He still owes me a hand. We didn't finish."

Pathwell was quiet a long moment.

"I could play it," he said. "His hand. For him. If you'd—"

"No," said Milo. He said it at once, without any doubt in it at all. "It's not yours. It's his."

Pathwell looked at him.

"You play yours," said Milo.

"All right," said Pathwell, after a moment. "Yes. Mine."

They played. Pathwell lost by twenty-six points, which, Milo explained to him with great kindness afterward, was the most you could lose by.

"Shade was better," said Milo.

"Then I've got work to do."

"Again?"

Pathwell looked at The Eraser, and at Mama Baga. "Am I finished for the day?"

"I'm not in charge of you," said Mama Baga, offended.

"You're finished," said the archivist.

"Again," said Pathwell.

Elizabeth stayed for the second game to see if he could lose worse. He could.




In the third week, a family came walking into Camp along the north road: a mother and father, a grandmother, three children, and a goat on a rope, and all of them, even the goat, had the look on them that Elizabeth knew from the inside.

She was only there because Milo had demanded another game. She watched Mama Baga see them, and start across the ring toward them, and then stop, halfway, and turn round, and look for someone.

"Pathwell," said Mama Baga. "Go and make them welcome."

Pathwell set down the crate he had. He looked at her, once, to be sure. She just looked back.

He went.

Elizabeth couldn't hear what he said to them. It went on a while, and there was a good deal of waving at the wagons, and the smallest child laughed out loud at something. Then he stopped in front of the grandmother, who was carrying a canvas bag that was clearly much too heavy for her, and he didn't take it off her. He held out his hands, and said something.

The grandmother looked at him. Then she nodded, and let him have it.

He'd asked.

"Is he always like that?" the grandmother said to Mama Baga, as they came past, loud enough for the whole ring.

"No," said Mama Baga. "He's getting better at it." And she went off to see about the goat.




Stansbury still had his scar. Elizabeth's shoulder still told her when it was going to rain. The empty case in the museum had a small card in it, in the curator's handwriting, that said IN CONSERVATION. Ada Lowe was being mended, one thread at a time, by somebody very patient, in a room Elizabeth would never see.

In The Eraser, on the shelf by the door, at about the height of Milo's chin, Shade's notebook sat behind its card. Somebody had already asked to see it. The archivist wouldn't say who.

Elizabeth sat on the steps of the pink wagon in the late sun with a cup of Camp coffee, and looked off at the far end of the ring, where the road went away between the trees.

Elizabeth smiled.
