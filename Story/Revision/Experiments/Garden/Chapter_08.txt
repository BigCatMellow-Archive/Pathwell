Chapter Eight

Elizabeth picked the ditch a mile before she got to it.

It ran along the right side of the road, wide and shallow and soft with long grass, and there was a gravel farm drive crossing it on a concrete pipe. No house near it. No lights coming the other way. Farther on there was a telephone pole, which would have been worse, and then trees, which would have been a great deal worse. She had time to choose, and she chose the grass.

Behind her, they'd stopped talking. They'd been quiet for most of a mile, the kind of quiet two people keep when they're waiting for the third one to be dropped off.

"Which prune?" she said.

Nobody answered.

"Stansbury said it went wrong. Which one?"

"It doesn't matter yet," said Pathwell.

"That isn't an answer."

Stansbury looked out of his window.

"Pathwell."

"Elizabeth."

"Which one?"

"Not while you're driving."

"Then I'll stop driving."

"There's nowhere to—"

She lifted her foot off the gas, and the big car began to slow, and the dark fields went by a little less fast.

Pathwell leaned forward between the seats. "Elizabeth. Keep going. Or pull over and let me drive. I'm told I'm superb."

"You told yourself."

"I'm a reliable source."

The gravel drive came up pale in the headlights.

She moved her hands up the wheel, to the top, the way you're not supposed to.

Stansbury looked at her. She watched him work it out. It took him about a second.

"Seatbelt," she said.

His hand went to his belt, which was already on, and then he turned his head. "Put yours on," he said to the back seat.

"Why would I—"

She turned the wheel.

For a moment the Cadillac didn't believe her. It was long and heavy and it had been going straight for an hour, and it went on going straight a little while after she told it not to. Then it went. The front wheels dropped off the edge of the road, and the whole car tipped and lurched down into the ditch, and the grass was suddenly up in the headlights, a wall of it, white and roaring along the sides of the car. Something in the back bounced off the roof. Pathwell said a word she'd never heard him say. She stood on the brake. The car plowed on, slower and slower, through the grass, like a boat into a reed bed, and then the nose went down into the soft bottom of the ditch, and stopped, with a long, deep, metal groan from somewhere underneath, the sound of something important giving up.

The engine ran a second longer. Then it stopped too.

Grass settled against the windows. Somewhere up on the road, a hubcap was going, ringing round and round on the asphalt like a dropped plate, slower and slower, until it lay down.

Her heart was going so hard she could feel it in the wheel.

It was the crash that woke her.

She sat there with her hands still on the wheel and found that she was not afraid, and not sorry, and not anything she had a name for. She was awake. That was all. Everything was very loud and very clear, the tick of the cooling engine and the grass against the glass and her own breath, and for the first time since four o'clock that morning she knew exactly where she was.

"Elizabeth," said Stansbury.

She looked at him.

He looked at her. Then at the hood, which was pointing down into the ditch at an angle hoods aren't meant to point.

"That," he said, "was my car."

"I know."

"You did that."

"I did."

From the back, from under the box of foam swords, which had come down on him and spilled, Pathwell said, "You put us in a ditch."

She turned around in her seat. He had a foam sword across his lap and another one over his shoulder, like he'd been knighted by something careless. His face was white.

"On purpose," he said.

"Yes."

He stared at her. She'd been ready for him to be angry. She'd been looking forward to it, almost. But he wasn't angry. He was looking at her the way people look at a cat that has just opened a door by itself, as if he'd been wrong about something for a long time and had only now noticed.

She unbuckled her belt. "There. We've stopped."

Stansbury tried the key. The car made one small, polite click and nothing else.

"Comprehensively," he said.

"I'm sorry about your car."

"I believe you."

"I'll pay for it."

He looked at her. "With what?"

She didn't have anything. She had two dollars and sixty-five cents, minus a coffee, lying on a bar ten miles back.

Stansbury tipped his head toward the back seat. "Put it on his tab."

"I don't have a tab," said Pathwell, from under the swords.

"You've had a tab since six o'clock this evening, and it's growing."

She got out. The car was tipped so far forward that she had to climb up out of the ditch on her hands, through long wet grass, to the road. Her legs shook under her when she stood up. They weren't hurt. They were catching up.

Out on the road, the night was huge. Fields to the edge of the dark on both sides, and a line of trees somewhere far off, and above everything the clouds were coming apart and there were stars in the gaps, more of them every minute.

Stansbury climbed up after her, slowly, with his hand pressed to his side.

"Are you hurt?"

"I was hurt before. This is the same hurt, louder."

"I really am sorry."

"I know." He looked down at the Cadillac, nose-deep in the grass. Then, unexpectedly, the corner of his mouth went in. "I've wanted to do that to him for about two hundred years."

Pathwell came up out of the ditch on his hands and knees, swearing gently, and stood up on the road, swaying, and looked at her.

For the first time all night, nobody was talking over her head.

"The prune," she said.

His eyes went to his brother.

She laughed. It got out on its own, short and sharp, and it surprised all three of them. "You can't do it, can you? You can't even look at me while you don't tell me."

"I'm thinking."

"You think out loud at everyone but me."

He looked at the car, and the hubcap lying on the road, and the dark.

"Somebody needed something," he said. "A long time ago. I paid for it with a future I hadn't got round to. Most of what I paid went where I meant it to go."

"And the rest of it?"

"I never found the rest of it."

While he talked, his right hand had come away from his side. It was turning, slowly, palm out. Toward the road behind her.

She looked at it. He looked at her looking at it.

He put the hand flat on his own chest and held it there, the way you'd cover a cough. And then he went very still, and he wasn't looking at her anymore. He was looking past her.

"Again?" said Stansbury.

Pathwell nodded.

Elizabeth turned around.

Headlights. One car, a long way back, coming up the empty road slow. It slowed down more when its lights found the hubcap, and the black stripe of crushed grass going down into the ditch, and it pulled onto the shoulder fifty yards away and stopped there with its engine running.

Nobody got out.

They stood there on the road, the three of them, in its light, and nobody got out of the car, and nothing happened, for so long that Elizabeth's feet started to get cold.

"Well, this is stupid," she said, and started walking toward it.

"Elizabeth—"

She kept walking.

It was an old sedan, dark blue or black, she couldn't tell, with a dent in the back door. The driver's window was down. There was a man behind the wheel, with both hands on it, and as she came up alongside he turned his head to look at her, and she stopped.

It was Pathwell's face.

It wasn't. It was the face that Pathwell's face would have been if somebody had taken it apart and put it back together very carefully, from instructions, and not quite trusted the instructions. The hair was flat. The coat was buttoned to the top, every button in the right hole. And the eyes looked at her with no idea at all of who she was.

His right hand let go of the wheel.

It came up off the wheel and turned on its wrist and opened, palm out, fingers spread a little. Not toward her. Past her. Toward Pathwell, standing back there in the road.

She had seen that hand before, under a yellow bulb, in a mirror with the silver going.

The man's jaw tightened. He took his right wrist in his left hand and pulled the hand back down onto the wheel and held it there.

"That's the hand," said Elizabeth.

He looked at her.

"In the mirror," she said. "At the bar. His hand did that. Except it wasn't his."

The man was quiet for a moment.

"There was a mirror," he said slowly, "in a gas station. Yesterday evening. I was washing my hands." He looked down at his own hand, held on the wheel. "And for a second it wasn't a gas station. It was a hallway. With a dead phone. I thought I was very tired."

"You were in it," she said. "You were the one in the mirror."

"I don't know what I was." He looked past her, up the road. "Is anybody hurt?"

"No."

"Bruised," called Stansbury, who had come up behind her without her hearing him and was standing a careful car's length off. Pathwell was still back in the road.

The man nodded, as though that settled one column.

"How did you find him?" said Stansbury.

"I didn't find him." The man's voice was quiet and even and very exact. "Something in here"—he lifted the hand on the wheel an inch, and set it down again—"knows which way he is. Not where. Only which way. I can ignore it a while. Then I can't. So I drive."

"How long?"

"Long enough to be very tired of driving."

"What's your name?"

There was a pause, as if it were the hardest question so far.

"Shade," he said.

Back in the road, Pathwell made a noise.

Shade looked at him through the windshield for the first time. They looked at each other for a long moment, across fifty feet of road and two sets of headlights, and nobody said anything, and Elizabeth thought she had never seen two people look at each other like that, as if each of them were a word the other one had been trying to remember.

"You," said Shade. Not loud. He didn't need to be.

"Apparently," said Pathwell.

Elizabeth bent down to the window.

"Who are you," she said, "to him?"

Shade took his time. When he answered, he said it to the windshield.

"I remember things I didn't live. A staircase with a loose step I've never climbed. A dog I never had. How to tie a knot nobody taught me. People's faces, and no names for them." His hand moved on the wheel, and he held it still. "They're his. They were in here when I started. I don't know anything of his since. I don't know you."

"Started," said Stansbury.

"Existing. There isn't a good word. You pick."

"The prune that went wrong," said Elizabeth. "You came out of it."

"I have pieces of it." Shade looked at her. "Not enough. Enough to know he won't hand you the rest cleanly."

"You don't know me," said Pathwell, from the road.

"I know some of your excuses," said Shade, without raising his voice. "They came with me. They weren't good then, either."

"Nobody knows enough yet," said Stansbury.

Shade nodded. "That's true."

It seemed to bother Pathwell more than anything else had.

The cold had got all the way into Elizabeth now. Her hands were shaking again, worse than in the parking lot, and she put them in the blazer's pockets, and they kept shaking in there.

Shade looked at her hands, at the pockets.

"I'm going to get coffee," he said. "There'll be some somewhere down this road. There always is."

"Is that an offer?"

He thought about it. "I don't know. I've never made one."

Pathwell came up the road toward them, fast, with his arm around his ribs. Not toward Shade. Toward her.

"You're not getting in that car."

He said it the way he'd said shoes. The way he'd said drive. For half a second her body nearly went along with it, out of nothing but habit.

Then she heard it.

"No," she said. "You don't get to say that kind of thing to me anymore."

"You don't know who he is."

"Neither do you. That's sort of the point."

"Then I'm coming."

"No," said Shade, through the window. Pathwell stopped. "Not in my car. Not you."

Elizabeth looked at Stansbury. He was standing at the edge of the headlights with one hand on his ribs, looking at her, and he didn't tell her what to do. She could see it took something out of him, not to.

She took the foam dagger out of her blazer and held it out to him, handle first.

He looked at it, and at the car, and at her. "Keep it."

She put it back.

"You had all night," she said to Pathwell.

"I don't know what he knows."

"Then I'll go and ask him."

She walked round the front of the sedan to the passenger side. Her hand went to the front door, and stopped there. It was too close, the front. It was too much like belonging to somebody.

She opened the back door and got in.

Shade's eyes found her in the mirror. "The front is empty."

"The back is fine."

He looked at her a second longer, and seemed to write that down somewhere inside himself, and put the car in gear.

Through the windshield Pathwell was standing in the middle of the road in their lights. He didn't look angry. He looked frightened, and very tired, and for a moment she almost got out.

Then she remembered the back of her own head in the Cadillac, and his voice saying drive.

She shut the door.

Shade pulled out and around, past the ditch and the hubcap and the dark stripe of crushed grass. Pathwell stepped back to let them go by. Beside him, Stansbury lifted one hand. Elizabeth lifted hers.

She watched out of the back window until the dark had both of them.

It was only then, with her hand still up, that she noticed. She was in the back of another man's car, going somewhere because he'd sounded sure.

It was a little late to call it an accident.
