Chapter Twelve

It was still night on this side.

That was the first thing. The museum had been gray with morning, and here the stars were still out over the trees, and the fire in the middle of the ring had burned down to a red eye, and the only people up were the ones who never seemed to go to bed. The wall closed behind her and was just a wall of trees.

The second thing was Milo.

He was crossing the ring at a run with an empty bucket, one sock down round his ankle as usual, and he saw her, and stopped dead. He looked at her arm, held against her side. He looked at Shade beside her. He looked at Shade's face for a long time.

Then he set the bucket down, very carefully, right where he was, and ran for Mama Baga's wagon.

The bucket tipped over, slowly, and rolled in a half circle, and stopped.

Shade watched it the whole way.

"He left it," he said.

"He'll come back for it."

"He put it down first. Carefully." Shade seemed to file this. "Before he ran."

Elizabeth laughed, and the laugh went straight into her shoulder, and she bent over round it.

Shade's hand came out toward her and stopped short, an inch off her sleeve.

"Can you walk?"

"I've been walking."

He walked beside her. He didn't touch her.

Mama Baga came down the wagon steps before they'd got there, with her shawl pulled round her and her scarf on crooked from sleep. She looked at Elizabeth's face, and the arm, and the glass in the blazer's creases, and then at Shade, and Elizabeth watched her see the face, and see it wasn't Pathwell's, and decide something about it, all in about a second.

"Where is he?"

"Museum," said Elizabeth.

Mama Baga said something short in a language Elizabeth didn't know, and didn't need to.

"You sound like you already know what happened."

"I know what it looks like when he is somewhere else and somebody is hurt." She looked at Shade. His right hand had started to come open at his side, toward the trees, and he shut it.

Mama Baga saw. She didn't ask.

"Inside," she said.

Elizabeth went up the first step and the shoulder went white and she stopped there, holding the rail.

Nobody reached for her. Mama Baga waited, and Shade waited, and nobody told her to take her time or put a hand under her elbow.

She went up the rest of the steps by herself.




It was the same wagon, close and warm and smelling of tea and dried things, with the little bench under the window. The cot at the back was empty now, the blankets folded. The young woman from the wagon was gone.

The healer wasn't. She was at the basin, washing her hands, and she looked round when Elizabeth came in and didn't seem surprised at all.

"Sit."

Elizabeth sat.

She looked at Elizabeth's arm, and at the sling of blazer Elizabeth had made of it, holding it to herself. "Well," she said. "You do get around."

The healer pulled a stool over and sat in front of her, knee to knee.

"Can I touch it?"

Elizabeth hadn't expected that. She sat there with her mouth a little open.

It had taken her a year to learn to ask. Nana had been very particular. You couldn't just do up her buttons for her, even when her fingers weren't working, even when you were late for the doctor. You had to ask. "I'm not a coat, Lizzy," she'd said, the first time, snatching the button out of Elizabeth's fingers and doing it herself, crooked, taking five whole minutes over it while the car idled outside. "Ask first." After that, Elizabeth always asked. Most of the time Nana said yes. The point wasn't the yes.

"Yes," said Elizabeth. "You can."

The healer eased the blazer off the shoulder, and then the blouse, with care, and felt along the bones. The collarbone. The top of the arm. Round the back, to the shoulder blade.

"Tell me if the fingers go numb."

"They don't."

"Good." She pressed, gently, near the joint.

The whole world went white and narrow for a second, like looking down a pipe.

"There," said the healer. It wasn't a question.

Elizabeth found she couldn't answer, and nodded instead, several times.

Mama Baga put a folded quilt behind Elizabeth's back. Shade stood just inside the door, out of the way of everything, with his hands in his pockets.

The healer glanced at him. "Is he staying?"

She didn't ask Shade. She asked Elizabeth.

Elizabeth looked at him. He was looking at her, waiting, the way he'd waited in the car for her to choose the back seat.

"He can stay."

He nodded, once.

"It's come out of the socket," said the healer. "Partway. More than partway. I don't feel anything broken, but I want it back where it lives before it swells up and makes this harder for everybody."

"Is it going to hurt?"

"Yes," said the healer, as if it were the time of day. "Then it'll stop. Mostly." She moved her stool in. "You say when."

"Can't you just do it? Before I know?"

"I could." The healer waited, with her hands ready. "You say when."

Outside, the Camp was starting to wake up. A pot lid. A cough. Somewhere, someone tried three notes on the fiddle, didn't like any of them, and stopped.

Elizabeth breathed.

"When," she said.

The healer moved.

The whole wagon went white, and the white had a sound in it, and then something under the skin went in, deep, a great dull clunk like a door shutting in a big house, and the white went down to gray, and the gray went down to an ache. An ordinary ache. An enormous one, but ordinary.

Her shoulder was back where it lived.

"Fingers?"

Elizabeth moved them. They moved. "Hello," she said to them, and the healer very nearly smiled.

The healer bound the shoulder and the top of the arm with long strips of cloth, firm, with something on them that smelled bitter and green, and then she made a sling out of a folded square and tied it at the back of Elizabeth's neck. There was no paper in any of it. No writing. Just hands and cloth and someone who knew how.

"Nothing in that hand," said the healer. "Not a cup. Not a bucket. Not a baby, if anyone hands you one, and they will."

"I wasn't going to carry anything."

The healer looked at her. Mama Baga looked at her. Even Shade, by the door, looked at her.

"Fine," said Elizabeth.

The healer put a cup in her good hand. "Drink that."

She smelled it. "What is it?"

"Willow bark."

"Is that bark?"

"It's willow."

"Is willow bark?"

Mama Baga's mouth moved at one corner. "Drink."

She drank. It was bark.




When the healer turned around, Shade was still standing by the door, and his right hand was hanging at his side, and there was blood on it, dried, all down the inside of the fingers.

"You," said Mama Baga.

He looked at her.

"Hand."

He held it out. The long cut across the palm, from the brass rail.

Mama Baga pointed at the basin. "Wash it."

He washed it.

The healer looked at the hand, and then she looked at his face, and Elizabeth could see her see it, too, the face, and decide it was nothing to do with the hand.

"Can I touch it?"

Shade looked at her, startled, as though nobody had ever in his life asked him that, and possibly nobody had.

"Yes," he said.

She cleaned it and bound it with a strip of clean cloth, and he watched the cloth go round and round his hand with great attention.

"This is very undignified," he said.

"Yes," said the healer, and tied it off.

Mama Baga looked from one of them to the other. "Hungry?"

Elizabeth was. She found out all at once, and it was almost embarrassing, how hungry. "Yes."

Shade took longer. He seemed to have to go and look. "Yes," he said, eventually. "I think so."

"There is stew."

"Food stew," said Elizabeth. "Not bark."

"Food."

Mama Baga held the door. Shade went down first.

Elizabeth stood up slowly. The healer reached out and touched the knot of the sling at her neck, checking it.

"If the fingers go numb. If it swells more. If the pain changes and stops just being pain. You come back."

"I'll come back."

The healer looked at her until it stopped being the polite answer, and started being the real one.

"I'll come back," Elizabeth said again, and meant it, and the healer let her go.




Camp was coming awake one person at a time. Somebody feeding the fire. Somebody shaking a blanket out. Somebody carrying water up from somewhere Elizabeth couldn't see.

Milo had found his bucket. He was standing by the big fire with it, looking at Shade.

Shade looked back.

Milo held the bucket out.

Shade looked at the bucket. Then at Milo. Then at Elizabeth, as if there might be instructions.

"He wants you to fill it," said Elizabeth. "From wherever the water is."

"Why me?"

"Because you're standing there," said Milo. It was the first thing he'd said to anyone all morning. "Everybody carries something."

Shade took the bucket. He looked at it in his good hand.

"Where's the water?"

Milo pointed. Shade went that way. He came back with it full, slopping a little, and set it down where Milo showed him, and Milo nodded once and gave him a second, empty one, and pointed again.

Elizabeth sat on a log by the fire with a bowl of stew on her knee and watched him do it four times.

The fifth time, he came back and set the bucket down and stood there a moment with his wet bandaged hand, breathing a little hard, and then he took out his notebook and wrote something. She could see from where she sat that it was one short line.

He sat down on the log across the fire from her and somebody gave him a bowl of stew, too. He held the spoon in his left hand, the way you'd hold a pen you'd found in the street, and looked at it, and then at her.

"I've never done this with this hand," he said.

"He's right-handed?"

"I don't know about him. I'm right-handed." He tried. Most of the stew got there. "I'm going to write that down later."

For a while, neither of them talked about Pathwell. Nobody at the fire asked Elizabeth what had happened at the museum. Nobody asked Shade who he was, or what. They gave him another bucket, at one point, and he carried it.




When her bowl was empty, she noticed the diary. It was in the blazer's inside pocket, against her ribs on the good side, and when she moved, its corner pressed into her.

She put her hand over it.

She thought about the nurse in the museum, writing down what men wanted said for them. Tell her he's eating. Sell the mule. To nobody; he wanted it written anyway.

Her diary wasn't like that. It was her dentist, and the bus, and a list she'd never finished, and a page with a cat's foot on it. Nobody would ever put it in a case.

But she'd written it anyway. For four years, at two in the morning, to nobody. She'd wanted it written.

She looked across the ring at the archive wagon. Its door was open. The lamp was on.

She stood up.

"Where are you going?" said Shade.

"The archive."

He looked at the sling. He didn't tell her not to.

"All right," he said.




The archive smelled of paper and cedar and candle wax. The archivist was at his desk, stooped over a ledger, with a pencil in his hand and two more stuck behind his ear, exactly as if he hadn't moved since she left.

The blue road notebook was on the desk beside him, open, with a sheet of fresh notes clipped to it. It was still being used. It was still itself.

Nana's cookbook was on the reserve shelf by the door, where Milo had put it, at about the height of Milo's chin. Someone had made a support for the spine out of stiff card. There was a card in front of it, propped up, in the archivist's small square capitals.

CASSEROLES & SUCH
WRITTEN BY HER GRANDMOTHER. GIVEN BY ELIZABETH.
2 PAGES USED (SUGAR COOKIES; CHICKEN & DUMPLINGS).
LAST 4 PAGES WRITTEN WITHOUT INK. SHE DIDN'T STOP.
RESERVE / ASK BEFORE USE.

Elizabeth read it, and then read it again, and then she had to look somewhere else for a minute.

The archivist looked up at her sling, and then went back to his ledger and wrote something in it, as if she were weather that needed recording.

"Can I hold it?" she said. "The cookbook."

He put his pencil down, and got up, and took it off the shelf, and held it out to her across the desk without a word, which she understood was the answer.

She let it fall open on her good arm. Nana's letters, leaning. A margin note about salt. A grease spot shaped, if you looked at it sideways, a bit like Ohio.

She stood there and let herself wait for it. The kitchen, the open window, the voice.

It didn't come. She hadn't really thought it would. There was only the book, and the smell of the book, which was flour and old paper and very faintly onions.

That was all right. It turned out that was all right.

She gave it back before she could start turning every page, and he put it on the shelf exactly where it had been, and squared the card in front of it with one finger.

Then she took the diary out.

The cover was scuffed and bent at one corner from the night. Inside was her own handwriting. Her crossings-out. Her complaints about the dentist. Her attempt to write about the funeral, which she'd given up halfway down the page because every sentence had sounded like somebody else's grief. Thumper, in the middle of it, walking through the ink on six toes.

The archivist looked at the diary in her hand and didn't reach for it. He didn't ask her anything. He just waited, with his pencil down, the way the healer had waited with her hands ready.

"I'd like you to have this," Elizabeth said.

He went on waiting. He seemed to know that people often say a thing a second time, more truly, if you leave them room to.

"For good," she said. "Not to mind it for me. To have."

She put the diary down on the edge of his desk and opened it with her good hand to the last page she'd written on. It was the list, from the week after the funeral. Call hospice about the bed. Return library books (hers). Find a drawer for the spoon. None of it crossed off.

"Can I borrow a pencil?"

He took one from behind his ear and held it out.

She bent over the desk. It was hard to write with only one arm to hold the page still, and the archivist reached out without being asked and put one long finger on the corner of the paper to keep it flat. Under the list, at the bottom, she wrote one more line.

Owe Shade breakfast.

She looked at it. It was crooked. It was in pencil. She left it.

She gave him back his pencil, and shut the diary, and held it out.

He took it in both hands, the way Mama Baga had taken the cookbook.

He opened a drawer and took out a card. "Conditions?"

She thought about it. "Ask me before anyone reads it."

He wrote it down. "It won't be yours," he said, not unkindly, as he wrote. "You understand. Asking you is a courtesy."

"I know. I'd still like the courtesy."

"Anyone in particular?"

"Pathwell."

The pencil stopped. He looked at her over the desk for a moment. Then he wrote, in his small square capitals, pressing quite hard:

ASK DONOR FIRST.
NOT PATHWELL.

"That's all it needs to say?"

"He reads cards," said the archivist. "It's people he has trouble with."

He didn't put it up on the shelf next to the cookbook. He put it in a shallow wooden tray by the door, with a few other new arrivals waiting their turn, a bundle of letters and a child's school report and a pair of reading glasses that had apparently been donated by mistake.

Elizabeth walked past the tray on her way out and did not touch it, and that was harder than the shoulder.




Outside, morning had come all at once while she wasn't looking. The sky over the trees was going yellow. The fire was up again, and somebody had put a big pot on it.

Shade was on his log. He had a different spoon now, from somewhere. And there were three full buckets lined up by his feet, and Milo was sitting next to him with his chin on his knees.

Shade looked at her hand, the one that had had the diary in it. He didn't ask.

Elizabeth sat down across the fire from them. The shoulder throbbed with her heart. Pathwell hadn't come. Mama Baga came by and put a blanket over her good side without a word and went on.

She pulled it up round her, and watched Camp wake up.
