Chapter Fourteen

Pathwell came through first, and the first thing he saw was a game of cards on the ground.

He looked worse than he had on the museum floor. Stansbury's square of gauze was still taped above his ear, gone brown at one corner. There was dried blood down the side of his collar. He held himself very straight and very carefully through the middle, like a man carrying a full glass of water inside his own chest.

Stansbury came through after him. He was still holding the museum's broom.

The light between the trees narrowed to a line, and went out.

For a moment nobody in the whole of Camp said anything.

Then Milo pointed at Pathwell. "He was winning."

Pathwell looked at Milo. Then at the cards in the dirt. Then at Shade, standing by the table with his right arm stuck out stiff toward him and the rest of him held perfectly still.

"Was he," said Pathwell.

"He had the queen."

"He did not have the queen," said the woman in the knitted cap, gathering cards. "I had the queen. He had the face of a man with the queen. It's different."

Shade didn't take his eyes off Pathwell.

Pathwell's eyes went round the ring, fast, the way they did. Elizabeth watched them do it. The green jacket. The card table. The girl on the healer's steps, opening and shutting a hand that was gray under the nails. The smell, still faint in the air from the stream. He took all of it in and put it away in about a second and a half, and he thought nobody had seen him do it.

She had.

"New coat," he said to Shade.

"Yes."

"A lot of pockets."

"I like pockets."

"That's not one of mine," said Pathwell. "I've never liked pockets."

"I know," said Shade. "That's why I like it."

Stansbury looked at the broom in his hand as if he'd only just noticed it, and leaned it very carefully against a wagon.

"Are we finishing the hand?" said the woman in the knitted cap.

Everybody looked at her.

"I was winning," she said. "I'd like it noted."

The archivist bent and picked the queen of spades up off the ground. No — it was the eight of clubs. He looked at it, and put it face down on the table. "Noted."

For one odd second the morning nearly picked itself up and went on.

Then Pathwell swayed.

Stansbury had him by the elbow before he'd finished. "Sit."

"I'm standing very well."

"You're listing."

"That's a sailing word."

"You're doing it on dry land. Sit down."

Pathwell's eyes found Elizabeth. They went to the sling first, and stopped there, and whatever had been in his face to make the joke went out of it.

"How bad?"

"It came out. They put it back."

He looked at the sling for a long moment. "Lizzy—"

"Elizabeth."

He shut his eyes. "Elizabeth."

Stansbury pushed an upturned crate against the back of his brother's knees, and Pathwell sat down on it rather suddenly.

Mama Baga had been on the steps of her wagon this whole time, with both hands on the rail. She hadn't said anything. She came down now, slowly, and stopped in front of him and looked at him. At the gauze. At the collar. At the way he was sitting. She took a long time over it.

"Who did that?" She meant the gauze.

"Stan."

"Cleaned first," said Stansbury. "Eyes checked. Twice."

Mama Baga looked at Pathwell until he understood that she was waiting for him to say something more than that.

"It's a scratch," said Pathwell. "Honestly. The bookshop cat's given me worse, and at least the cat—"

"Healer," said Mama Baga. "Afterward."

"After what?"

She didn't answer that. She went and stood by her steps again, and folded her hands, and watched.






He started emptying his pockets.

That was all it looked like. A tired man sitting on a crate, taking things out of his coat one at a time and laying them on his knee because they were digging into his bruised ribs. Two brass pins, each about as long as his finger. A dull metal ring, big enough to slide over a fist. A flat bit of wood with pale waxed string wound round and round it. Another pin, from another pocket.

"You carry too much hardware," said Stansbury.

"You carried a broom through a door in a wall."

"I put the broom down."

Elizabeth put her good hand inside her blazer, to the split dagger. It was still warm. Stop using it, Stansbury had said, and she had, and she hadn't been able to make herself throw it in the fire either. She left it there and didn't say anything.

Pathwell was unwinding the string. His fingers were slow and clumsy at it. He looked like a man fidgeting, the way men fidget in hospital waiting rooms, with whatever's in their hands.

Shade was watching the ring.

"I remember buying that," he said.

Pathwell's fingers stopped.

"It was raining. A street made of stones. A man in a doorway with a tray of them. You argued about the price for nearly an hour, and then you gave him what he'd asked in the first place." Shade's wrist trembled, and he held it. "I've never been wherever that was."

"That's mine," said Pathwell. "Don't go through it."

Shade's face shut like a door. "I'm not going through anything. It's in here. Nobody asked me if I wanted it."

There was a pause.

Pathwell heard what he'd said. She watched it get to him, a second late, the way the sound of a dropped plate gets to you after you've already seen it fall.

"No," he said. "Nobody did."

He leaned forward round his ribs and put the ring down on the ground between his boots. Then he pushed one pin into the dirt beside it with his thumb, slowly, like a man planting a seed he didn't think much of.

Stansbury put a paper bag on his brother's knee. "Eat that."

"What is it?"

"A biscuit. From the coffee shop that used to be a bank."

Pathwell took it in his right hand, and with his left he put the second pin into the dirt a foot from the first, with the ring between.

"This is tyranny dressed up as concern."

"It's a family business."

Shade's hand went again, hard. It swung his arm round toward Pathwell and his hip into the card table, and the table jumped.

Milo grabbed the cards before they could go. "Again?"

"Apparently," said Shade, through his teeth.

"How strong?" said Pathwell, not looking up from the dirt. "Before I came through."

"Strong."

"And now?"

"Now you're sitting in front of me."

"Which way does it—"

"You know which way it goes," said Shade. "Ask your own."

Pathwell broke the biscuit in two, and didn't eat either half. He tied the string to the first pin, and passed it through the ring, and tied it to the second. It looked like nothing much. It looked like something a child would leave in the yard.

"The curator would like an inventory," said Stansbury. "By tonight."

"She'll get one."

"And a full account."

"Fine."

"Handwritten."

Pathwell looked up at that, wounded. "Now you're just being cruel."

"Do it," said Elizabeth. "You should."

His eyes went to her, and stayed there a moment. "I will."

He went back to the string. He nudged the ring along it, until it hung in the exact middle.

Shade stood up. The stool went over behind him.

At the card table the woman in the knitted cap stopped shuffling, and Milo stopped with the deck held tight in both hands.

"Sit down," said Pathwell. Mildly. Not looking.

"What is that for?"

"It's only for looking. At the line between us. So you don't have to pull your arm out of its socket every time I come within a mile of you."

Elizabeth heard the joke land on her own shoulder and lie there.

"Not today," she said. "You don't get that one today."

His face changed. "No." He looked at the ring. "I'm sorry."

Shade came round the end of the table. His right hand went out ahead of him toward Pathwell like it was being reeled in, and he held it back against his chest with his left.

"That isn't for looking at anything," he said. "Two pins. One line, between. It's for bringing something back where it came from."

Stansbury had opened his mouth to say something. He closed it.

Pathwell's face didn't do anything at all. Elizabeth knew that face now. It was the one he used when any expression would be a confession.

"You're building a rejoining," said Shade.

The whole ring had gone quiet. Even the fire seemed to be listening.

"Milo," said the archivist pleasantly, "take the cards back to the archive, would you? The top drawer. And bring me the copy of the north route while you're there."

"But I—"

"Now, please, Milo."

Milo looked from face to face. Then he took the deck and ran, across the ring and up the archive steps and in at the open door.

"Tell me he's got it wrong," said Elizabeth.

Pathwell put the two halves of the biscuit down on the bag, side by side, very neatly. "He hasn't."

"Brother," said Stansbury.

"Don't."

"I was only going to ask what you think—"

"I know what you were going to ask."

Pathwell picked up the third pin off his knee.

Shade looked at it.

Then he did the thing Elizabeth wasn't ready for. He let go of his own wrist. His right hand swung out at once toward Pathwell and stayed out there, open, shaking, pointing. He didn't fight it. He let it point. Everything else about him stood still as a post.

And with his free left hand, he unbuttoned the inside pocket of the green jacket, over his heart, and took out his notebook, and held it out to her.

"Would you keep this," he said. "Please."

She looked at it.

"If he does it," said Shade, "and it works, I don't know if whoever's left over will be the one who wrote that." He didn't look at Pathwell. "It isn't his. I don't want it to be his."

She took it in her good hand. It was warm from his pocket. She put it inside her blazer, on top of the broken dagger, and did up the button over both.

Shade breathed out, a long breath, like a man setting down a bag he'd been carrying a long way.

"No," he said to Pathwell.

"You don't know what I mean to do."

"I know what it looks like."

"You remember what it looks like. That isn't the same as understanding it."

"You said that to somebody once," said Shade. "Before me. It wasn't true then either." He lifted his chin at the pins. "You'll use what's already between us as the way home. You'll put the bit of you that came loose back against where it came loose from, and tell it we're one thing, and finished. Instead of two."

Pathwell didn't answer.

"Have I got it wrong?"

"Not all of it."

"What happens to me?"

Pathwell looked down at the pin in his fingers.

"What happens to me," said Shade, again, quite gently.

"I don't know. Not exactly."

Shade looked down at himself. The green jacket. All the pockets. His own hand, out in the air, pointing, not his.

"Am I still here afterward?"

Pathwell got up too fast. The ribs caught him and folded him half over, and he had to put a hand down on the crate. Stansbury moved toward him, and he waved him away.

"I think so," he said. "I think you could be."

"Could."

"Yes."

"Not would."

Pathwell looked away from him. "No."

Shade nodded, once, as if a sum had come out the way he'd thought it would.

"Then no," he said.

"He said no," said Elizabeth.

"I heard him."

"Then put that away."

Pathwell looked over at the healer's steps, where Posy had stopped flexing her gray fingers to listen, and his voice came out very low.

"If I leave it how it is, those things keep coming. They came this morning because he was here, and one went straight past her. Straight past her. I can smell it on her hand from here. Next time it mightn't go past."

Nobody had an answer to that quickly. The archivist looked over at Posy for a long moment.

"Then ask them," said Shade. "Ask Camp if they want me gone. Don't sit there asking yourself if you'd like me put away."

His hand shook in the air between them.

"I'm the one who lives in here," said Shade. "And no."

He looked sideways at Elizabeth. "You don't have to stay for this."

"I'm staying," said Elizabeth. "I want to see what he picks."




Mama Baga came across the ring.

She didn't hurry. She came across holding her arms quite still at her sides, and people stopped what they were doing to let her by, and somewhere the fiddle, which had started up again, stopped in the middle of a note.

She stood over the two pins and looked down at them, at the ring and the string, and at the third pin in his hand.

"Stop," she said.

"Mama—"

"He asked if he could stay. I said stay." She said it to everyone. "I had already seen that child's hand when I said it. I knew. That was mine to decide, and I decided it, and you don't get to take that off me by spending yourself to make it tidier."

"You haven't seen what it does."

"I have seen a great many things you have built," said Mama Baga, "to bring something home that did not want to come."

"It's not the same."

"It's the same pride," said Mama Baga, "in a better coat."

Shade looked down at his green jacket.

"Put the last one away."

"It's getting worse," said Pathwell. "The line between us. I can feel it. It's fraying."

"Then don't hang any more of yourself on it."

"That's the only bit of this I've got any say in."

Stansbury heard that. She saw it go into him like a splinter.

"No," said Stansbury. "You're not paying for this."

"It'll take a prune. It's made to."

"I heard you."

He stepped across into the space between his brother and the pins. Not onto anything. Not touching. Just there, where Pathwell would have to go round him.

"You don't get to buy your way out of a room," said Stansbury. "I said that to you this morning, and I'll say it every morning. You bought this lot their paper because you'd decided they needed paper. You bought Ada Kell's girl a bus home that she never asked to get on, because you'd decided what was best for the pair of them. How many times are you going to call that being responsible, before you notice the rest of us are standing here watching you?"

Pathwell's face went flat. "Move."

"No."

"Don't make him the reason," said Shade.

They both turned.

"If it goes wrong because he was standing there," said Shade, "he'll spend his whole life explaining that bit. Not the rest of it." He looked at Stansbury. "Let him choose it. All of it. With nobody in the way to blame."

Stansbury looked at him for a long moment.

Then he stepped back.

Elizabeth watched it take the last excuse out of Pathwell's hands.

He looked down at the pin. For a moment his fingers went slack on it, and it nearly fell.

There was the way out. She could see it. So could he. Put the pin in the coat. Pull the string loose. Leave the morning undone, and the problem a problem, and sit in it.

His fingers closed.

"I can't keep leaving wreckage everywhere I go."

"You're making some," said Elizabeth. "Now."

"Not if it works."

"You still think if it comes out right," said Shade, "then it was all right to do."

"I'm trying to make it right."

"Right for who?"

Pathwell didn't have an answer ready, for once.

"You don't want the pull gone," said Shade. "You want me gone. So you don't have to keep seeing your face on somebody who isn't you."

"Stop," said Mama Baga.

"You paid," said Elizabeth, "so you choose. That's all this is, isn't it. It's the bus all over again."

He pushed the third pin into the dirt.




The ring came up off the ground.

An inch. Two. It hung there on the string, and the string went tight between the three pins, and hummed, low, like a wire on a windy day.

Stansbury said something under his breath that she'd never heard him say.

"Nothing's gone yet," said Mama Baga, fast and low, kneeling now. "Do you hear me? Nothing's gone. Stop now, and nothing's gone. Do not let go of anything."

"I know." His breathing had gone short and shallow.

"Then stop."

His shadow changed on the ground under him.

She'd seen this once before, in the bookshop, coming up out of a book: pale light, branching, given a shape so the eye would have something to look at. That time, it had come up out of the book as high as his chest before it ever split. This time it came out of his own shadow on the dirt, and it was thin, and it split low, and there were fewer branches on it, and a great many short black stubs among them where branches had been. But it was his. It was all still his.

The ring turned toward it, slowly, like a sunflower.

Shade stumbled. The thing between them had pulled tight as a rope. His arm went out straight toward Pathwell, all the way, and he set his feet and let it go.

Elizabeth's hand went to her blazer. The dagger was in there, under his notebook. It cut what clings, Stansbury had said. Made not to cut people. But whatever was holding Shade's arm out like that, whatever ran from him to Pathwell, it might be something stuck where it shouldn't be, and it might be Shade himself. She didn't know which. She didn't know whose it was to cut. And the thing had cracked in two places already.

She took her hand away.

"I'm not going to fight you," said Shade. "Whatever happens now, it's yours."

"I know."

"Your mind is in two places," said Mama Baga, very close to him, not touching anything. "Look at me. You are telling it two things."

"I'm not."

"You are. You built it to make him part of you. And you want him kept separate, inside. You can't want both. Not to this."

"I can keep him," said Pathwell. "Inside it. I can keep him whole in there."

Shade laughed, once. "In there."

"You don't know that," said Mama Baga.

"No."

"It hears what you mean. Not what you wish you meant. You're giving it two answers. It cannot do anything with two answers."

Don't think. Favorite. Don't think.

He'd said it to her in her own living room, with the door coming in and his hand flat on the sugar cookies. She understood it now. He'd needed one answer from her. One thing, true all the way through, with nothing else mixed in it.

He didn't have one. Not today.

The branches trembled. The ring dipped on its string, and the whole thing began to loosen, slowly, the way a held breath begins to go. He could still let it. Mama Baga saw it.

"Lift your hand," she said.

His right hand was hanging over the ring, not quite on it.

"Lift it. Let it fall. Nothing's gone. Let it go."

Shade's arm shook out toward him. "Leave me how I am," said Shade. "How I am is fine."

The ring loosened more. Pathwell's hand came up off it. An inch.

Then he looked at her sling.

The gauze above his ear had begun to bleed through again. She watched his eyes go from the sling past Mama Baga to the archive wagon, to its open door, and she knew everything he was seeing there, because she could see it too. The blue road notebook. The weather books. Everybody's letters. Nana's cookbook on its shelf, with its card. Her diary in its tray. Everything people had handed to that wagon to keep.

He looked back at Shade. "If I stop, they keep coming."

"Yes."

"And the pull stays."

"Yes."

"And you stay tied to me. For good."

"Yes," said Shade.

Pathwell closed his eyes.

She knew. She knew a whole second before his hand moved.

"Don't."

He pressed the ring down flat against the dirt.

The branches broke in toward it all at once, and something left him. She didn't see it go. She felt it, the way you feel a door shut somewhere in a house. Something that had been his, and wasn't now.

It went through him first. He folded over the pins as if someone had kicked him. The gauze went dark. His whole right arm shook till his hand was a blur, and the three pins went down another inch into the earth on their own.

Then the ring pulled toward Shade. Then back toward Pathwell. Then both at once, and the string on the ground threw two shadows, side by side, from one light. One of them said together. The other one said apart. They weren't words. She understood them anyway.

Pathwell looked up. She saw the fear get to his face before anything else could.

"Take your hands off it," said Mama Baga.

"It's gone. It's let go."

"I know it has. Stop pushing."

"I can still make it settle."

"You cannot."

He put both his hands round the ring.

Shade didn't move.

"Pathwell," said Elizabeth.

He looked at her.

"Let it fail," she said.

"I can hold it."

"You said that in the museum."

The ring made a sound. Not a working's sound. Just metal, being made to carry more than metal can, a high tearing shriek. And then it couldn't anymore.

It didn't go toward Shade. It didn't go toward her. It went back the way it had come, all of it, into the one man who'd given it two answers and paid for both. The light came back up out of the pins in a white flash and hit him in the chest and threw him flat on his back in the dirt, and then it went past him.

It went out of the frame in a ring, flat and white and hot, a single breath of it, across the ground in every direction. It knocked the card table over. It blew the cards off. It went under the wagons and over the fire and it hit the old dry wooden steps of the archive wagon, and the wall above them, and the wall went up.

Not with any light. Just fire. Ordinary, yellow, small for half a heartbeat, catching at the edge of the rag rug inside the open door.

Then the wall.

"Water!" The archivist was already running.

Pathwell lay on his back among the pale, loose threads of everything he'd just paid. They were all over the ground round him, faint and thin and belonging to nobody.

People ran past him with buckets. The fire went up the wall. Inside the wagon something heavy went over.

At the edge of the ring, Posy had stopped dead with an empty bucket in her one good hand. She wasn't looking at the fire. She was looking past it, at the trees.

"It's in there," she said. "In the trees. The one from the stream. It's coming."

Someone took the bucket out of her hand and ran with it to the line. Posy pointed again. Nobody was looking where she pointed. The archivist was shouting for Milo.

The tray by the door had gone over into the aisle. Elizabeth could see her own diary from where she stood, lying open on the boards inside the door.

Beyond it, on its shelf, Nana's cookbook sat in its little support.

Further in, something else fell. And then, high, surprised: "Hey! Hey!"

The archivist went white to the lips. "Milo?"

The smoke came across the doorway in a sheet. The fire reached the first of the shelves inside, the letters and recipes and diaries, and only then did it change. A hundred small lives came loose into the heat at once.

A dog barking at a gate.

Flour on a windowsill.

A road, going by under wheels.

A man laughing at a funeral and hating himself for it.

A woman writing her daughter's name three times on one page, because once hadn't felt like enough.

Ordinary fire had found a wagon full of people, and the people all answered at once. The whole wagon rocked on its wheels.

Inside, a shelf went down.

"I'm stuck!" Milo screamed. "I'm stuck!"

Elizabeth was already running.

Her shoulder shrieked in its sling. Somewhere Stansbury was shouting. Somewhere Mama Baga was calling the bucket line round, and the fiddle was lying in the dirt where somebody had dropped it.

Pathwell lay where he'd fallen, awake, and couldn't get up.

Shade was still standing.

The line between them still held.

The archive was burning.

And Milo was inside it.
