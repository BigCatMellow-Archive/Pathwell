Chapter Three

She found him two blocks up, sitting on a bus bench under a streetlight, reading her diary again.

He had it open on his knee with the cookbook in his lap underneath it, and he was turning the pages with one finger, and he looked up when she stopped in front of him as if she were the bus.

She put the cake on the bench. Then she took the diary out of his hands.

He let her. He seemed a little surprised by it, and then a little pleased, as though she'd done a card trick.

"I'll need that one back too," she said, and held out her hand for the cookbook.

"You'll get it." He stood and tucked it under his arm. "At the counter."

"What counter?"

"The one we're going to. I've got to drop some things at a bookstore, and then I'll answer anything you like. Even the ones you haven't thought of yet."

"Nothing's open at this hour."

"This one keeps odd hours. Most of the good ones do."

He dug in his coat pocket and came out with a piece of paper folded into a small, fat square, and pressed it flat against the brick wall behind the bench.

Light came through the creases. It ran out along the folds in thin lines, the way a crack goes across ice, until there was a glowing square on the wall about the size of the paper. He peeled one fold back and the square doubled. He peeled another and it doubled again. By the last fold it was taller than he was, and there was a door in the brick, open, and warm air coming out of it that smelled of old paper and older glue.

Inside were shelves. A lot of shelves.

Her ears popped.

"What is that?"

"The bookstore," he said patiently. "I did say. The coffee's terrible, you'll love it."

"And if it's a trap?"

He actually thought about it, which was worse than if he'd lied.

"Well," he said. "You'd have to come in to find out."

He went through with Nana's cookbook under his arm, turned left, and was gone.

She stood on the sidewalk with her diary and a cake.

A loose sheet of paper slid out of the doorway on the warm air and came to rest against her shoe. It was a letter, in pencil, so faded she could only make out *Dear* and, further down, *the hens*. It smelled faintly of lavender.

He had the cookbook.

She picked up the letter, because she couldn't just leave it on the sidewalk, and went in.



It was the quiet she noticed first. Out on the street there had been a city, and in here there was nothing, not even the hum a building makes. Her own footsteps sounded rude.

Then the shelves.

Most of what was on them wasn't really books. There were journals, with cracked spines and pages gone wavy from being wet once. There were letters, bundled in ribbon or string or, once, a shoelace. Recipe cards with grease on them. School notebooks with doodles on the covers. Address books. A whole shoebox of birthday cards, every one of them signed *Love, Mom*. They were jammed into every shelf, floor to ceiling, and the ceiling was a long way up.

"Close the door, would you?" His voice came from somewhere ahead and to the left. "It lets the heat out."

She turned and pushed it shut, and it went into the wall and was gone, and there was a shelf there, as if there always had been.

"Pathwell?"

Nothing.

"Pathwell!"

From somewhere deep in the rows, someone said "Shh."

She laid the lavender letter on the nearest shelf and went forward, because standing still was worse.

The first aisle was only dusty. In the second, a woman she'd never met was getting married. Elizabeth felt it all at once, the whole morning of it, the nerves and the borrowed earrings and somebody's hands doing up the buttons on her back, and her throat went tight with how happy it was. She stopped. She put her hand out to the shelf beside her and the spines there were warm, like a car seat in summer.

She took her hand back. That wasn't hers. She knew that much. That wasn't hers.

The next aisle was somebody's father dying, and she had to lean on the shelf to get through it.

The one after that was nothing she could name. It came up through her feet and into her chest and her heart jumped like it had missed a step in the dark, and then kept missing it. Her hands went cold. There wasn't enough air in the aisle for her, there wasn't enough air anywhere, and all the shelves were leaning in to tell her something, every book at once, all of them so close and so sure that she was the one who needed to hear it.

The floor came up gray.

She kept hold of the cake.



Nana's house had thirteen stairs. Elizabeth had counted them the first week she moved back, carrying a tray, and then she never needed to count them again.

It was supposed to be for a few months. She'd said so to everybody, her boss and her friends and Nana, who had looked at her over her glasses and said nothing at all, which was worse. It went on for four years. Elizabeth learned the pills, and what the pills were for, and which ones Nana would pretend to have taken. She learned to carry a tray up thirteen stairs with a glass of milk on it and not spill. Mostly she learned to carry the tray back down.

"You didn't touch it."

"I touched it. I moved the peas."

"Nana."

"I'll eat the toast," Nana said, "if you sit down."

So Elizabeth sat down on the end of the bed, and Nana ate half a piece of toast, and they watched a cooking show with the sound off and argued with it.

Nana wrote in the cookbook right up to the end. Her letters got smaller and pushed into the margins, and then they got shaky, and then the blue marker ran dry. Elizabeth bought her new ones, a whole pack, every color. Nana said thank you and put them in a drawer and went on writing with the old one. She pressed so hard that the last few recipes went into the paper without any ink at all, just the shapes of the letters dug in, and you could only read them if you tilted the page to the window.

After the funeral the trays stopped, and Elizabeth carried boxes down the stairs instead. She kept the cookbook, and the spoon, and her diary. Everything else left in garbage bags and in other people's cars.

Then a new apartment, as far across the city as she could get. Three weeks of boxes. Then a party she hadn't asked for.

It turned out she still knew how to do a party. She knew where the ice was before anyone asked. She kept the chips full and the trash empty, and whenever she ran out of things to say she picked up an empty cup and went to find three more, because there was always something to carry. She even told the landlord story, about the man who'd shown her the apartment and said the radiator "has opinions" and then left before she could ask which ones, and the quiet man by her boxes laughed out loud.

When it was over and everybody had gone, she stood in the kitchen with what was left. Without thinking about it, she made up a plate. The good cheese, a few crackers, a corner of the cake, the way Nana liked it, with the most frosting.

She was in the hallway with it before she remembered there weren't any stairs.

She put the plate in the fridge, and went to bed, and set the alarm. She had a meeting at nine.



She woke up in an armchair with her feet on a footstool. An orange cat was sitting on the floor in front of her, perfectly still, watching her the way a cat watches a glass near the edge of a table.

A mug came into view. Pathwell was behind it.

"There she is," he said. "Drink that."

She took it, because someone was handing it to her, and drank. It was awful. It was burnt and thin and had been sitting on a warmer since about midnight, and it tasted exactly like the coffee out of the machine on the third floor of the hospital, by the elevators, where she had stood at three in the morning more times than she could count.

"That's terrible."

"I know."

She drank more of it. Her hands stopped shaking.

"Why does it help?"

"You know it. That's all it takes, sometimes." He sat on the arm of the other chair. "It's a lot in there, if you're not used to it. I should've warned you."

"You should have warned me."

"I just said that."

The cake box was on the floor beside her chair. Someone had set it down there carefully, with the lid on straight.

The room came together around her. A long wooden counter, worn pale where a lot of elbows had leaned on it. Lamps that weren't making much of an effort. Behind the counter a small, neat man in a cardigan was writing in a ledger with a fountain pen, and beside the ledger, squared up neatly, sat Nana's cookbook, and on top of it, her diary.

She looked at her empty hands, and then at Pathwell.

"You dropped it," he said. "When you went down. I picked it up."

The cat got up and rubbed against her shin, purring. She reached down to scratch its ears, and it opened the back of her hand.

It was fast and very tidy, one claw drawn through the soft place between her thumb and her wrist. She snatched her hand back. A thin line of blood came up.

The cat sat back down and washed its paw.

"Boots takes a toll," said the man at the counter, without looking up. "From anyone who's new. You're new."

"A toll."

"He's not unreasonable about it. He only takes the once."

She looked from the cat to the man to Pathwell, waiting for somebody to tell her it was a joke.

"Hey. You're all right." Pathwell took her hand and turned it over. He had a strip of paper out of his pocket, old and soft, folded many times, and he unfolded it and read it first, which seemed polite. "Do you like me," he read. "Check yes or no." He turned it so she could see. Someone had checked yes, then scribbled it out, then checked it again harder.

He laid it across the cut and held it there and said something under his breath. The paper went warm, and then it went into her hand, and the cut went with it, and so did the sting. For a second she was eleven and somebody had passed her a note in math class and her whole face was hot.

Then it was just her hand, with a thin pale line across it.

"Scars I can't do," he said, and ran his thumb along the line. "Sorry. Not my department."

"You used somebody's love note on me."

"It was a good one. They were very sure, eventually." He let go of her hand. "That's Boots. He does security. The rest of the time he's more of a minor deity. Blood, mostly. Some mice."

"We pay him in mice," said the man at the counter. "He prefers it to money."



She put the mug down and got up and went to the counter and reached for her books.

The man put one finger on the cookbook.

"His transaction first," he said.

She looked at Pathwell.

"You said at the counter."

"And here we are." He spread his hands. "Counter."

"That's not the same as getting them back."

"It's in the same family."

"He brought them in with the rest," the man said, and went on writing. "Into an open account. I'll sort them out when he's settled. I don't like paperwork."

He had a great deal of paperwork, for a man who didn't like it.

At the far end of the counter was a pile. Pathwell must have built it while she was out. Journals, mostly, and bundles of letters, and some sheets of music, and a stack of blank paper, thick and creamy, the good kind. At the bottom, holding everything up, was a wooden crate tied with twine. The counter had a slight lean to it at that end.

The man looked at the pile over his glasses.

"Some of these are very old, Mr. Pathwell. Did Camp Cunnan ask you for all of this?"

Pathwell retied a knot in the twine that didn't need retying. "They'll need it."

"That isn't what I asked."

The knot got a great deal of attention.

The man turned back through his ledger, a long way back. He ran his pen down a column and went on to the next page, and the one after that, and Elizabeth watched the pages go by and stopped counting.

"And your account with it," he said. "All of it, today. I've let this run a long while."

"I'm good for it."

"You've been good for it for some time, Mr. Pathwell. You've sold me a good deal of what you were good for."

"There's more."

"There's some."

"What does that mean?" said Elizabeth.

Neither of them looked at her. The nearest bundle of letters had slid crooked. She squared it up with both hands, edges together, without meaning to, and made herself stop.

The man's eyes went to the cookbook under his finger, and then to Pathwell.

"I take it this isn't part of the payment," he said.

"No," said Pathwell.

"No?"

"No."

The man looked at him a moment longer, and then he took his finger off the cookbook and slid it, and the diary, a few inches down the counter toward Elizabeth. Not to her. Just away from the pile.

"These were never his to spend," he said.

She didn't know what to do with that, so she put her hand on them, and kept it there.

"Then it's the usual," said the man.

"It's the usual."

"You're sure."

"Let's do it before I'm not."

The man put down his pen. He turned to the paneled wall behind him and slid a section of it aside, and behind it was a small black safe with no handle and no dial. He laid a sheet of paper over the front of it and moved his hands across the paper, and it went the pale blue of a gas flame, and something in the safe turned over with a sound like a big clock, and the door swung open.

He took out a book. It was large and dark, bound in leather, the edges of the pages silver, and pressed into the middle of the cover was a small dot with rings around it, like a stone dropped in water. He set it on the counter with both hands, the way you'd put down something sleeping.

"Say it, then."

Pathwell laid his right hand flat on the cover.

"What I might have been," he said, "for what's needed now."

The silver edges lit. Thin roots of blue light came up out of the pages and closed around his wrist, and then they went up his arm, splitting and splitting again, and then they went in.

He didn't make a sound. He set his jaw and let them.

From the middle of the book something grew, a thin crooked trunk of light. It climbed to his chest before it ever began to branch, and when it branched it went everywhere at once, a hundred ways. Some of the branches were bright and full. More were bare. A lot of them were nothing but stubs, dark and short, as if somebody had cut them back years ago and they'd never come again.

"You don't have much left, Mr. Pathwell," the man said, quite gently.

"So I keep hearing."

Fire came into the branches. It was orange against all that blue and it moved fast, from the tips in toward the trunk, and it took the bright ones and the bare ones the same. The roots in his arm burned orange with it.

Then he did make a sound. It came out of him in a lot of voices at once, a whole crowd of them shouting, and every voice was his. One by one, they stopped.

When the fire was out there were a few branches left close to the trunk, and a lot of black where the rest had been. Pathwell had gone down on his knees with his arm still held up by the roots, like a man who'd fallen asleep on a bus holding the rail.

Then the roots let go of him. He grabbed for the edge of the counter, missed, and got it the second time. Elizabeth was already coming around the end of it.

"Thank you, Mr. Pathwell." The man had his pen again. "That's you settled. All of it." He blotted the page. "Would you like a receipt?"

"God, no."

Pathwell got himself turned around and sat down on the floor with his back against the counter. He held his right arm against his chest. It was shaking from the shoulder down, all of it, and there wasn't a mark on it anywhere.

"You just sold your future," Elizabeth said.

He flexed his fingers. They didn't flex very far. "Some of it."

"Which part?"

"No idea." He let his head tip back against the wood. "You don't get to choose. That's sort of the arrangement."

She stood there looking down at him, on the floor of a bookstore that wasn't one, gray in the face, holding his own arm like it belonged to somebody else.

Then she went and got the cake.

There wasn't a knife. She opened the box and broke off a corner with her fingers, the corner with the most frosting, the one with the Z on it, and set it on the lid and brought it around the counter and held it out to him.

He looked up at her over it.

"Go on," she said.

He took it with his good hand and ate it slowly, the way he'd eaten the dumplings, as if it mattered.

"It's from a grocery store," she said.

"I know," he said, with his mouth full. "That's what's good about it."

She sat down on the floor beside him, with her back against the counter too.



After a while the man in the cardigan came out from behind the counter pulling a handcart. He pulled it out of a gap that couldn't have held a handcart, and it unfolded once, and then again, and put out a handle, and stopped, a good deal bigger than the gap it had come out of.

Pathwell stared at it.

"How long have you had that?"

"Always."

"I've carried everything out of here by hand. Armfuls. I did two trips in the rain in March."

"Yes."

"For years."

"Many years," said the man, and put the first bundle of letters on the cart. "You never asked."

Pathwell looked at him as though he'd been stabbed.

Elizabeth laughed. It got out before she could stop it, a short, startled sound, and she put her hand over her mouth. The man in the cardigan glanced at her, and the corner of his mouth went up a very small amount, and then he went back to loading the cart.

They loaded it between them, the man and Pathwell, Pathwell one-handed and complaining the whole time. The journals and the letters and the music and the good blank paper, and last of all the crate with the twine, which Pathwell picked up with both arms and a lot of noise. Boots got up on the counter to watch.

When it was done, the man turned to her.

"I can put you back on your sidewalk, miss, if you like. Where you came in. It's twenty past four." He said it kindly. "You'd be home before five."

Home. No door. The smell. The banner in the blob. Her spoon on the counter.

The dry cleaner at seven-thirty, with no ticket.

She looked at Pathwell, leaning on the handcart, gray and pleased with himself and pretending he wasn't listening.

"What do I owe for the coffee?" she said.

The man raised his eyebrows. "It's on his account."

"I'd rather it wasn't."

He looked at her over his glasses for a long moment, the way he'd looked at the pile.

She put what was left of the cake on the counter. ELI, it said now.

"It's from a grocery store," she said.

The man lifted the lid and looked inside. Boots looked inside too.

"Paid," said the man, and entered it in the ledger.

Then she took the cookbook and the diary off the counter and carried them over to the handcart, and set them down on top of the pile, on top of Camp Cunnan's books, and kept her hand on them.

Pathwell looked at the books, and then at her, and didn't say anything.
