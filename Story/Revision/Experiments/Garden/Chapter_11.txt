Chapter Eleven

The bus got them to Marlow a little before dawn, which was about as fast as a school bus had ever been driven by a man who was authorized to move it.

The museum sat at the end of the main street, past a hardware store and a church and a bank that had turned into a coffee shop. It was a low brick building with white trim and a flagpole, and a painted sign out front, MARLOW COUNTY MUSEUM, and under that, HOME OF THE SOLDIERS' WALL, and under that, on a separate board that had been screwed on later by someone with feelings about it, RESTROOMS FOR PATRONS ONLY.

There was one car in the lot. A dark sedan with a dent in its back door.

Elizabeth saw it first. "He's here."

Pathwell had gone still in his little seat. Not the pull. He knew the car.

Stansbury brought the bus in and parked it across two spaces, perfectly, exactly in the middle of both.

Shade was leaning on his sedan with his hands in his coat pockets. The right one kept shoving at the cloth from inside, toward the bus, and he kept holding it down. He looked worn out. Not the gray, coming-apart way Pathwell looked. Plainer than that. Like a man who'd driven all night with a stone in his shoe and hadn't stopped to take it out because stopping was worse.

Elizabeth got down off the bus.

"You're early," said Shade.

"You're earlier."

"I didn't sleep." He looked past her at the bus. At the steps. At Pathwell coming down them one at a time, holding the rail. His hand pushed at his pocket. He held it. "I'd like him to stay on that side of that bus."

Pathwell stopped on that side of the bus.

"How did you know to come here?" said Elizabeth.

"I didn't. The hand said this way." Shade nodded at the building. "Then I saw that sign, and I knew it. Or he did. I knew there was a wall in there. I knew there was a way to Camp in the wall, if you know where to touch. I knew there's a floorboard by the back room that sounds like a duck." He looked faintly annoyed about it. "I've never been here in my life."

"Are you going to Camp?"

He turned that over, the way he turned everything over, both sides, before he spent it.

"I'm going somewhere," he said, "where this isn't the only thing my body wants."

"That isn't an answer."

Something went across his face, quick. It might have been the start of a smile.

"Camp," he said.

"Me too."

"That's not ideal," said Pathwell, from the far side of the bus.

She turned round and looked at him.

He put up a hand. "I know. I heard it."

"Then stop saying things you have to hear afterward."

Shade made a small sound that, in anybody else, would have been a laugh, and took his hand out of his pocket, and put it straight back in.




Pathwell had a key.

He went round the side of the building to a plain green door and took it off his own key ring, between a brass one and a very old iron one, and fitted it to the lock.

Elizabeth looked at it. "Why?"

"I was the night watchman. Here. For a while. In 1971."

"And you kept the key."

"Nobody asked for it back."

"He was dismissed," said Stansbury.

"I was let go."

"He was found asleep in the Union tent. By a school group."

"It's a very convincing tent," said Pathwell, and opened the door.

Cool air came out to meet them, smelling of floor wax and old wool and paper kept a long time in the dark.

Elizabeth went in first. Shade came behind her, keeping a room's width from Pathwell, and Stansbury came after him. Pathwell came last, and locked the door again behind all of them, and stayed at the back.

The front room was the war. It was dim, with a few small lights on over the cases and the tall windows still black at the top and just going gray at the bottom. There was a canvas tent, the convincing one. Two uniforms on stands, a blue and a gray, the same size exactly, so it looked as if one boy had worn them on different days. A drum. Maps. A long row of tintypes of young men who looked as if nobody had told them how long they'd have to sit still.

Elizabeth had done the war in school. Dates and generals and the names of fields. Nobody had ever shown her the socks.

There was a case with just socks in it. A pair, gray wool, darned and darned again at both heels in different colors of thread, and a card underneath in old handwriting: Darned by his mother, by his sister, by himself, and by a woman in Pennsylvania whose name he never learned.

"Four people darned these," said Elizabeth.

Stansbury bent and looked. "Five. The card's wrong. Look at the left toe. That's somebody else again."

Behind them, Pathwell said, "He walked a long way in those," and didn't come any closer.

The next case had a tin plate in it with J.T.R. scratched into the rim, and beside it, open, a little notebook no bigger than a pack of cards. It had belonged to a nurse, the card said. She'd written letters home for men who couldn't, or couldn't anymore, and in this book she'd kept a list of every one, and next to each name a few words of what they'd wanted to say.

Pvt. Addison. To his mother. Tell her he's eating.

Cpl. Mears. To his wife. Sell the mule. Don't sell the dog.

Pvt. Lyle. To nobody. He wanted it written anyway.

Elizabeth read the last line three times.

Beside her, Shade had stopped too. He wasn't looking at the notebook. He was looking at nothing, at the floor.

"He walked through this room fast," Shade said quietly. "When he worked here. Every night. He never stopped in here once."

Behind them, Pathwell didn't say anything.

Against the far wall, in a long case, there was a quilt. It was faded nearly to gray, made of squares of different cloth, and in the middle of each square someone had stitched their own name, each in their own hand, some careful and some crooked, some in thread that had once been red. A church had made it, the card said, for a boy who was going away, so he'd have every one of them with him. He'd brought it home. By then most of the names on it were gone.

They went through the rooms slowly after that, the four of them, like a family out on a Sunday afternoon, if the family had let itself in with a key it wasn't supposed to have. Stansbury read every label down to the last line, donors and all. Shade stopped now and then in front of things he knew without knowing how, and frowned at them, like a man trying to remember where he'd put his glasses. At the door to the back room, Pathwell stepped over one particular floorboard without looking down.

Shade, behind him, stepped on it.

It quacked.

Pathwell looked back. Shade looked at the ceiling.




The wall had the whole back room to itself.

It wasn't a wall, quite. It was what was left of a house: one long side of it, old gray boards, standing up on its own in the middle of the floor behind a low brass rail, with the museum built up around it like a box around something that might break. And every inch of it, from the floor to as high as a tall man could reach, was written on.

Names. Dates. Initials, cut in with a knife. Towns. Regiments. A face with its tongue out. Sums. A sentence about a sergeant that would have got somebody into a great deal of trouble. A horse, drawn very carefully and very badly, with five legs, one of them crossed out.

Men had camped in this house on their way somewhere, and most of them hadn't come back from wherever it was, and before they'd gone, they'd written on the walls. Pencil, knife, charcoal from the fire. Anything they'd had in their pockets.

Elizabeth stood at the rail.

The Space Between had come at her. This didn't. It didn't push, or reach for her, or try to get in. It just stood there and was heavy, the way a room is heavy when a crowd of people goes quiet all at once and you can feel every one of them.

"This is the door?"

"This is the wall," said Pathwell, from the doorway. "It's a door as well, if you know where to touch."

Shade had come up to the rail a few feet down from her. He was reading the names. All of them, it looked like, one after another, slowly, the way you'd read a list to find out whether you were on it.

He took his notebook out. He uncapped the pen from the spiral. Then he stood a long time without writing anything, looking at all those people who'd put down that they were here, here, here, with whatever they'd had.

Then he wrote something. One line. He put the notebook away.

She didn't ask. He didn't offer.

"You're for Camp," he said to her.

"Yes."

"Not for him."

"For Camp."

He nodded, and stepped up to the rail to go over it.

From the doorway there came the scuff of Pathwell's shoe.

"No," said Elizabeth, without turning round. "Stay there."

The scuffing stopped.

She turned round anyway, to look at him. "What were you doing?"

"Keeping an eye on him."

"With your feet."

"I need to be near enough to—"

"You don't need to be near anything."

Stansbury had leaned himself in the corner of the doorway with his arms folded over his ribs, and he said nothing at all, and the nothing was very loud.

"I need the wall," said Shade, to her, not to anyone else.

"We don't know what it'll do," said Pathwell. "If he uses it. Him. Being what he is."

"You don't know what happens if I eat a pear either," said Shade. "I do now. I didn't like it. Nothing happened."

Elizabeth looked at Pathwell. "I came here to go to Camp."

"I know."

"So let him open it."

"Elizabeth—"

"I don't need to be rescued."

It came out level, all of a piece. She hadn't known she was going to say it. Once it was out, she found she meant every word.

"I'm standing right here," she said. "I can see him. I can see you. I picked Camp, and he knows the way, and nobody in this room needs you to do anything. Let him open the door."

Pathwell looked at Shade. Shade didn't argue. He waited, with his hand in his pocket.

Pathwell looked back at her.

"All right," he said.

For a second, she believed him.

Shade went over the rail, carefully, and stood in front of the boards. He put his left hand up flat against the names, about the height of his heart. His right hand stayed down at his side, shut.

"There's a mark," he said, mostly to himself. "Low. Under the horse."

He bent to look for it.

And the hand went.

It went the way it had never gone before, not in the car, not at the diner. It came open, and up, and out, toward the doorway, toward Pathwell, so hard it dragged his arm after it and his shoulder after that and turned him half round, and his knees went. He went down sideways against the rail, grabbing at his own wrist, and his face went white, and he made a sound she'd never heard a person make.

She caught him.

She didn't decide to. She was there, at the rail, and he was falling, and you catch people who are falling. She got her good arm round him, under his arms, and took his weight, and he grabbed for something to hold, and what he got hold of was her arm, hard, his fingers digging in.

And from the doorway, for half a second, it was a simple, ugly picture. Him coming over the rail at her. His hand clamped on her arm. Her pulled half off her feet.

"I'm all right," said Elizabeth, holding him up. "He's falling. I've got him."

Pathwell's hand was already in his coat.

"No," she said.

It came out with a folded paper in it.

Stansbury came off the doorframe. "That's out of my drawer."

Shade knew what it was. He looked at Pathwell standing there with the page in his hand, and he knew that way of standing, she saw him know it, from the inside, the way you know your own walk in a shop window.

"Don't," Shade said. Hoarse, still half on the floor.

Pathwell had the page open. "Let go of her."

"He's not holding me," said Elizabeth. "I'm holding him."

"Let go of her."

She saw the first line in his hand, in that neat, frightened writing. Don't come home. And she understood, a second too late to be any use, what kind of letter it was. It was somebody who loved somebody so much that she'd put a door between them.

"Pathwell."

"I'm only putting some distance in."

"There's nothing to put distance in. He fell. I caught him."

"I can keep it small." His eyes were on Shade. Only on Shade. Not on her at all. "I can hold it."

She heard the bus in it. She heard Ada's kitchen. I knew the quickest way through.

"I said no."

He didn't look at her.

The last line let go of the page. The page went white between his fingers and tore itself in two, along the fold.

Something came down in the middle of the room like a door slammed by someone the size of a barn. She couldn't see it. She saw what it did. It came down between Pathwell and Shade, flat and clear and absolute, a pane of nothing, and it took Shade off her arm and threw him back against the boards of the wall, and it took her the other way, and the rail came up out of the floor, screws and all, and swung, and the end of it went into the nurse's case, and the glass burst.

And it kept going. He'd meant it for one man. It wanted all of everything apart from all of everything else. It pushed out from the middle, and the rail went, and the chairs, and a little sign that said PLEASE DO NOT TOUCH THE WALL, and the whole of the nurse's case, glass and legs and all, which went over slowly, like a felled tree.

The floor tilted under her feet.

"Get down!" Stansbury shouted.

The nurse's notebook came out of the case in a spray of glass and landed open on the floorboards, and the edge of the case came down across the middle of it.

Then the room breathed in.

It was every one of them at once. Tell her he's eating. Sell the mule, don't sell the dog. All of those men, all of them wanting somebody at home to know, wanting it so much. And under them, holding all of them up, a woman with a pencil, tired past tired, writing it down for every single one because somebody had to.

It came loose into the room with nothing to shape it and went through Elizabeth like weather, and she found that she was crying, hard, sobbing, and had no idea who for.

The quilt's case went next. The glass held, but the frame twisted, and inside it, the old cloth tore straight down the middle, through somebody's name.

A great warm wave came off it. Church, and Sunday dinner, and everyone's hands, and a boy going away with all of them on his back.

And then everything in the room that wasn't nailed to something began to move toward the door. All of it at once, chairs and the sign and a coat stand and a little bench, as if the room had remembered all at once a great many people who wanted to go home, and had decided home must be that way.

Stansbury's arm came round her middle from behind and pulled her out of the way of the bench.

The lights in the ceiling came on. Nobody had touched them. They went off again. Somewhere a cabinet door was banging, and banging, and banging. Out in the front room the big drum went, once, all by itself, a deep wooden boom that she felt in her teeth.

"Pathwell!" Stansbury was shouting.

Pathwell was on the floor in the doorway, on his knees, his arm across his ribs, and there was blood coming out of his hair.

The pane in the middle of the room broke. It didn't go cleanly. It folded in, like a card house, and as it went it threw one last great flat shove out across the room.

She saw the case coming. It was the one at the end, the tall one with the uniforms' buttons in it, coming over sideways. She moved. Not fast enough. Stansbury's shoulder hit her and turned her, so that it didn't get her head. The heavy wooden corner of it came down on her left shoulder instead, and put her on the floor.

She heard it first. A wet, deep click, from somewhere inside her own body.

Then there was nothing.

Then there was pain, so white that the room went away entirely.

She heard someone make a noise, and it was her. Her arm wasn't anywhere it was supposed to be.

Stansbury was there, on his knees beside her, with one hand flat behind her head and the other flat on her upper arm, holding it exactly where it was.

"Tell me your name."

"You know my name."

"Tell me anyway."

"Elizabeth."

"What day is it?"

"It's—" She tried. "Saturday?"

"Near enough. Move your fingers. That side."

She moved them. Pain went up the arm like a struck match.

"That's good," said Stansbury. "That's very good. Don't do it again."

Across the room, Shade pushed the rail off himself and sat up against the wall. There was a long cut across the palm of one hand. He looked at it as though somebody had handed it to him.

In the doorway, Pathwell tried to get up.

"Stay where you are," said Stansbury, without taking his eyes off Elizabeth. It wasn't loud. It didn't need to be.

Pathwell stayed where he was.

The last of it went out of the museum in smaller and smaller waves. A breath of somebody's kitchen. Wet canvas. A boy going away. A woman's pencil, moving. Then less. Then less again. Glass tinkled onto glass somewhere and stopped. Out in the front room, the drum went once more, very small, almost sorry.

Then it was a room again. A smashed one. A quiet one.

Shade got up. He came across the room, through the glass, and stopped by the nurse's notebook, lying in two halves on the floor, and stood over it.

"You said all right," he said.

Pathwell's face had nothing left in it. "I know."

Shade looked down at the notebook. At Pvt. Lyle, who'd wanted it written anyway. Who'd been written anyway, for a hundred and sixty years.

"Do you," he said.

Pathwell didn't answer him.

Elizabeth got herself sitting up with Stansbury's hand behind her back. The shoulder went white again. And something else was wrong too, lower down, against her ribs on the inside of the blazer, something hot.

She got it out with her good hand.

The foam dagger had split. A long crack, from the point down into the handle. And it was warm. Not warm like something you'd held. Warm like something that had been in an oven.

If one cracks, or splits, or gets hot when it shouldn't, stop using it.

She looked at it. Then she put it back inside her blazer anyway, because she didn't have anywhere else to put it, or a free hand to put it there with.

"Elizabeth," said Stansbury.

"Camp," she said.

"Yes."

"Now."

In the doorway, Pathwell opened his mouth. She turned her head and looked at him, and whatever he saw in her face shut it again.

Shade went to the wall. The frame round the old boards had split down one side, and the rail was gone, but the boards were standing, and the names were all still there. Every one.

He looked up and down the third board, and the fourth, frowning.

"Third from the left," said Pathwell, from the floor. "Low. Under the horse."

Shade looked round at him.

Pathwell had his eyes shut. "If you want the way," he said, very tiredly, "that's where it is."

Shade looked at Elizabeth. She nodded.

He crouched in front of the third board. Under somebody's initials, and the horse with five legs, there was a short scratch in the wood, so old it was almost the grain. He found it with one finger and drew the finger along it.

The room changed again. Not like before. Quieter. Like a door in an old house remembering, all on its own, that it opened onto somewhere else. A line of light came out between two boards. Firelight. Woodsmoke. And very faint, a long way off, a fiddle being played badly, and somebody singing along with it worse.

"It's open," said Shade.

Elizabeth looked at the light. And then at Pathwell, on the floor in the doorway, with blood down one side of his face. For once in his life, he didn't tell her what to do.

"Healer," Stansbury said to her, low. "Before you sit down. Before you eat. Before you carry one single thing for one single person. Promise."

"I promise."

"That was too fast."

"I promise," she said again, slower, and he took his hand away from her back.

"You first," she said to Shade.

He went. He stepped into the line of light and through, and turned round on the other side, and waited for her.

She went toward the wall. Every step went up into the shoulder. Halfway there she had to stop and breathe. Then she went on.

At the wall she looked back once. The glass on the floor. The tipped-over cases. The quilt, torn through somebody's name. And Pathwell, in the middle of it all, on his knees.

"I picked Camp," she said.

He swallowed. He nodded.

"I'm still picking it."

She went through. The wall closed up behind her, and was boards.




After that, the room was quiet the ordinary way.

Stansbury stood and looked at the wall a while. Then he went out to the front desk, and came back with a green metal first-aid box and a cold pack and a broom, and threw the cold pack at his brother. It hit him in the chest.

He knelt and turned Pathwell's face toward the gray window and looked into his eyes, one and then the other, for a long time. Then he cleaned the cut in his hairline with something that stung, and Pathwell breathed in through his teeth.

"Hold still."

"I am holding still."

"Hold still on the inside."

Pathwell opened his mouth. Then he shut it again. He looked past his brother at the room.

The nurse's notebook, in two halves, in the glass. The quilt torn through the middle of someone's name. Things that had come through a war, and attics, and grandchildren, and three moves, and a flood in 1936, and a committee, and a night watchman falling asleep in the tent.

Until about five minutes ago.

"I'll make it right," Pathwell said. "I'll pay for—"

"No." Stansbury pressed a square of gauze to his head, harder than he needed to. "You won't pay for it. Not with anything of yours. Not this time. You don't get to buy your way out of a room."

"They'll have photographs. Of the notebook. The words are—"

"The words," said Stansbury.

Pathwell shut his eyes.

Stansbury got up off his knees, slowly, with a hand on his side. "Here's what happens. We sweep the glass up so nobody cuts themselves. We telephone the curator. And you tell her what you did. All of it, in the right order, with the clever parts left out."

"She's going to hate me."

"She already hates you. You slept in her tent." Stansbury picked up the broom. "After that, Camp."

Pathwell sat with the cold pack against his ribs and looked at the place on the floor where Elizabeth had been.

"I put her on the floor," he said.

"Yes."

"I told her all right. And then I did it anyway."

Stansbury stopped sweeping. He stood with the broom and looked at his brother for a long time.

"Yes," he said.

Through the tall windows the sun came up over the hardware store, and the church, and the bank that was a coffee shop now, and lay along the floor of the wrecked room, in all the glass.
