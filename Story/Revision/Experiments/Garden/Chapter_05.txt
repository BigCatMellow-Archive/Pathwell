Chapter Five

He wouldn't make a door anywhere near Camp. "It upsets the horses," he said, though she hadn't seen any horses, and they walked until the trees thinned out and the sky went gray and then pale, and they came out at the edge of a field gone gold with old grass.

Pathwell looked at the field. Then he lay down in it.

"Five minutes," he said, with his eyes shut.

He slept until well past noon.

Elizabeth sat beside him in the grass with her knees up. She didn't sleep. She watched the sun come up over the trees and climb, and at some point it climbed past nine o'clock, and somewhere in the city a meeting started without her. She waited to feel something about that. Mostly she felt the sun on her face.

She took the diary out of her blazer pocket, because her hands wanted something, and opened it.

It was strange to read it knowing he had. Things she'd written at two in the morning, things she'd written on the bus. Three pages of complaining about a dentist. Thumper's paw print was in there somewhere, from the winter she was fourteen, when he'd walked across a page while the ink was wet and she'd been so angry she'd cried, and then kept the page forever. She went looking for it and didn't find it, and found instead a page with its corner folded down.

She hadn't folded it. She didn't fold pages.

It was a list, from the week after the funeral.

Call hospice about the bed.
Return library books (hers).
Cancel the paper.
Thank-you cards. (How many?)
Find a drawer for the spoon.

Nothing on it was crossed off. Under it there was nothing else at all, just the rest of the page, and then the next one, which was about the move.

She looked at the folded corner for a long time. Then she unfolded it, and smoothed it flat with her thumb, and shut the diary.

Pathwell slept on in the grass with his arm across his chest, like a man guarding something.




When he woke, he sat up all at once, as though someone had said his name, and looked around the field like he was measuring it for carpet.

"Good," he said. "Right. Ready?"

"For what?"

"Stay close, Lizzy. This next part's fun."

"Elizabeth."

"Elizabeth. Stay close."

He reached inside his coat and came out with a fat envelope, soft at the corners, and tipped it out into his palm. Ticket stubs. Hundreds of them. Bus and ferry and train and cinema, a county fair, a ball game, a museum, and every single one had a date on the back in pen, in the same small upright hand, going back and back.

"Whose were they?"

"A woman who kept every ticket she was ever given," he said. "She never once went anywhere she didn't want to see twice."

He held out his other arm. "Hold on. Don't let go."

"What happens if I let go?"

"Last fellow who let go ended up in Portugal. He was very nice about it."

She took his arm.

He closed his hand on the stubs.

She smelled popcorn first, then diesel, then cut grass and wet pavement and the inside of a bus at night, and under all of it was a feeling she knew she'd never had herself, the plain, happy, stupid pleasure of having a ticket in your pocket. Her feet wanted to go. All around them the grass laid itself down flat in a ring, the way a crowd steps back to give somebody room, and the ring began, slowly, to turn.

The wind came up out of the middle of it. It pulled her hair loose and filled her borrowed blazer and took the breath out of her mouth, and she shut her eyes and held his arm with both hands.

Then it stopped.

It was dark. It smelled of floor polish and old sneakers. Something was pressing against her face that turned out, when she pushed it, to be a basketball net.

"Where is here?"

"Not sure yet," said Pathwell cheerfully, somewhere next to her. "Let's find out."

They were under a set of bleachers, folded up against a wall. Light came through the slats in stripes. Pathwell found the end and slid out sideways, and she followed, and they came out into an empty gym with lines painted all over the floor and a banner on the far wall, in letters a foot high, glitter on posterboard: HAVE A GREAT WEEKEND, EAGLES!

She read it twice.

"It's Friday," she said.

"Is it?"

"It's Friday afternoon. You slept through my meeting."

"I slept through a great many things," he said. "Your meeting was one."

He was already out through the gym doors. She went after him into a hall, and stopped.

Every inch of wall between the classroom doors was covered in children's art. Paper suns with faces. Turkeys made out of traced hands. A rocket with a dog looking out of the window. A family in a row outside a house, tallest to shortest, holding hands, while a sun in the corner wore sunglasses. Pathwell went down the hall slowly, leaning in at each one, the way he'd leaned over her diary.

"Stop that," she said.

"They're warm."

She held her hand near a drawing of a different dog. Under its feet, in crayon: MY DOG BISCUIT HE IS 9.

It was warm. Not the paper. Something coming off it, like heat off a sidewalk. She felt it go up her arm and into her chest, and it was pride, a small, fierce, ridiculous pride, the kind you only get once or twice in your life after you're seven.

She stepped back from the dog.

"Whose are they?"

"The kids'." He straightened up. "And my brother's, in a manner of speaking."

A bell went off over their heads like a fire alarm, and every door in the hall opened at once.

Children. Waist-high, all of them, and all of them with backpacks, and all of them going somewhere at top speed and at top volume, around and between and very nearly through the two of them. Elizabeth flattened herself against the rocket. Pathwell stood in the middle of the hall like a rock in a river, with children breaking around him on both sides, and didn't seem to mind.

Then they were gone, around the corner, and the hall was quiet, and there was one girl left.

She was about ten. She had a yellow sash across her chest that said HALL MONITOR, and a clipboard, and she was standing directly in front of Pathwell with the clipboard held up between them like a shield.

"Hall pass," she said.

Pathwell looked down at her. "I'm sorry?"

"You need a hall pass. Or a visitor sticker." She looked at his chest, where a sticker would go, and then at Elizabeth's. "You don't have either one."

"We were just—"

"Everybody says that."

Pathwell looked at Elizabeth.

Elizabeth crouched down to the girl's height. She'd done this before, with the neighbor's grandkids on Nana's porch, a hundred times.

"You're right," she said. "We should have stopped at the office. We're here to see Mr. Stansbury. Do you know where we could find him?"

The girl considered her. Then she wrote something on her clipboard.

"Mr. Stansbury doesn't have a room," she said.

"No?"

"He's everywhere." She said it the way you'd say it about weather. "He's the one who takes the art down."

"Thank you, Priya." A man had come around the corner while they weren't looking. "I'll take it from here."

Priya looked at him, and at them, and wrote something else on her clipboard with great firmness, and walked away down the hall with her sash very straight.

He was tall and broad through the shoulders, with black hair combed flat and a vest over a pressed white shirt, and a lanyard round his neck with a ring of keys on it that would have opened every door in the building. He looked a great deal like Pathwell, if Pathwell had ironed things.

"Stan," said Pathwell warmly.

"Don't."

"You look well, Stan."

"You don't." The man's eyes went straight to Pathwell's right arm, held against his chest, and stayed there. "You pruned."

"Nice to see you too."

The man turned to Elizabeth and put out his hand. "Stansbury."

"Elizabeth."

"I'm sorry," said Stansbury, and it was hard to tell what for.




Stansbury didn't have a room, but he had a closet. It was at the end of the art hallway, and it was bigger than a closet should have been, and it was full of drying racks, tall wire racks with dozens of thin shelves, and on every shelf was a painting, drying. Hundreds of them. Houses and suns and dinosaurs and what was probably a mom. The air was warm and close and smelled of poster paint, and every one of those wet pages gave off a little of that heat she'd felt in the hall, all of it together, like standing too near an oven.

In the middle of the closet was a long table, and on the table was a cardboard box.

"Mama sent word," Stansbury said. He leaned back against the table with his arms crossed. "Something followed you into an apartment in the city. Walk me through it."

"It was at the door before I did anything," said Pathwell. "Then I started a working. Then it broke, and when the thing got in, it went for the waste."

"I broke it," Elizabeth said. "I took the book back halfway through."

Stansbury looked at her, for a moment, with something that wasn't quite respect but was in the neighborhood.

"So once it was inside, it did what they do."

"It was very rude about it."

"They aren't rude. They're thorough." Stansbury turned to Elizabeth. "They clean up. Spills, failed workings, messes nobody claimed. If you don't get between them and the mess, they're mostly just unpleasant."

"That would have been nice to know yesterday."

"I'm sure." He tapped one finger on the table. "What I don't like is the first part. Why it was already at your door."

"Her door," said Pathwell.

Stansbury looked at him.

"Was it?"

Pathwell's face didn't do anything. It didn't do anything so thoroughly that Elizabeth opened her mouth to ask whose door it had been, then, if it hadn't been hers, and found she didn't want to watch him not answer.

She looked in the box instead.

It was full of foam swords. Bright plastic handles and soft gray blades, the kind you'd find in a toy aisle, and under them smaller things, foam daggers and short batons and two round shields with cartoon knights on them.

She reached in.

Stansbury slid the box away from her, along the table, without looking, and kept talking.

"Those aren't for playing with."

"They're foam."

"They're not for playing with," he said again, exactly as before, the way you'd tell a child twice.

Pathwell lifted a dagger out of the box as if it might bite him. "Stan makes toys."

"I make tools that look like toys. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Lift your foot."

Pathwell looked at him. "What?"

"Your left foot. Lift it."

Pathwell lifted his left foot. There was a gray wad of gum on the sole of his shoe, with a long string of it still trying to get back to the floor. Pathwell regarded it with deep personal betrayal.

Stansbury took the foam dagger out of his brother's hand. He touched the gray blade to the string of gum, and the blade lit up, faintly, a thin orange line along its edge, and the gum came apart. Not torn. Not stretched. It just let go, cleanly, the floor half and the shoe half, and then the shoe half slid off the sole and dropped onto the tile in one piece, like a coin.

"It cuts what clings," Stansbury said. "Things that don't come apart easily. Bindings. Things stuck where they shouldn't be."

"People?" Elizabeth said.

"It's made not to." He turned the dagger over. "None of these are indestructible. If one cracks, or splits, or gets hot when it shouldn't, stop using it. Immediately."

"What happens if you don't?"

"Something you didn't want to cut comes apart." He put the dagger back in the box.

Elizabeth looked around at the racks. All those paintings. All that warmth.

"Is this what you do here?" she said. "You don't teach?"

"I haven't taught a class in years."

"He steals children's feelings," said Pathwell.

"I do no such thing." Stansbury didn't even look at him. "Kids make things, and while they make them, it comes off them. Like heat. It's gone by the time the paint's dry. I catch a little of it before it goes, and put it somewhere useful." He nodded at the racks. "Every one of those goes home on Monday. They don't lose a thing. They put it on the fridge, and in ten years it's in a box in an attic."

Elizabeth looked at the box of swords, and then at Pathwell. "Isn't that what he did? At the bookstore? Sold something he wasn't using?"

"No," said Stansbury.

It came out flat and fast. Then he said, more quietly, "Nobody here is paying for anything. Nobody's spending what they'll need later."

He said it to the drying racks.

Pathwell didn't look at him either.

Stansbury picked up the box. "My car's by the loading dock," he said. "I've finished for the week. I want a drink."




It was an old maroon Cadillac, long as a boat, parked by itself under the only tree in the lot. Stansbury put the box of swords on the back seat.

Elizabeth got into the back, next to the box.

Pathwell, after a moment's thought, got into the back too, on the other side of the box.

Stansbury stood holding the driver's door and looked at the two of them, sitting in the back of his car like they were waiting for him to take them to the airport.

"I'm not your driver."

"You're everybody's driver, Stan," said Pathwell, and put his feet up.

Stansbury got in and slammed the door harder than he had to.

The radio was tuned to a station that was mostly static and one farm report, and Stansbury left it on. They drove out of the town and into fields, and the road went straight for miles in both directions, flat as a ruler.

Stansbury looked at his brother in the mirror.

"How thin?"

Pathwell watched the corn go by. "He said I didn't have much left."

"He who?"

"Him. At the shop."

Stansbury's jaw moved. "For what?"

"Books."

"Of course."

"And paper. They were writing on the backs of things."

"Well," said Stansbury. "That's all right, then."

Elizabeth leaned forward. "Much left of what?"

The road went on a long way before anybody answered.

"Of what he might have been," Stansbury said at last. "He sells it. Bits at a time. It doesn't come back."

Pathwell kept looking out of the window.

They came to a crossroads with a store on one corner that was also the post office and also, according to its sign, the bait shop, and behind it, sharing its gravel lot, a squat brown bar with one neon sign in the window, advertising a beer Elizabeth was fairly sure nobody had made in her lifetime.

Stansbury pulled in and turned off the engine.

"I'm going in," he said. "I'm having one drink, and I'm having it sitting down, and nobody is going to bleed or prune or be followed by anything while I do. You're both welcome."

He got out. Pathwell got out after him, and stood a moment at the bottom of the bar's steps, looking not at the bar but past it, at nothing in particular, at the corn across the road. He turned his shoulders a little to the left. Then a little to the right. Then he shook his head as though there was water in his ear, and went up the steps.

Elizabeth sat in the back seat beside the box of swords.

The bar door opened, and Stansbury came back down the steps and across the gravel and opened her door.

He looked at her. Then he reached into the box and took out the gray dagger and held it out to her, handle first. He did it the way people hand over their car keys to a teenager.

"Keep it inside your coat," he said. "Don't play with it."

"I'm a grown woman."

"Don't play with it," Stansbury said, and went back inside.

She turned the dagger over. It was light and soft and silly, and her hand closed around the handle like it had been waiting to.

She put it inside the borrowed blazer, against her ribs, where it poked her every time she breathed, and got out of the car, and went in.
