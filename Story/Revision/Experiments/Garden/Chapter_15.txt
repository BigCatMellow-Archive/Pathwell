Chapter Fifteen

The bucket line was still sorting itself out when she reached the archive steps. Hot air came down them at her in a rush, smelling of varnish and old paper.

Somebody behind her was yelling about the trees.

She looked round.

Shade was in the bucket line. Of course he was. Somebody had shoved a bucket at him and he'd taken it, the way he'd taken every bucket all morning, and he was bringing it up toward the fire in his left hand with his right arm still dragged out stiff behind him toward Pathwell on the ground, and the water slopping down his trouser leg as he came.

He took a step. The next one didn't come.

He looked down.

There was something on his foot. It lay flat on the ground like a puddle of ink that had decided to be there, black and wet, and it had hold of his boot. And it went back from his boot, along the ground, to the edge of the clearing, where the rest of it was coming out from between two trees. The trees didn't want it to. She could see that. The air between them had gone tight and wrong, as if Camp were trying to shut a door on it.

It came through anyway.

Low tide, under the smoke.

Shade let go of the bucket. It fell over and the water ran out. He pulled at his foot. The black came with it, and climbed, over the boot, to his shin, and tightened there, and he grabbed at it with his free hand and had to let it go again.

"Help!"

Not to anyone. Just help. The thing you shout.

Pathwell was lying on his back by the scattered pins, among the loose threads. He'd turned his head. He was close enough to hear.

A man came out of the bucket line at a run, a short wide man with a beard, and got both fists into the back of the green jacket and leaned back on it with all his weight. "I've got you," he said. "Hamid's got you. Pull." Shade got his free arm round Hamid's, and they pulled, the two of them together. Shade's shoulders came forward. His leg stayed where it was.

"Again," said Shade.

Inside the archive, a shelf went down with a crash.

"Milo!" The archivist was shouting from round the far side of the wagon.

And from inside, high and thin and choked with smoke: "Help! I can't get out! Help!"

Shade looked up at the archive door.

She'd heard both of them. He knew she had.

He looked at her.

Her good hand had already gone inside her blazer to the dagger. Split, from the museum. Warm. She'd cut Pathwell out of one of these in a parking lot. It had taken her four cuts and both arms and her whole weight dug in on her heels. She had one arm today. The other was strapped across her chest and hurt before she'd done anything with it at all.

Four cuts, maybe more, one-handed, with a blade that might give up at the first one. And then the door, with the smoke already coming out of it.

Or the door. Now.

Hamid got a fresh grip on the jacket. Behind the archive, Mama Baga was pulling people back from the burning end. Stansbury was gone round the back somewhere. She couldn't see him.

"Elizabeth!" Milo shouted from inside.

Shade was still pulling. He looked at the archive door, and back at her. Then he took his free hand off Hamid's arm, just for a second, and pointed it. At her. At the door.

"Go," he said.

"Not because you said so," she said. It came out cracked straight down the middle. "Because he's eleven."

She took the dagger out and put it into his hand, handle first, the way Stansbury had given it to her outside the bar. It would have to be him that cut.

Then she turned and went up the steps into the fire.




The heat was a hand on the side of her face, pushing. The rag rug inside the door was all flame, and the flames were climbing the dry wooden wall in quick yellow licks, and further in, the first shelves had begun to come apart, and every one of them was full of people.

Somebody's relief, so sudden and huge she nearly laughed out loud. Somebody's grief on top of it, and the laugh went out before it got to her mouth.

None of it was hers. All of it was in her way.

"Milo!"

"Here! Here!"

Low down. Left.

Her diary was on the floor just inside the door, where the tray had tipped. She nearly put her foot on it. It had landed face up and open, and the smoke was going over it in sheets, and there was her own handwriting, looking up at her off the floorboards.

Call hospice about the bed.
Return library books (hers).
Find a drawer for the spoon.

And under that, in pencil, crooked: Owe Shade breakfast.

Not crossed out. She'd paid it, that very morning, eggs over easy, one-handed, and it wasn't crossed out, because it wasn't hers to cross out anymore. She'd given it away, that same morning.

Her hand moved toward it. All by itself. The old checking, the thing her fingers had done a dozen times a day for four years, reaching for the one thing she never let out of her sight.

Something cracked further in, like a gunshot.

"Elizabeth!"

He knew her name.

She stepped over it.

The cookbook was just past it, on its shelf by the door, at the height of Milo's chin. Its little card support. The card in front. SHE DIDN'T STOP. The fire hadn't got there. She could have put out her hand and had it.

Milo screamed her name again.

She went past that too.




The shelf had come down across his legs.

It had come down at a slant, with its top corner jammed into the next cabinet, so it had him pinned and not crushed, and she understood in one look that that was the only good thing in the whole wagon and she was going to hold on to it.

He was on his back between two cabinets with his arm across his face and boxes all over him.

His sock was up. Today, of all days.

"Can you move your feet?"

He took his arm off his face. He was gray under the soot. "One."

"Which?"

"Left."

"Show me."

His left boot scraped.

"And the other?"

"It hurts."

"Hurts isn't can't. Try it."

He tried. It moved, under the shelf, about half an inch.

"Good," she said.

"It doesn't feel good."

"I know," she said, and heard herself, and heard Pathwell in it, and Shade, the three of them.

She got her good hand under the edge of the shelf and heaved. Nothing. Not an inch. She'd known it wouldn't. She'd had to try.

Nothing in that hand, the healer had said. Not a cup. Not a bucket. Not a baby, if anyone hands you one, and they will.

She looked at the shelf. It was big and solid and full of boxes, and she couldn't lift it with one arm.

But she'd moved house three weeks ago. She'd moved Nana's whole house down thirteen stairs before that, box by box, and she knew perfectly well, everybody who has ever moved house knows, that you don't lift with your arms.

"Milo," she said. "When I say, you pull your legs out. Both of them. Don't wait for me to say it twice."

"Okay."

She turned her back to the shelf. She crouched down, all the way, until she could get her back under the slanting edge of it, her good shoulder, her neck, the whole width of her. The bad arm she held tight against her stomach with the good one.

It was going to hurt. She knew how much. She did it anyway.

She stood up.

Not with her arms. With her legs, the way you stand up under a box of books, slow and straight, from the floor, and the shelf came up on her back, one inch, two, three, and her bad shoulder screamed and the world went white at the edges and she kept standing.

"Now!"

He went. She heard him scrabble and kick and twist, and a boot came off somewhere, and then he was out from under, all of him, and she let the shelf go behind her and it came down with a crash that shook the whole wagon on its wheels.

She was on her knees. She didn't remember getting there.

Milo crawled to her. "Can you get up?"

She laughed. It was the stupidest question anybody had ever asked her. "Can you?"

He got up. One bare foot. One sock, still up.

"Come here, then."

He came. She got her good arm round his ribs and his arm round the back of her neck, and she stood up with him, into the smoke, and took his weight on her good side.

He wasn't light. He was eleven, and all knees and elbows, and the smoke was in both of them. But she knew how to do this. Four years of it. Somebody's weight on your good side, and the stairs, and step, and step, not fast, never fast, because fast is how people fall.

"Door," she said.

They went.




The diary was still on the floor inside the door. They came to it together.

Milo saw it, and started to bend to pick it up, the way you would, the way anybody would.

She held on to him. "No."

"But it's yours—"

"Out."

He looked at her. Then he did what she said.

The cookbook was still on its shelf. The fire had got to the shelf below it, and the corner of its little card support had started to go brown.

She saw that too. She didn't stop.

She got Milo out through the door.

That was the choice.

Everything else burned afterward.




Outside, the bucket line had moved. Milo sat down hard in the dirt at the bottom of the steps and coughed, and coughed. She stood over him with her hand flat on his back, feeling him breathe, and looked across the smoke toward the scattered pins.

Shade wasn't there.

Hamid was on his knees in the dirt by the pins, looking at his empty hands as if he couldn't think what they were for. Behind him, the black thing was moving over the ground, slowly, where the loose threads lay. It went across one, and the thread went out underneath it like a match. Then another. Then another.

"Where is he?" said Elizabeth.

Hamid looked up at her. Then at the dirt next to him.

"We couldn't get him loose," he said.

He held up the dagger. It was in two pieces. It had split the rest of the way, point to handle, and when she took the pieces from him they were cold. As cold as if they'd been in a river.

"He kept cutting," said Hamid. "Every time it let go a bit, it closed up again. He kept on. He didn't stop." He swallowed. "Then it had him."

Pathwell was still lying where he'd fallen, on the far side of the thing. He hadn't moved. His head was turned toward the place where Shade had been standing.

The black thing went past him. For a moment it leaned his way, the way a dog leans to sniff at a man it doesn't know. Then it went on. It took up the last of the threads, one by one, and went back and forth over the ground a while, looking, and when there was nothing left to find, it drew itself back toward the trees, and in between them, and the air there closed up behind it with something like relief.

There was nothing left by the pins that looked like Shade. No green jacket. No hand held out.

There was a bucket, lying on its side, empty.

And beside it, face up in the trampled dirt, a playing card. The queen of spades. It must have come out of one of his pockets. He'd had so many of them.

Elizabeth went and picked it up. Then she put her hand flat on the front of her blazer, on the button, over the notebook, and held it there.




Stansbury came round the back of the archive at a run.

His sleeves were pushed up. His face was black on one side. In his left arm he was carrying the blue road notebook and a bundle of letters wrapped in a cloth, and his right hand he was holding out away from his body, stiff, the fingers spread, and the back of it was red and shiny all the way from the knuckles to the wrist.

"The back door," he said to her, before she could ask. "There's a back door. The shelf by it was going. I put my hand on the wrong side of it." He looked down at the hand as if it belonged to someone careless. "It's fine."

"It isn't fine."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He held out the notebook and the bundle. "Take these. I can't hold them properly."

The archivist was there, on his knees beside Milo, and he took them. The blue road notebook, with forty years of creek crossings in it, and arguments, and DO NOT TRUST THE SOUTH BANK down one margin in somebody's furious capitals. Damp, and blackened along one edge, and still itself.

Stansbury turned back toward the wagon.

Elizabeth caught his good sleeve. "No."

"There's a box by the back door. One more."

"You said you don't have to save everything."

"I said I know."

"Knowing it isn't the same."

He stopped. He actually stopped, and looked at her, and she watched him check.

Behind the wagon, the roof went with a long tearing sound, and sparks went up into the morning.

"No," said Stansbury quietly. "Not that one."

The healer reached him with a basin. She sat him down on an upturned crate and took his burned hand in both of hers without asking this time, and he let her.




The archive burned.

Every so often the things inside it came loose in bursts. Somebody laughing. A lullaby, two lines of it. A man saying a woman's name, over and over, like a man calling a dog home in the dark.

Elizabeth looked at the door.

The shelf by the door was alight now. Nana's cookbook had caught along the bottom edge. Nothing glowed. Nobody was spending it. It was only fire, going through old paper and floury card the way fire goes through anything. That was all fire knew how to do.

The cover lifted at one corner, and curled, and went black.

For one breath the smoke smelled like Nana's kitchen. Pepper, and flour, and sun through a window over a sink.

Then it was only smoke. She went on watching until she couldn't have told Nana's pages from any of the others.

The diary was nearer. The fire came to it along the rug. One page lifted, and browned, and went. Then the cover.

Her handwriting went out, a page at a time, without being used for anything. The dentist. The bus. The cat's foot. The list, and the crooked line under it.

She stood there with her one good hand. In it she had half a foam dagger, and the other half, and a playing card.

Behind her, Milo was coughing. He was breathing.




"Back," said Mama Baga. "Everyone. Back now."

Elizabeth went back, because it was sensible to.

Stansbury sat on his crate with his hand in the basin and watched the archive burn and didn't try to get up again.

The blue road notebook was lying on a folded blanket by the archivist's knee. The cloth bundle was next to it, still tied.

Not everything. Something.

Pathwell was still on the ground by the pins. Nobody had gone to him yet. There were other people hurt. There was other work to do first.

Elizabeth looked at the place where Shade had been. Hamid had gone back to the bucket line. There was nothing there now but trampled ground, and the bucket.

She thought about his coffee, black as a well. Then it's yours. The green jacket and every one of its pockets. Seven tricks, and Milo setting the trap the whole time, and Shade watching him do it. I'd like to finish that game.

Paid, he'd written. And drawn a line.

She put her hand on the blazer again. On the notebook. It was still warm. From his pocket, or from her. She couldn't tell anymore.

The roof of the archive fell in. The sound of it pushed everybody one more step back.

Elizabeth didn't look away from the empty ground.

The smell of the low tide thinned in the smoke, and thinned, and then it wasn't there.

Over by the pins, slowly, Pathwell's right hand opened on the dirt, and lay there open, and didn't close, and didn't point anywhere at all.
