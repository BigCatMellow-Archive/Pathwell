Chapter Nine

Shade drove the way people drive a borrowed car, carefully, as though somebody might ask for it back.

He drove at exactly the speed limit, with his hands at ten and two, and every mile or so the right one would come loose on its own and turn on its wrist toward the road behind them, like a dog lifting its head at a sound nobody else had heard. He'd put it back. Once, when it wouldn't go back, he sat on it and drove one-handed for a while.

"Is that him?" said Elizabeth.

He glanced up at the mirror. "Which?"

"Your hand. Doing that."

"Yes."

"Where is he now?"

His fingers tightened on the wheel. "Behind us."

She looked out the back window. There was nothing behind them but road.

"Can you hear him? Like, his thoughts?"

"No."

"Would you want to?"

"No."

She sat back. That was the most honest conversation she'd had in two days, and it had taken eleven words.

The road went through two towns that had shut down for the night and one that had never really opened. A gas station with one pump lit. A church sign that said ALL ARE WELCOME, ESPECIALLY YOU, DALE. A motel with a vacancy sign missing its V, so that it said ACANCY.

"Front's still empty," Shade said, after a while.

"I know."

He didn't ask again.




The diner sat by itself at the edge of a gravel lot under a big yellow sign that said, simply, DINER, and under that, in smaller letters, OPEN.

Shade parked and turned off the engine. "Coffee."

"You meant it."

"I usually do." He got out. He didn't look back to see whether she followed.

She sat in the back seat for a minute, looking at the warm yellow windows, at the three other cars, at a woman in a uniform going past the glass with a pot in her hand. There was nobody to tell her to come in, and nobody to tell her to stay put.

It turned out she wanted pancakes. She went in.

A bell over the door made a small, tired sound. The waitress looked up from the counter. She was about Nana's age, with her hair up in a net and reading glasses on a beaded chain, and her eyes went over Elizabeth, over the borrowed blazer and the green bandana and the gold chain and the dirt on her knees from the parking lot, and came to a private conclusion.

"Wherever you like, hon."

Shade had already taken the corner table, with his back to the wall. Elizabeth sat across from him, on the chair nearest the aisle, where she could get up without asking anybody to move.

The waitress came with two cups and a pot. Shade turned his cup over. After a second, Elizabeth turned hers over too.

It was terrible. Of course it was. It had been made a long time ago and kept warm ever since, and it tasted like the inside of a thermos. She drank it, and felt her shoulders come down.

Shade watched her do it.

"You like it bad," he said.

"I don't like it."

"Your face did something. When you drank it." He looked at his own cup. "His does that too. With bad coffee. I didn't know if that was his, or just a thing people do."

"I think it's a thing people do."

"Good," said Shade, and seemed to mean it.

The waitress came back with a pencil. Shade ordered eggs and toast, carefully, the way somebody orders in a language they learned from a book. Elizabeth looked at the menu and found she was starving, and ordered pancakes, and bacon, and a side of grits with extra pepper because she couldn't decide, and the waitress wrote it all down without looking at the pad and went away.

Shade reached into his coat and took out a notebook. It was small, the kind with a spiral at the top that you buy at a gas station, and he opened it on the table and took a pen out of the spiral, and wrote something.

Elizabeth watched him.

He wrote small and very straight. When he'd finished, he turned the notebook so she could read it, as if that were simply the polite thing to do.

Fri. Night. Diner (yellow sign). Coffee. Bad. Woman in back seat, Elizabeth. Not his.

"Not his," she said.

"You're not someone he knows. Anyone he knows is already in here." He touched his temple. "You're not. So you go in there." He touched the notebook.

She looked at the notebook. It was about a third full. "Can I see?"

He thought about it. Then he slid it across.

It started three weeks back. The handwriting was the same on every page, small and careful, the pen pressed in hard as though he didn't trust ink to stay. Almost none of it was important.

Rain. First time? Don't know. Cold. I didn't like it, then I did.
Dog outside the hardware store. Brown. It came to me. (Not because of him. I asked it.)
Bought this notebook. $1.29. This pen. Free with notebook.
Ate a pear. Didn't like it. Nothing in my head about pears. That one's mine, then.
The hand again. Bad today. Drove two hours. Stopped. Turned back. Drove two hours.
Learned the word "Tuesday" is mine now too.

She turned the pages slowly. She was very careful with them.

"Everything else," Shade said, looking out of the window, "is something I found already in here. It's his. It came with me. This is the only thing I have that isn't."

She closed the notebook and slid it back to him across the table, and he put it away inside his coat, on the left side, the side his hand didn't pull.

The food came, all at once, more plates than the table had room for. The pancakes were the size of hubcaps. Elizabeth ate half of one before she remembered to breathe.

Shade tasted his eggs. Then he salted them. Then he tasted them again, and wrote something down.

"What do you remember?" she said. "Of his."

He turned his cup around on the table. "Pieces."

"Give me some."

"A dog called Mister who never once came when he was called. How to fold a letter so it fits an envelope that's too small for it. A woman laughing on a ferry; I don't know who she is. Rain on a tin roof in a city I've never been to." He stopped. "How to say thank you in a language I don't speak. I don't know which language. I only know it's thank you."

"Is it a life? Like, can you remember being him?"

"No." He looked down at his hand, the right one, lying on the table. "There are too many pieces. There's nothing in between them. It's not like remembering. It's like having somebody else's pockets."

"Do you want to remember being him?"

He looked up. "No."

She ate some grits. They were very good, and very peppery, and she had the strange, clear feeling she sometimes got in waiting rooms, of being exactly where she was, all the way through.

"One of those things came through my door," she said. "In my apartment. With him standing in my living room. And another one, tonight, in a parking lot. It swallowed him."

Shade put his fork down. "It had him?"

"All of him."

"And now it doesn't."

"I cut him out of it."

Shade's eyes went to the foam dagger, lying on the table by her plate, and then back up to her.

"Good," he said.

"That's all?"

He thought about it. "You got him out. He's alive." He seemed to go back over that to see if he'd left anything out. "Then good."

His right hand was lying by his cup. While she watched, it turned over by itself, palm up, and the fingers came open, reaching a little, toward the window and the dark road. He looked at it the way you'd look at a dog that had got up on the furniture. Then he folded it shut and held it shut.

"No," he said, very quietly, to the hand.

He held it until it stopped.

"Does it hurt?"

"After a while."

"Then why not just go to him?"

He looked at her as though it were a strange question. "Because I don't want to."

She thought about the Cadillac, nose-down in the ditch, and her own hands on the wheel. She didn't say anything.

"Why did you come," she said, "the first time? To my building."

"I didn't know it was your building. I didn't know about you at all. The hand pulls. I follow it till I can't stand it, and then I stop following it till I can't stand that." He shrugged. "It doesn't tell me anything about where. Or who else is there."

Elizabeth put her fork down.

"He brought me with him," she said. "Because he decided the thing that came through my door was after me."

Shade frowned. "Why would it be?"

"It came through my door."

"With him behind it."

"Yes. With him behind it." She heard her own voice. "You think it was after him."

"I know what pulls me to him." Shade looked at his hand. "I don't know what pulls those. But one of them came to where he was, and so did I, and you were the only one there who wasn't either of us."

She sat with that.

"So it was never about me," she said.

Shade drank some of his bad coffee. "Then you were never the point."

He said it without any meanness at all. It was just a fact, the way pears were a fact.

It went into her a long way anyway. She'd been dragged out of her home, and down a street, and through a wall, and into the woods, and across half a state, because she was the point. That was what she'd been carrying, she realized, under everything else. A man had looked at her in the wreck of her living room and decided she mattered enough for something to come for her.

She hadn't. It hadn't.

"So what was the point?" she said.

"I don't know."

"You?"

"Maybe."

"Him?"

"Maybe."

"Both of you?"

"That would explain why they keep getting it wrong." He moved a piece of toast across his plate, very precisely. "We're too close together. Like two people with the same handwriting."

"What did he do?" she said. "To make you."

"I don't know all of it."

"What do you know?"

He was quiet a while.

"The edge of it," he said. "There's a kitchen table. Somebody's hands around a cup, an old woman's hands. She never drinks from it. And him, sitting across from her, being certain." He looked at the window, at his own faint reflection laid over the dark lot. "Then it stops. There's nothing after that I can find."

"Certain about what?"

"I don't know. That's not the part I have. I have the feeling." His hand lay still on the table. "Not that he was right. That he'd got to the point where it didn't matter anymore whether he was right. That he was going to fix it, and that was the end of the question."

Elizabeth thought of him in her doorway, saying marked. Saying shoes. Saying drive.

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because he looked at you," said Shade, "when you were talking. On the road. He doesn't look at me."

"He looked at you plenty."

"He looked at me the way you look at a stain."

"So I'm useful."

He didn't pretend otherwise. "Partly."

"What's the other part?"

He took the notebook out again, and didn't open it, and just held it.

"I'm tired," he said, "of carrying somebody else's answer around in my head to a question I never got to ask."

"What do you want from him?"

"For him to stop deciding what I am," said Shade, "before I get the chance to."

She looked down at her plate.

"So what do I ask him?"

"Don't ask me for what he did. It isn't mine to hand you." Shade looked at her across the table, straight, for the first time since the road. "He knows what he did. Ask him."




The waitress came by with the pot and lifted it at them, eyebrows up.

Elizabeth put her hand over her cup. Shade held his out, and watched it fill, and wrote something in his notebook.

"I'm going back," Elizabeth said.

"All right."

"Not for you."

"I didn't think it was."

"You don't get to think anything about it yet."

He considered this. Then, gravely, "All right."

She looked at the check where the waitress had left it, face down on the table. She looked at it the way you look at a bill when there's nothing in your pockets but your hands.

"I don't have any money," she said. "I left all of it on a bar. As a tip."

Shade took out a wallet. It was new, the leather still stiff, and there wasn't much in it, and he counted out what the check said and then the tip, exactly, to the cent, using a little arithmetic in the margin of his notebook to get it right.

"I'll pay you back," she said.

He stopped with the pen over the page.

"You'll owe me," he said.

"Yes."

He looked at her as though she'd handed him something he'd never seen and wasn't sure which end you held.

"Nobody's ever owed me anything," he said.

Then he wrote it down, very carefully, at the bottom of the page.

Elizabeth owes me breakfast.

He looked at it for a while after he'd written it.

Then, because she couldn't help it, and because there were dishes, she stacked their plates, and his cup on hers, and the cutlery on top, and carried them up to the counter. The waitress raised her eyebrows at her over the reading glasses and took them and said, "Well, aren't you a treat."

"Which way's the road back to the county highway? Toward the fields?"

The waitress told her. Then she told her again, slower, because of something she saw in Elizabeth's face, and then she took the pencil from behind her ear and wrote it on the back of an order slip in big square capitals and underlined the second left twice, and drew a little water tower next to it.

Elizabeth looked back at the booth.

Shade was sitting by himself with his coffee, writing. His right hand opened on the table, and he shut it again, without looking up.

She went out.

Outside it was still full dark and colder than before, and the cold found her wrists straight away. She stood in the yellow light of the sign with the order slip in her hand. She had no idea where Pathwell was by now. She knew where she'd last seen him, which would have to do.

She had a question now. She started walking.
