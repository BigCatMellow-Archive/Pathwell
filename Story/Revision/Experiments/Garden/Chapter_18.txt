Chapter Eighteen

Twenty-five days after the archive burned, Elizabeth walked round the corner to the dumpling place and left a message.

The woman behind the counter was doing her crossword. She looked up when Elizabeth came in under the red dragon, which had been mended and now had all four of its legs lit, and she looked at Elizabeth the way you'd look at a cat that had come back after a month away.

"He's not here," she said.

"I know. Can you give him a message?"

The woman put down her pen. "I always do."

"Tell him seven o'clock. Outside my building. Tell him I'd like to go to the bookshop."

The woman wrote it on the edge of her crossword. Then she looked up. "He'll want dumplings first."

"He can want them," said Elizabeth.

The woman looked at her a moment longer, and very nearly smiled, and went back to her crossword.




He was there at three minutes past seven. She made him wait until five past, because she was tying her shoe.

When she came down he was standing by the front steps with his hands in his coat pockets, and there was nothing wrong. Nothing following him. Nothing on fire. He looked strange like that. He looked like a man waiting for somebody, which, she supposed, was what he was.

The cut above his ear had healed to a thin pink line. He still turned carefully at the waist, from the ribs, and pretended he didn't, less than he used to.

He looked at her left arm, out of its sling now, and then looked away without asking how it was.

"Hello," he said.

"Hello."

"You have a door."

"I have a door."

"It's very brown."

"You're not allowed to look at it."

"I wasn't going to look at it."

She looked at him.

"I'm capable of not looking at things," said Pathwell, wounded. "I've been practicing."

"Three weeks ago you tried to put a skylight in a pink wagon."

"I thought about putting in a skylight."

"Out loud."

"And then I didn't. And it's still pink."

"It's still pink," Elizabeth agreed, and they started walking.

He didn't ask her why she wanted to go back. Not at the first corner, not at the second. She noticed, because the man she'd met at her own party would have asked before he'd even said yes, and then answered the question himself before she'd got to the end of her first sentence.

"You're not asking," she said.

"Asking what?"

"Why."

"I thought you'd tell me if you wanted to."

"That sounds like something you've practiced."

"I've had to practice it a great deal."

They walked. A bus went by. A man went past carrying a big bunch of flowers upside down, stems in the air, the heads knocking against his knee.

"I want to see it," she said, "when nothing's chasing us."

He nodded. And then he didn't say anything, and the not saying anything was all right.




It wasn't the brick wall by the bus bench. She'd thought it would be.

He took her the other way, round the corner past the laundromat that was always shut, to the little park she'd never seen before that first night. One bench. One tree. Both of them dry, this time.

He went up to the tree and stood in front of it and knocked on it. Three times, not loud, the way you'd knock on the door of somebody you knew was asleep.

Nothing happened.

He knocked again.

"Wrong tree?" said Elizabeth.

"There is only one tree."

"Wrong knock?"

"I am going to pretend you haven't said anything," said Pathwell, and knocked a third time, two short and a long, and the bark between two of the roots went soft and dark and opened, like a curtain somebody had drawn back from the other side, and there was warm air, and the smell of old paper, and somebody's very bad coffee.

She smiled before she could help it. He saw. He didn't make anything of it.

She went through first.




The coffee had not improved. She found that out inside four minutes.

Boots found her inside two. He came round the end of the counter with his tail up and stood in front of her and looked at her hand.

"No," said Elizabeth.

He sat down and washed his ear, as if it had never crossed his mind.

The shopkeeper looked up from his ledger. He looked at her. He looked at Pathwell coming through behind her. He looked at the two of them for a long time, at their entire lack of blood, and smoke, and monsters, and handcarts, and collapsing practitioners, and urgent errands for Camp, and his face became quite troubled.

"What's happened?" he said.

Pathwell stopped. "Nothing."

The shopkeeper's frown got deeper. "I don't care for it."

Elizabeth put a box down on the counter.

It was a cake. A small one, from the grocery store on the corner, white, with yellow roses on it made of something that wasn't quite icing, and across the middle, in blue, in letters she had asked the woman at the bakery counter to pipe on for her while she waited, it said THANK YOU.

The shopkeeper looked at it over his glasses.

"It's from a grocery store," said Elizabeth. "It's for the coffee. In advance."

The shopkeeper looked at it a while longer. Then he lifted the lid. Boots got up from his ear and came to look too.

"Paid," said the shopkeeper, and wrote it in the ledger, and put the lid back on very carefully, and moved the box behind the counter, out of Boots's reach, where it sat for the rest of the evening being looked at by the cat.

"You've never once brought him anything," Elizabeth said to Pathwell.

"I've brought him a great deal."

"Bought from him. Not brought."

Pathwell looked at the cake box, behind the counter, and didn't say anything to that.

"Your account," the shopkeeper said to him, "remains at nothing."

"You say that as if it's going to rain."

"A month isn't a habit, Mr. Pathwell. It's a month."

"Cruel."

"Bookkeeping," said the shopkeeper.

Elizabeth laughed. This time, nobody looked surprised.




She went into the shelves.

The first time she'd come in here, they'd crowded in on her until she couldn't tell where she stopped and the room began. They still pressed. She could still feel them, coming off the spines, all those people. A row of swollen journals that made her ache with somebody's homesickness, and she stepped away from it. A bundle of school notes so full of embarrassment that she smiled, without having the faintest idea who'd been embarrassed, or why. A charcoal drawing of three children by a fence that made her want to telephone someone whose number she didn't have.

She didn't touch any of them. She kept walking, and let them be.

Pathwell stayed near the counter. Not following her. Not guarding her. Talking quietly with the shopkeeper about something that seemed to involve a bridge, and a debt the shopkeeper insisted had been run up in 1912 by a man who'd died owing it, and whether inheriting a man's toolbox meant you'd inherited his library fines.

Elizabeth came to a narrow shelf of recipe books. Not printed ones. The other kind. Spiral notebooks fat with use. Tins of index cards. School exercise books with grease gone through the corners. Pages written in one hand and corrected in another and argued with in a third.

And the place where Nana's book had been, inside her, opened up all at once, like a door in a wall.

The flour on the cover. The sugar cookies, torn. Chicken and dumplings. MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. The last pages, pressed in without any ink. SHE DIDN'T STOP.

The fire.

She put her hand in her coat pocket. Not to check for the diary. Just to have somewhere to put it. There was an index card there, folded once, in her own leaning handwriting. WATCH THE PEPPER, LIZZY. And a playing card, soft at the corners now from being carried.

On the end of the shelf, a book was lying on its own. A school composition book, black and white, the cover worn gray, and stuck across the front, a strip of masking tape gone brown, and on the tape, in a big round hand:

MOM'S BOOK. DO NOT LEND. (THIS MEANS YOU, DEBBIE.)

Pathwell had stopped talking. She looked round. He was looking at the same book.

Of course he was.

"Just came in," said the shopkeeper, following his eyes. "From a family. Three sisters. They copied out every recipe before they brought it. None of them had room for it, and none of them would let either of the others keep it."

"That's sad," said Elizabeth.

"It's extremely common."

Pathwell came across. Elizabeth stayed where she was.

He stopped beside her and looked down at the book without touching it. "That's a strong one."

"I thought it might be."

"You can feel it?"

"No," said Elizabeth. "I can see the gravy on the cover."

He looked. "Could be soup."

"It's gravy."

He looked at the shopkeeper. "May I?"

The shopkeeper looked at him over his glasses for a moment, as though Pathwell had said something in a language he hadn't known Pathwell spoke.

"Yes," he said.

Pathwell picked it up, and opened it.

Inside the cover were names, one under another. Different pens, different pressure. Dolores. Then Dolores's Marie. Then three more, in three young hands, all on one line, all crowded, as if they'd been made to share it: Annie, Kath, Debbie.

A recipe for meatloaf, with the oven heat crossed out twice and a note in the margin: MOM LIED ABOUT THE OVEN.

And under it, in a different hand: SHE DID NOT. YOUR OVEN IS WRONG, KATH.

Elizabeth leaned in, without meaning to.

On the next page: SOUP FOR WHEN YOU'RE SAD. And under that, years later, in Debbie's round hand: ALSO WORKS FOR BREAKUPS. And under that: NOT VERY WELL.

Elizabeth laughed, quietly. "They sound all right."

"For a family," said Pathwell.

He turned another page, and his smile went.

He was looking at the shopkeeper's ledger now, lying open on the counter under the cat. He looked at it, and didn't look away.

Elizabeth knew what he was thinking. She could see the whole sum of it on his face. A book, as full as Nana's had been. A gap in her that a book had left. A ledger, open. And all of him that he had left to pay with, in that arm.

His left hand came over and closed round his right wrist, on the outside of the sleeve, and held it.

Then he shut the book, gently, and set it back down at the end of the shelf exactly where it had been, square to the edge.

He took his hand off it.

For a while nobody said anything at all.

The shopkeeper took his glasses off and polished them on his cardigan. "I think I may need to sit down," he said.

"You are sitting down."

"Inside," said the shopkeeper. "I'm sitting down inside. Don't spoil it."




The shopkeeper poured her coffee. She took half a cup, anyway.

It was just as bad. Shade would have liked it.

Pathwell said no to his.

"Coward," said Elizabeth.

"I'm basing it on evidence."

He moved the empty cup an inch to the left on the counter, and then back where it had been. Down at the far end, the shopkeeper had found something in his ledger that needed a great deal of his attention.

"I said I'd tell you," said Pathwell. "The rest of it."

She put her cup down.

"When it had hold of him," he said. "In the field. I was lying on the ground. I couldn't get up. I'd have tried, and I couldn't have. But I could talk. I could have shouted. Stan was thirty feet away. Mama was right there. There were twenty people with buckets." He was looking at the empty cup. "He was calling out. Help. Not to anybody. Just help. And I lay there, and I heard him, and I thought: if it takes him, it's over. The pull. The things coming. All of it. Finished, and nobody has to choose anything."

He stopped.

"So I didn't shout," he said. "I could have. I didn't."

The shop was very quiet. Somewhere in the shelves, something settled, the way old paper does.

"I didn't help him either," said Elizabeth.

He looked up.

"He was calling, and then Milo was calling. And I went to Milo." Her voice was steadier than she'd thought it would be. "I left him with Hamid and a knife that was already cracked, and I went into the fire."

Pathwell shook his head. Slowly.

"You went in to get a child out of a fire," he said. "With one arm. You heard two people, and you went to the one you could reach. That's what you did." He put his hand flat on the counter. "I heard one person, and I lay there, and I hoped. That's what I did. They're not the same thing, Elizabeth. Don't let me borrow yours."

She didn't say anything. The shop let her not say it.

She'd thought, coming here, that if he told her, she'd know what she felt about it. She didn't. She felt a great many things, and none of them were forgiveness, and none of them were the opposite.

She took her hand out of her pocket. In it was the playing card. The queen of spades, soft at the corners, a little bent from being carried for three weeks against an index card.

She laid it face up on the counter between them, next to his empty cup.

He looked at it.

He didn't touch it.

"Shade," he said.

Just that. It was the first time she'd heard him say it.

She left it there a moment, on the counter. Then she picked it up again and put it back in her pocket, with the recipe, where it lived.

Then she went back into the shelves.

She didn't look at anything for a while. She stood in an aisle where nobody could see her and held on to the edge of a shelf, the way you hold on to a rail on a boat, until her breath came back from wherever it had gone.

Then she looked. She was there a long time. It was hard to tell how long, in there, with no windows and no clocks and nobody, for once, in any hurry to spend any.

She found a recipe for a cake that somebody's grandfather had only ever made once, in 1951, with a note at the bottom that said NEVER AGAIN, and another note, years later, in another hand, that said WE MADE IT FOR HIS FUNERAL. IT WAS AWFUL. HE WAS RIGHT. She found a diary kept entirely in the form of weather reports. She found a single envelope with nothing written on it at all, and didn't open it.

When she came back to the counter, Pathwell was still there. He hadn't moved from it. He hadn't gone near the recipe shelf again.

"How do I get out?" she said to the shopkeeper.

"Out to where?"

She thought about it.

The first time she'd stood here, he'd offered her the sidewalk, and she'd chosen a cart full of somebody else's books instead, and a road in the dark, without having the least idea where it went. Now home was there, if she wanted it, with its brown door. Camp was there, and a pink wagon, and a boy who'd want a rematch. Her job would still be there in the morning, with its forty emails. Her surviving plant would still want water in a way she found faintly manipulative.

She looked at Pathwell.

"Where can you actually get us back from?"

He blinked. "That's a very responsible question."

"I've had to practice it," said Elizabeth.

He thought. "From here? Four. Four without making him invent anything."

"Five," said the shopkeeper, writing.

"The fifth is seasonal."

"The season started on Tuesday."

Pathwell frowned at him. "I hate it when you know things about your own shop."

"It's my shop."

"Tell me all five," said Elizabeth.

He told her. She listened.

One came out by a bus station, which was handy. One came out on a bridge, at night, which was lovely, and full of people. One came out in a place you could only leave on Thursdays. He told her about the fourth one for a long time, and in great detail, which made her think there was something wrong with it. About the fifth, he said very little, and kept looking at the floor.

"What's wrong with the fifth one?"

"Nothing's wrong with it."

"You made a face."

"There's a lighthouse," said Pathwell.

"That's not a face kind of thing."

"The keeper charges a song. To pass. Everybody. Every time."

"You have to sing?"

"I have to sing."

Elizabeth looked at the shopkeeper. He went on writing.

"Is that true?"

"He sings flat," said the shopkeeper.

Elizabeth turned back to Pathwell.

"That one," she said.

"Of course."

"Is that a problem?"

She watched the old answer come up into his face, the one he'd have given her a month ago, the one about how it wasn't ideal. She watched it get there. Then she watched him put it down.

"No," he said, and he smiled. "No problem."

She turned to the shopkeeper. "Can we use it?"

"Yes."

"Both of us?"

"If he's going."

She looked at Pathwell. He was standing at the counter with one hand flat on it, by the empty cup.

"Do you want to come?" she said.

He looked surprised. As though nobody had asked him that in a very long time. He looked at the recipe shelf, and at the ledger under the cat, and at the bit of wall where a door hadn't appeared yet. Then at her.

"Yes," he said.

"Then come on."

The shopkeeper shut his ledger.

A narrow door was there, beside the shelf of journals, where there hadn't been a door. Old wood, a brass handle worn pale by a lot of hands, and a thin line of cold, salty air coming in underneath it, and somewhere on the other side of it, a long way below, the sound of the sea.

Somebody on the other side cleared his throat, meaningfully.

Pathwell's face fell. "He remembers me."

Elizabeth put her hand on the brass handle. "Then he'll be glad you came."

She looked back. He'd already come away from the counter, and he came and stood next to her. His right hand hung loose at his side. Nothing pulled it anywhere.

She kept her hand on the door. Her other hand was in her pocket, round both of the cards.

"Are you ready?"

"No."

"Perfect."
