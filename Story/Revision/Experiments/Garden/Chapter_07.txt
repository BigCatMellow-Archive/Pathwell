Chapter Seven

The smell came around the car before anything else did. Low tide on a hot day, the same as her hallway, the same as her coat.

Elizabeth put the keys in her pocket. Her hand found the foam dagger through the blazer on its own.

Pathwell had stopped in the middle of the lot. Not the way he usually stopped, with a flourish, as if the world had been waiting for his entrance. He'd just stopped.

"Where?" said Stansbury.

"Other side."

Something came up out of the tall grass beyond the Cadillac's back bumper. It came up the way a wave comes up, all at once and too big, a wet dark fold of something that had no business being that size in a gravel lot behind a bar, and it hung there a moment at the height of the car's roof, the evening light sliding off it.

Elizabeth stepped back.

It didn't look at her. It didn't have anything to look with. But it went past her the way water goes past a rock, and it went for Pathwell.

"Oh," said Pathwell.

Stansbury already had the back door of the Cadillac open. He came out with a foam sword in his fist and swung it across the thing's leading edge, and the sword lit orange and went in up to the handle, and the blob shivered from one end to the other and kept coming.

Pathwell went through his coat. Every pocket, fast, the inside ones and the outside ones, and came up with a button, two brass pins, a bit of string and a toffee.

"Paper!" Stansbury shouted. "Any paper!"

"I gave it all to Camp!"

"All of it?"

"They were writing on the backs of things, Stan!"

It hit him at the waist. It folded up over him from the bottom, smooth and fast, like somebody pulling a sheet up over a bed, and for a second there was still a hand, his hand, out at the edge of it, opening and closing on nothing.

Then there wasn't.

"Pathwell!"

Stansbury caught her arm. "Don't touch it—"

She pulled her arm out of his hand.

It cuts what clings.

She had the dagger out. It weighed nothing. It was foam, and gray, and silly, and her hand was shaking so badly the point drew little circles in the air.

The smell was so thick up close that her eyes ran. There wasn't a surface to it she could hold her eyes on; every time she looked straight at an edge it slid off sideways, like trying to read a word in a dream. She could see bits of him through it. The heel of a shoe. The tail of his coat. A white patch of wrist, gone again.

She put the dagger in where the black met his coat.

It should have bent. It didn't. The gray blade lit along its edge, thin and orange, and something gave, not like cutting meat or cloth but like a zipper, like something that had been closed coming open, and the whole mass flinched away from it in one long ripple.

She drew the dagger down, and the opening came down with it. His shoulder. His jaw. His face, gray, eyes shut, mouth open as if he'd been about to say something clever when it got him.

"Come on," she said.

She got her free hand into the front of his coat and pulled. Nothing. She set her feet in the gravel and pulled harder, and the thing pulled back, and where its edge touched her sleeve the cold went straight through the cloth and into her arm and up it, and her fingers stopped working.

She cut again, across his chest, where it held him tightest.

It let go all at once. Elizabeth went over backward, and he came with her, and they hit the gravel together, him on top, and every bit of breath she had left her.

Nobody moved.

Then he coughed. Once. Twice. It was a horrible, wet, rattling sound, and she had never in her life been so glad of a noise.

She got out from under him and rolled him over. "Breathe."

He opened one eye. "Working on it."

"Work faster."

"Noted," he said, and then he coughed for a while.

Pins and needles were coming back into her hand, hundreds of them. Off to one side, Stansbury had dropped the sword. He had a short foam baton now instead, orange from end to end and glowing, about as threatening as a pool noodle, and as the blob gathered itself up to come at Pathwell again, Stansbury stepped in between them and struck the gravel once with the end of it.

Heat. Like opening an oven door with your face too close. A line of white light ran out through the stones under the thing, and it buckled.

Stansbury hit it again, square, and fire went through it. Not on it. Through it, from the inside, catching on nothing else, not the grass, not the gravel, not the Cadillac's tires a yard away. The blob fell in on itself. It came apart into heavy wet pieces that hissed and flattened and thinned until there was nothing left but some black streaks in the gravel, and a smell that showed no sign of leaving.

Inside the bar, somebody laughed at something. The screen door didn't open. Nobody had noticed a thing.

"Is it dead?" said Elizabeth.

Stansbury put the baton down on the hood of his car, carefully, the way you'd put down a hot pan. "It's gone."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what I know."

Pathwell got himself up onto his elbows, and then sitting. His hands were shaking. Not just the right one now. Both of them.

She took his arm. He leaned on her, and didn't make a joke about it, which worried her more than the shaking.

Stansbury crouched in front of him. "Look at me."

"Do I have to?"

"Look at me."

Pathwell looked. Stansbury held his chin and looked into one eye and then the other, and then he put two fingers against the side of his brother's neck and waited, with his own eyes on his watch.

"Did you lose anything?"

"My dignity. A toffee."

Stansbury pressed his ribs, and Pathwell's breath stopped halfway in and stayed there.

"Bruised," Stansbury said. "Not broken. You're going to hate breathing for a week, and you're going to tell me about it every day."

He sat back on his heels. He looked at the black streaks in the gravel, a long time.

"It went for you."

Pathwell didn't say anything.

"It went right past her," Elizabeth said. "It didn't even slow down."

Pathwell looked at her. Then at the gravel.

Elizabeth was still holding his arm. She'd been holding on to it since she got him out, she realized, and she didn't let go.

"In the hall," Stansbury said, still to his brother. "You came back from the hall wrong. What happened in the hall?"

"Nothing."

"His hand," said Elizabeth.

Both of them looked at her.

"There's a mirror back there." She heard her own voice, flat and very careful, like a witness. "His real hand was a fist. In the mirror it was open. Like this." She held her own right hand out, palm up, toward Pathwell. "Like it wanted him to come with it."

Nobody said anything.

Pathwell closed his eyes.

"Thank you, Elizabeth," he said.

He said it so politely.

Stansbury didn't look surprised. That was the thing she noticed. He looked like a man who'd been told a number he'd been afraid of, and was now doing sums with it, very quietly, behind his face.

"Is that bad?" she said. "What does that mean?"

"Car," said Pathwell, and got up.




He tried the passenger door. Stansbury put a hand flat on it.

"Back."

"I hate the back."

"You were happy enough in it this afternoon."

"That was a choice. This is a sentence."

Stansbury opened the passenger door for himself and got in.

Elizabeth looked at the keys in her hand, and at the car, which was very long, and very old, and very much not hers.

She got in behind the wheel. She had to pull the seat forward almost to the dash. The engine caught on the second try, with a cough like an old man getting up from a chair.

Pathwell got in behind her, gingerly, with one arm around his own ribs.

She pulled out of the lot and onto the road the way the car was already facing. In the mirror, the bar's one neon sign got smaller and went out.

There was nothing out here but fields and the dark and the car's own headlights. She held the wheel at ten and two, which she hadn't done since her driving test, and found out how much car there was in front of her.

For a while nobody said anything.

Then Stansbury said, low, toward the back seat, "How long?"

"A while."

"How long is a while?"

"Long enough that I stopped noticing it was new." Pathwell's voice came from the dark behind her. "A pull. Not all the time. Like something caught on a nail."

"Toward what?"

"I don't know."

"Since when?"

Pathwell didn't answer.

"Since the prune that went wrong," said Stansbury. It wasn't a question.

"Maybe."

"Which one?"

Pathwell said nothing at all.

Elizabeth looked in the mirror. She could see half his face in the light off the dash. "What prune?"

Nobody answered her.

"Hello?"

"It's nothing you need to worry about," Pathwell said.

"I just pulled you out of a monster."

"And I'm grateful."

"I saw your hand in the mirror. I'm the one who told him."

"I know."

"So what prune?"

"Elizabeth." He said it gently, the way you'd say it to a child at a funeral who's started asking questions too loudly. "Drive."

Her hands went still on the wheel.

It wasn't a cruel word. It was worse than that. It was a useful one. It was the word you said to the person whose job it was to get everybody where they were going, so that the people who mattered could talk.

She drove.

Behind her, the two of them went on, low, in a kind of shorthand she couldn't follow, half sentences and names she didn't know and long silences that meant more than the words did. Stansbury asked something about Ada, and Pathwell said no, and then said, not like that, and then didn't say anything. Every now and then one of them glanced at the back of her head, the way you'd check on a sleeping dog.

The road went on, straight as a ruler. The headlights found a reflector, and a mile marker, and then nothing, for a long way.

She'd been good at this, she thought. For four years she'd been very, very good at this. Driving to appointments. Sitting in the hall while the doctor talked to Nana. Waiting in the car.

She drove, and listened to them not tell her, and her hands got steadier on the wheel instead of less.
