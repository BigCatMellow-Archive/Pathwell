Chapter Two

He took the stairs two at a time, and she took them one at a time with a cake, so by the second landing he was a whole floor ahead and talking to her over the railing.

"Keep up, if you can."

"I'm carrying a cake."

"Nobody made you."

The lobby smelled the way it always did, carpet cleaner and somebody's cigarettes, which after her living room was almost nice. He shouldered the front door open and held it, and she came out after him into the cold.

It took her breath. She stood on the step getting it back in short white puffs.

Her window was the only one lit on the third floor. She'd left the kitchen light on. Under it, on the counter, was Nana's wooden spoon, the one thing she'd unpacked in three weeks. She'd wanted something of Nana's out where she could see it while she decided about the rest. She'd been meaning to find a drawer for it.

Her feet turned back toward the door on their own.

"Don't," he said, not unkindly.

"My spoon's up there."

"It'll keep. Spoons are very patient."

She looked up at the window one more time. Something crossed it, a dark shape against the light, and then it was just the light again.

She came down the step.

He was already walking, and she caught him up at the corner.

"Where are we going?"

"Dumplings. There's a place right around here."

"I have a meeting at nine."

"Right around the corner. Swear."

"I have a meeting at nine," she said again, in case he hadn't heard the first time, or had heard it and didn't care, and both were bad. "And my landlord. What do I even tell my landlord about the door? I have to pick up my dry cleaning before work, they open at seven-thirty. I don't have my wallet. I don't have my keys. I don't think I locked—" She stopped. There wasn't a door to lock. "Should I call the police?"

He let her run all the way down, nodding along, the way you'd wait out a car alarm. He was looking up at the streetlight while he did it.

"What's the meeting about?"

"What?"

"At nine. What's it about?"

"I don't know yet." She moved the cake to her other arm. "It's my first one."

He looked at her then, and seemed to decide against whatever he'd been going to say.

They turned a corner she didn't know, and then another. She'd lived here three weeks and walked to the train every morning and back every night, and there should have been a coffee place on this corner and a dry cleaner after it. Instead there was a laundromat with its shutter down, and a little park she'd never seen, one bench and one tree, both wet.

"This isn't my neighborhood."

"It is, technically. You just haven't been to this part."

"There isn't a this part."

"And yet."

The dumpling place was a counter open to the street, under a red neon dragon that had lost the light in one of its legs, with a few plastic tables out on the curb. It was the only thing lit on the block. The woman behind the counter looked up from her crossword, saw him, and looked back down.

"Two of the fried," he said. "For me."

"I'll get my own," Elizabeth said.

He raised his eyebrows. "Thought you didn't have your wallet."

"I don't."

She put the cake on one of the curb tables and went through her coat pockets. Coins, the ones she always forgot about. A lip balm. A hair tie. She laid the coins out on the table in a row and counted them, and then counted them again, as if that might help.

Two dollars and sixty-five cents.

The sign over the counter said an order was four.

"I'll spot you," he said.

"No."

"It's a dollar thirty-five."

"I don't want to owe you a dollar thirty-five." She went through the pockets one more time, all of them, in case. "I don't want to owe you anything."

Her fingers closed on a square of thin paper folded in half. She knew what it was before she had it out.

The dry-cleaning ticket. Bright yellow, number 4471, her gray suit, the one that made her look like somebody who'd been at a job longer than three weeks. Ready after 7:30.

She could still do it. She could be at the dry cleaner when it opened and at work by nine, in the suit, and sit in the meeting and nod and find out what it was about, and none of this would have happened, not in any way that showed.

She smoothed the ticket flat on the table beside her coins, so she could look at it.

A truck went past close enough to the curb to rock the table. The wind off it picked up the ticket, turned it over once, and laid it in the gutter, and the water there took it straight to the storm drain and through the grate.

She looked at the grate.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. I can just—"

There wasn't anything she could just. She pressed both hands flat on the table where the ticket had been, and the smell came up out of her coat again, wet and rotten, and her mouth opened for the next thing and nothing came.

She didn't know when she started crying, only that she couldn't make it stop. It wasn't the suit. It was three weeks of boxes she hadn't opened. It was a whole house she'd put into garbage bags, and a new place on the far side of the city she'd picked because it was far, and it hadn't felt like anything. She'd been waiting and waiting for it to feel like something.

She put her face in her hands.

The other chair scraped. He set the paper tray down between them, eager, and then he saw her.

He let out a long, put-upon sigh, and pushed the tray to the middle of the table.

"Half," he said. "Half, and that's the deal."

She looked at the dumplings through her fingers. They sat there shining with grease.

He picked up her coins, all of them, the whole two sixty-five, and dropped them into his own pocket.

"There," he said. "Now you've paid."

"That's not how—"

"It is tonight."

She took one. She didn't want to. It was hot and much too greasy, and it was the best thing she'd eaten in three weeks, and she was not going to tell him that.

He ate the rest of his half with great seriousness, and then started looking at hers.

Her face was a mess. Her nose was running. She used one of his napkins, and he let her.

"Who the hell are you?"

He looked up.

"Pathwell."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"That's your first name?"

"That's all the names. People keep trying to give me more, and I keep handing them back."

"Why were you at my party?"

"I like parties. Nobody asks who you came with." He looked at the cake box. Then he looked at her. Then at the cake box.

She put her hand on the lid.

"No," she said.

He looked hurt, for a second, really hurt, and then he laughed, short and surprised, as if it had gotten out without asking him.

"Fair," he said.

"Nobody from work knows you."

"Your work isn't very curious." He ate her last dumpling while she wasn't guarding it. "Your boxes, though. Your boxes were the most interesting thing in the building, Lizzy."

It went through her like cold water.

"Don't call me that."

"Sorry. Elizabeth." He said it carefully, like he'd had to look it up. "You're mostly Lizzy, in there." He tipped his head at the diary under his arm. "Somebody called you that a lot."

"You read it."

"Some." He didn't look sorry, but he didn't look at her either. "It's good. I mean it. People don't usually write down the true stuff."

"That's not an answer."

"It's a lot of one." He sat back. "So. Night's young. Where to?"

"Excuse me?"

"Anywhere you like. Pick."

"Home."

He made a small noise that might have been a laugh.

"You wrecked my apartment," she said. "There's that stuff all over everything. You won't let me go back. I have a meeting at nine that I can't miss, my suit is in a storm drain, and you want to know where to next."

"First off, I'd stay away from your place tonight, if it was me." He pointed a chopstick at her. "Second, you ate those dumplings. I watched you."

"They were terrible."

"They were. They're exactly as good as they look, every single time. You'd be amazed how rare that is."

He stood, and brushed his hands off. "That being said." He gathered up the diary, and the cookbook with it. "It's up to you, what you do next." He tucked them both under his arm. "The choice is yours, Lizzy."

Then he walked off up the street, not fast, the way people walk when they don't need to look back to know what you'll do.

She sat there in the red light from the dragon. The tray was empty. The woman at the counter filled in a word.

And then, from nowhere, she heard it again. Not his voice.

*Lizzy.*

Warm. The smell of butter. An oven door open, and a hip bumping her out of the way.

She looked at the table. The tray. The napkins. The cake.

He still had the cookbook.

She was on her feet before she'd finished thinking it. Then she came back for the cake.
