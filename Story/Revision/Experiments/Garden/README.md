# The Garden: Chapters 1–3 grown in Sunday Morning mode

## Status

**Experiment, not the manuscript.** bt 7274 asked (2026-11-02) for a version of the first act closer in tone to the 10 Kings Sunday Morning drafts, written the way George R.R. Martin describes a *gardener* writing: the beats are known, the route between them is allowed to grow. His terms: "fresh soil" (the current chapters are reference only, no prose transplanted) and Chapters 1–3 before he reads. After reading them ("I love it, let's continue") he asked for more; Chapters 4–6 followed the same day. The files in `Story/Chapters/` are unchanged and remain the current text. Nothing here becomes canon unless he chooses it.

His metaphor, as this experiment reads it:

- **Soil:** what has to be true for anything to grow. The fixed beats and the objects later chapters lean on.
- **Water:** what keeps things growing. What each person wants, scene to scene.
- **Pruning:** cutting what grew but doesn't serve the garden, even when it's pretty.

Every line in these chapters is scaffolding for him to write over ([who writes what](../../../Sunday-Morning/Craft.md#ai-and-the-author-who-writes-what)). The [growth log](Growth-Log.md) records what sprouted that wasn't planned, and what was pruned.

## What made the 10 Kings drafts a joy (the target)

1. Humor comes from the people: each character has one way of being funny, and it is how they care.
2. Plain narration; the joke is a quiet half-sentence riding on a straight one.
3. Small stakes with something big behind them.
4. One device planted early, paid late.
5. Objects carry history without explanation.
6. One quiet sad beat, left quiet.
7. Endings are gestures, not speeches.
8. A clean shape: one problem, a deadline, a new place and a new person per scene.

## Water: what each person wants

Using the [Scrubs scaffold](../../Author-Notes/2026-10-27_Irreverence-With-Heart-Scrubs.md): one habit, funny in one light and painful in another.

- **Elizabeth** wants something to carry. She spent years carrying plates up Nana's stairs, and now there's no one upstairs. Her habit: handle it, tidy it, be useful, accommodate. Funny when she worries about the door and the dry cleaning in the middle of a monster. Painful when there's nothing left to carry.
- **Pathwell** wants to be the one who fixes it. His habit: treat chaos as an errand and pay for it himself, fast, without asking anyone. Funny when a monster is a chore and dumplings are urgent. Painful when the cost is his own future, and he waves it off.
- **The shopkeeper** wants the books to balance. He dislikes paperwork and does nothing else.
- **Boots** wants tribute.

The act's one problem: **get Nana's cookbook back.** The deadline: **a meeting at nine.**

## Pruning: the test for anything that grows

1. bt 7274's removal test: if I cut it, does the story change? If not, cut it and don't replace it.
2. Does it feed a soil beat or water a person? If it does neither, prune it, however pretty it is.
3. One quiet sad beat per chapter, left quiet.
4. Chapters end on a gesture, not a speech.
5. Nothing explains what the scene already showed. Nobody gives a magic lecture.
6. Walk the scene physically: who holds what, who is where.
