Chapter Sixteen

She was sitting on the ground. She didn't remember sitting down.

All round her, Camp went on working, because the fire didn't care whether anybody had sat down. People were rolling the water barrels further off. Somebody was stamping out bits of burning paper that had blown under the wheels of the next wagon. The bucket line had moved off to the left, away from the front of the archive, because there was no front of the archive anymore for water to help.

Mama Baga stood in the middle of all of it with soot down one cheek and pointed at things.

"That wheel."

Two people went to the wheel.

"Children behind the blue wagon. All of them. Count them."

Somebody started counting.

"Leave the front. The front's gone. Wet the side, so it doesn't jump."

The line moved again.

Nobody said, can we save it. Everybody knew.

Milo was sitting six feet away on a horse blanket with his right trouser leg slit up to the knee. His right foot was bare. On his left foot, his sock had come down at last, and was round his ankle, where it belonged.

The healer was pressing along his shin with two fingers, one inch at a time, watching his face.

"There?"

"No."

"There?"

"Ow."

"Thank you."

"That's not a thank you thing," said Milo, outraged.

"It is to me."

The archivist sat behind him on the blanket, with one long hand flat on the middle of his back. Not holding him down. Just there.

Elizabeth watched the healer press, and Milo say ow, and the archivist's hand on his back.

Alive. The word kept coming to her, over and over, as if somebody had been told to keep bringing it until she signed for it.

Alive.

No diary. No cookbook. No Shade.

Milo said ow.

"Elizabeth."

The healer had finished with Milo and was crouched in front of her now, looking at her left side. The sling was gone, torn off in the archive. Her arm was just hanging there, inside the borrowed blazer, at an angle that didn't look like it was asking anyone's permission.

"May I?"

Elizabeth very nearly said yes without thinking. She stopped herself. She thought about it. The stopping didn't hurt as much as the shoulder did.

"Yes."

The healer's fingers went along the bone. Elizabeth said something she had never said out loud in her life.

Milo looked deeply impressed.

"Still in," said the healer, after a long time. "Swollen. Badly. You did every single thing I told you not to do with it."

"There was a shelf."

"I heard about the shelf. I heard about you standing up under it." The healer made a new sling out of the cloth from her own apron and tied it at the back of Elizabeth's neck, too tight, and didn't say sorry. "Cold cloth on it every hour. If your fingers go white, or cold, or blue, you come to me. Not to Mama. To me."

"I'll try to."

The healer kept hold of the knot and looked at her.

"I won't use it," said Elizabeth, "unless somebody's under something."

The healer didn't smile, quite. "Every hour," she said, and got up and went to Stansbury.




Stansbury was on his upturned crate with his hand in a basin. The water had gone faintly pink.

"Out," said the healer.

He took it out.

It looked worse than it had. All of the back of it was red, from the knuckles up over the wrist, and there were blisters coming up along the edges, and at the base of the thumb there was a strip of skin gone a pale, waxy white that Elizabeth didn't like the look of at all.

Stansbury looked at it the way a man looks at a tool he's had for thirty years that somebody has borrowed and brought back broken.

"Move your fingers."

He moved them. Slowly.

"Feel this?"

"Yes."

"And this one?"

"Yes." He touched his thumb to the end of each finger in turn, one, two, three, four, as if he were counting out change. "All present."

"Good," said the healer. "This is going to be a while."

"Then see to the boy first."

"I've seen to the boy."

"Then the rest of them. I'll wait."

"You're next," said the healer, and began, and Stansbury held his hand out to her and kept it perfectly still while she worked, the way he'd hold still for somebody taking his photograph.

Pathwell was watching from twenty feet off.

Someone had moved him off the bare ground after the thing went back into the trees, onto a blanket. His coat was open. The gauze above his ear was new. His hands lay on the blanket on either side of him, palm up, both of them, empty.

The healer had been to him first, because he'd been nearest. Ribs. A blow to the chest. Exhaustion. Whatever it was that a prune does to you, that she had a word for that Elizabeth didn't. Nothing that needed her more than the others did. So she'd left him, and he'd stayed where he was left.

He was waiting. Elizabeth had never seen him wait for anything. He caught her looking at him and looked away first.




The fire stopped being dangerous a long time before it stopped being a fire.

By the middle of the morning the archive was a black shape on its wheels. Charred ribs where the walls had been. Wet ash. Bits of it that still breathed out smoke whenever the wind went the wrong way. People went round it with long iron hooks, dragging apart anything that might still be holding heat.

The archivist sat on a barrel near the things that had been saved, with a flat piece of cardboard across his knees for a desk, because the desk had been inside.

The blue road notebook.

The bundle of letters in the cloth, still tied.

The one gray box Stansbury had brought out by the back door.

Another box, soaked through, that somebody had found under the wagon when the floor came down. It hadn't burned. It had only drowned.

A tin cash box full of little index cards.

Three pages of a ledger, black at the edges, like the edges of toast.

That was all of it, on the ground. It wasn't nothing. It was enough to start a list.

The archivist wrote each thing down on the cardboard, one under the other, with a pencil Milo had found him. When he got to the bottom he sat looking at it, and then at the black wagon, and then at the list again.

"How many?" said Mama Baga, beside him.

"Saved?"

"Start with saved."

He read out the list.

Nobody said it was a good number.

"And lost?" said Mama Baga.

He looked at the wagon a long time. "I don't know."

"Then write that down."

He wrote it. UNKNOWN. On a line on its own.

"Next," said Mama Baga.

"The road copies were in the back cabinet. Most of those are gone. We have the blue notebook, and one copy of the north route, because—" He stopped, and looked down at the blanket.

"I had it in my pocket," said Milo. "You sent me for it. I got it."

"You did," said the archivist.

Milo sat up a little straighter on his blanket.

"The reserve shelf," said the archivist. "Gone. Everything in intake. Gone. Weather books. Gone, mostly. Some of the family letters. The healer's—" He stopped again.

Mama Baga waited.

"Gone," he said.

Across the clearing, the healer, wrapping Stansbury's hand, closed her eyes once. Then she opened them, and went on wrapping.

Elizabeth's good hand went to the inside of her blazer, on the right, the pocket where she'd carried the diary ever since she took it back off him on a bus bench. It went there on its own, the way your tongue goes to the place where a tooth was.

There was nothing there.

Her hand stayed anyway.

She'd given the diary away. It hadn't been hers for a whole day. Her hip didn't know that yet. And the cookbook had been Camp's longer than that, and her thumb still knew exactly where on the cover the flour had worn in, the rough place, the one it had always gone to.

Her hand moved across to the left side, the buttoned side. There was something there. She undid the button, and took it out.

Shade's notebook. Gas-station spiral. A third full. Still warm, from being against her.

She held it on her knee and didn't open it.

Somebody put a bowl down on the ground beside her.

She looked up. It was a young woman, walking a little stiffly, the way you walk when you've been told to go carefully and are only half doing it. It took Elizabeth a moment, without the blankets, to know her. The cot at the back of Mama Baga's wagon. The fingers curled on top of the blanket that didn't move.

They moved now. She'd set the bowl down with them.

"It's chicken," the young woman said. "And dumplings. They're not as good as hers were, I bet."

She went back to the cooking fire before Elizabeth could say anything at all.

Elizabeth looked at the bowl. It was very full. There was a great deal more in it than one person could eat.

More if they're sick, she thought. They won't eat enough.

She ate it all.




After the healer had strapped up Milo's ankle (not broken, she said; badly bruised, maybe sprained, keep off it), Milo took this to mean he should limp about as much as possible, with great drama and a crutch. The archivist gave him a broom handle. It came up to Milo's shoulder.

He hobbled over and stood next to Elizabeth.

"Was that your book? By the door?"

She looked at the black wagon. "Which one?"

"The one on the floor."

"That one was the archive's."

He frowned. "But you wrote it."

"I did."

"So it was yours."

"It can be both."

He thought that over. "That's annoying."

"It is."

"And the other one? On the shelf?"

"My grandmother's. Also the archive's."

"You could have grabbed them," said Milo. He said it without any blame in it at all, the way he'd say it was raining, and that made it both easier and much harder. "On the way out. You were right there."

"I could have."

"But you got me."

"I got you."

He looked down at his bare foot. "My boot burned up," he said, sadly. "It was a really good boot."

"I'm sorry."

"The other one's still fine."

"That's the trouble with pairs."

He thought about it. "I could give it to someone with one leg."

She laughed. It went straight into her shoulder and she did it anyway, and Milo looked very pleased with himself.

Then his face changed. He looked past her, over at the place beyond the pins.

"Shade's dead?"

She looked at the same place. "Yes."

"The blob ate him?"

"Yes."

"Was he a blob too?"

"No."

"Was he magic?"

She looked down at the notebook on her knee. "I don't know. A bit. I don't know."

Milo waited.

"He was Shade," she said.

Milo nodded, slowly, as if she'd told him something with a lot of facts in it.

"He was bad at Hearts," he said.

"Very."

"But he was getting better. He was watching me." Milo gripped his broom handle in both hands. "He still owes me a hand. We didn't finish."

Her throat shut so fast it hurt.

"Does that count?" said Milo. "If he's dead? Does he still owe it?"

Mama Baga had come up behind them. Elizabeth hadn't heard her.

"Yes," said Mama Baga. "It still counts."

Milo turned and looked up at her.

Mama Baga looked at Elizabeth. "His name was Shade."

It wasn't a question, quite. Elizabeth nodded anyway.

"Shade," said Mama Baga, out loud, to the empty ground past the pins. Then she looked over at the card table, which somebody had stood back up on its legs, with a little stack of wet cards squared up neatly in the middle of it.

"Then Shade died here," she said, "this morning. At Camp."

The archivist looked up from his cardboard. He found a clean corner of the sheet, near the bottom, under UNKNOWN, and wrote in it:

SHADE. DIED AT CAMP.

Then, after a moment:

GREEN JACKET (HAMID'S). WENT WITH HIM.

Milo leaned over to see, on his broom handle. "And the queen," he said. "Out of the deck. He had it in his pocket."

"I've got it," said Elizabeth, and took it out, and showed them.

The archivist looked at it for a moment. Then he wrote:

QUEEN OF SPADES. RECOVERED.

"Is that a proper archive thing?" said Milo.

"The archive burned down," said the archivist. "For the moment, I'm deciding what's proper."

Mama Baga read the line twice.

"Leave it," she said.

Elizabeth looked at it too, until the letters went soft. That was the whole funeral. It was all the funeral Camp had time for, that morning. It was a good one.




Pathwell came over after the healer had said he could stand.

He came slowly. His ribs wouldn't let him do it any other way.

He stopped a little way off. He didn't sit.

He didn't say Lizzy.

"Elizabeth."

She looked up at him. There was ash on one knee of his trousers. There was dried blood under the clean gauze.

He looked tired enough that, any other day, she'd have wanted to get him a chair.

It wasn't any other day.

"I'm sorry," he said.

She waited.

He didn't put a because on the end of it.

"For which part?" said Elizabeth.

He looked at her. Then at the black wagon. Then back.

"I came to your party to go through your boxes," he said. "I read your diary. I took your grandmother's book out of its box, and I'd have spent it, if you hadn't taken it off me. I told you your home was marked so that you'd come. I said drive." He stopped. "The museum. Your arm. The working. I said all right to you, and I did it anyway. Twice."

She waited for the rest of it.

It didn't come.

"Shade," she said.

He looked over at the pins, at the trampled ground beyond them, for a long time.

"I lost him," he said.

That was all. He didn't say anything after it.

She looked at his face and couldn't tell what was under it. She knew there was something. She knew the shape of him well enough by now to know when he was carrying something with both hands behind his back.

"That's not all of it," she said.

"No."

"Are you going to tell me?"

He looked at her. "Yes," he said. "Not today. I'm sorry. Not here, today, with him—" His eyes went to the empty ground. "I will. I swear to you I will."

She didn't like it. She let him see that she didn't. But he'd said yes, and he'd said when, more or less, and he hadn't said it was nothing.

"Those things you said," she said. "They're not all one thing. Don't make them into one thing so you only have to be sorry once."

"No."

"I don't know if I forgive you."

"I'm not asking you to."

"Good."

It came out harder than she'd meant. She let it.

He nodded. He stayed where he was.

"What do you need?" he said.

She almost laughed. It sounded so strange coming from him. She looked at Milo, who had gone back to the archivist and was correcting his spelling. She looked at the empty ground. She looked at the notebook on her knee.

"I need you not to make me in charge of whether you get better," she said. "I'm not going to tell you what you're supposed to learn so you can go and learn it at me. I'm not going to say any of this was worth something. I'm not going to make your sorry useful. I'm not going to say that losing him was worth it because now maybe you'll—"

Her voice gave out under her, halfway. She tried again and didn't get any further.

He looked down at the dirt. "All right."

"If you say you know, I'll hit you with Milo's broom."

"I was going to say all right again."

"Good."

He opened his mouth. She could see the joke coming, a small one, to make the next minute easier for both of them. He shut his mouth on it.

"All right," he said.

And nothing came after that. And she looked away first.




Camp dealt with Pathwell after lunch, and not one minute before.

By then the heat had gone out of the black wagon. Milo had been fed twice. Stansbury's hand had been wrapped from the knuckles to halfway up his arm. Elizabeth's shoulder had been iced and bound again and threatened with consequences. The things that had been saved were laid out under a dry awning in three rows: wet, scorched, and fine.

Only then did Mama Baga fetch a chair. It was a kitchen chair with a rush seat, and she carried it out to the middle of the ring herself and set it down in the dirt and sat on it, and folded her hands in her lap, and waited for Pathwell to understand that the chair was not for him.

He stood in front of her. Stansbury sat off to one side with his wrapped hand propped up on a folded blanket. The archivist stood with his cardboard. The healer was there, too, because three of the people involved were hurt, and she trusted none of them.

Elizabeth sat at the edge, on a log. She had the notebook on her knee.

"What do you owe?" said Mama Baga.

Pathwell looked at the black wagon. "Everything that was in it."

"No."

He looked back at her.

"Can he give us back everything that was in it?" She nodded at the archivist.

"No."

"Can you?"

"No."

"Then don't stand there saying a great big number, as if the size of the number is the same as being sorry."

He didn't say anything.

"Again," said Mama Baga. "What do you owe?"

He took longer. Elizabeth watched him take longer.

"Work," he said. "Making good what can be made good. The things you need. Paper. Boxes. Shelves. Hands."

"And who says what's good?"

He opened his mouth. He stopped.

"Not me," said Pathwell.

Mama Baga sat back in the chair. "Better."

The archivist read off his cardboard. "Blank paper. Boxes. Waxed cloth. Two cabinets. Drying racks. Copying things, ink and pencils and such. The route copies made again, from the blue notebook and anything else we can find."

Pathwell nodded.

"You don't touch any original without asking."

"Yes."

"You don't prune for any of it."

Pathwell's eyes came up.

Mama Baga said nothing at all. The archivist waited.

"No," said Pathwell. "I won't."

"You don't decide that something's close enough to what burned."

Pathwell looked at the blue road notebook on its blanket. "No."

"You don't tidy anything without asking first."

From Stansbury's crate there came a small sound. It might have been pain. It might have been joy.

"You're enjoying this," said Pathwell.

"I've been waiting two hundred years for this afternoon," said Stansbury. "I'm going to have it."

"You are hurt because of him too," said Mama Baga, turning her head. "Don't sit there being decoration."

Stansbury's pleasure went out like a light. "Yes, Mama."

"The museum's separate," said the archivist. "Camp doesn't take that on."

"I know," said Pathwell.

Mama Baga turned to Elizabeth. "Anything you want said?"

Elizabeth looked down at the notebook on her knee. She thought about it properly.

"My arm isn't Camp's to pay for," she said.

"It's mine," said Pathwell. "I'll—"

"That isn't what I said."

He stopped.

"I don't want anything decided about it today," said Elizabeth. "Not today."

"Then it isn't," said Mama Baga.

Pathwell opened his mouth. And shut it.

"Whoever breaks the thing," said Mama Baga, looking at him, "does not get to choose when it's mended. Not because the waiting makes him itch."

"Understood."

She stood up out of the chair. She wasn't finished. "And the next time someone you love says stop, you stop. And if your hands can't keep still, find them something to do that isn't other people."

"Cards," said Stansbury.

From the far side of the ring, Milo shouted, "He'll be rubbish at it!"

For a second nobody laughed. Then the woman in the knitted cap did, very quietly, and then a few people near her, and then a few more.

Elizabeth didn't. She found she didn't mind that other people had.




By the evening, Stansbury's hand had stopped looking like an injury and started looking like something that would always be there.

The healer changed the dressing under a lantern by Mama Baga's wagon. Pathwell sat on the step below his brother holding the clean bandages, rolled, because that was the job he'd been given, and he was doing it very well.

"It moves well," said the healer, bending the fingers back and forward, gently.

"Good," said Stansbury.

"It'll scar."

He looked at it. "Probably."

"Not probably. It will. Across the back. Up the wrist, likely. It'll pull tight as it heals, and you'll have to work at it every day so it doesn't."

Pathwell sat up on the step. Elizabeth saw it from across the ring, where she was sitting by the fire.

So did Stansbury.

"No," said Stansbury.

"I didn't say a word."

"You breathed in like a man about to sell me something."

The healer looked from one to the other.

Pathwell held the roll of bandage very still in his hands. "There are people," he said, carefully, "who can do a great deal about a scar. After."

"Ordinary people?" said Stansbury.

Pathwell didn't answer quickly enough. "No."

"Would it take someone's letter? Someone's book?"

"It might. It might not."

"Would it take some of you?"

"No," said Pathwell. "I promised."

Stansbury looked down at his own hand. He turned it over, and back. Then he looked at the healer.

"Will it work? The hand. If I do what you tell me."

"If you do what I tell you, yes. Well."

"Then just do the hand."

He looked at Pathwell.

"Let it be," he said.

Pathwell's fingers tightened once on the roll of bandage. Then they let go.

"All right."

The healer held out her hand, and he gave her the bandage.




The dark made the empty place bigger.

All day there'd been someone in front of the black wagon, carrying something past it, counting something, wetting the ash. Now it was night, and there was nobody, and where the archive had stood there was just a long patch of darker ground, and the smell of wet charcoal, and the wheels.

Elizabeth sat by it, a little way off, on a log, with a blanket round her shoulders and her arm back in its new sling. Every now and then her good hand went to her empty pocket. She let it.

Milo had gone to sleep in someone's wagon with his foot up on a rolled coat and his one good boot on the floor beside the bed, for reasons nobody had questioned.

Stansbury was asleep, or pretending, with his wrapped hand on his chest.

Pathwell was moving the wet boxes. The archivist had said they had to go from the tarp by the wagon to the tarp by the fire, to dry, and Pathwell was moving them, one at a time, slowly, round his ribs, and putting each one down on exactly the spot the archivist pointed to. Once he reached out to turn one so that its label showed. He looked at it. He put his hand back in his pocket.

Mama Baga came and sat down beside her.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

Somewhere behind the wagons the fiddle started. It wasn't a tune Elizabeth knew. It wasn't a sad one, as far as she could tell. It was just someone playing softly, because it had been a very loud day.

"He liked terrible coffee," said Elizabeth.

Mama Baga looked at the fire. "Shade?"

"Yes."

"He made terrible coffee."

"He made it on purpose."

"I tasted it," said Mama Baga. "This morning." She was quiet a moment. "I thought about asking him to leave because of it."

Elizabeth laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, and it broke in the middle, and then she was crying, and her good hand went up over her mouth.

Mama Baga didn't touch her. She stayed exactly where she was, beside her.

Elizabeth cried for Nana. She hadn't, properly, not once, not at the funeral, not in all the packing, not in three weeks of boxes. She did now.

She cried for a man in a green jacket with too many pockets, who'd been alive about three weeks, and in that time had found out he didn't like pears, and that a dog would come to him if he asked it, and that he was right-handed, and that people at a card table could be read like a book, and who'd still found time to end up owing a boy a hand of Hearts.

When it slowed down, Mama Baga held out a clean handkerchief. Elizabeth took it and wiped her face.

She looked down at the notebook, on her knee, under her hand.

"He asked me to keep this," she said. "Before. So it wouldn't be Pathwell's. He said it was the only thing he had that was his."

Mama Baga looked at it.

"I don't know if I'm allowed to read it," said Elizabeth. "There's nobody to ask."

"There's never anybody to ask," said Mama Baga, "in the end. Then you ask what they'd have said."

Elizabeth thought about that.

He'd shown her the first page himself, at the diner, turned it round on the table for her so she could read it, as if that were just the polite thing to do. He'd shown her the page about breakfast, to show her it was paid. He'd never once shut it when she was looking.

She opened it to the last page he'd written on.

Sat. Camp. Carried: 11 buckets, 2 sacks onions, 1 baby (short), 1 ladder.
Breakfast. Eggs over easy. E. made them. Paid.
Coffee: worse than his. Mine.
Hearts: lost 5. Milo set a trap for 7 tricks. Want to learn that.
Mama B. asked did I want to go. Said stay.
Milo asked was I a person. Said yes. Then he let me sit down.

She looked at that last line for a long time.

Then she turned back. Just one page. To the museum.

Sat. early. Museum. A wall of names. Everybody who wanted somebody to know they'd been there.
Put mine in here instead of on the wall. Didn't want to get anybody in trouble.
SHADE. I WAS HERE.

She closed it, and put her hand flat on the cover.

"Was he a person?" she said. She didn't know who she was asking.

Mama Baga looked at the dark place where the archive had been.

"He said no," she said, "when somebody tried to decide what he was for him. He asked to stay, and he stayed. He played cards, badly. He made coffee nobody could drink. He carried things nobody asked him to carry." She looked at Elizabeth. "Then he died."

She was quiet a moment.

"Person is the word I have for that."

Elizabeth nodded.

Across the ring, Pathwell set down a box where the archivist pointed, and went back for another one. He didn't look over at her to see whether she'd seen.

Tomorrow there'd be more lists. The museum's, and Camp's, and the healer's. People deciding which pieces of the damage could be mended and which ones could only be written down.

She sat by the empty place with Shade's notebook under her hand until the fiddle stopped.

Then she went to sleep, because tomorrow was going to want things from all of them.
