Chapter Thirteen

By the time the sun was all the way up, Shade had carried eleven buckets of water, two sacks of onions, a baby (briefly, while its mother tied her shoe), and a ladder, and had written each of them down.

Mama Baga stopped in front of him on her way somewhere and took hold of his sleeve between two fingers and turned it to the light.

There was museum glass in the wool. Little bright bits of it.

"Off," she said.

He looked down at the coat. "I was going to."

"You were going to for an hour."

"I've been carrying things."

"You can carry things in a different coat." She nodded toward a line strung between two wagons, where shirts and trousers and a red shawl and three coats were hanging in the sun. "Take one that fits. Put that one in the basket."

Shade stood up from his log. His right hand came open, toward the trees, and he closed it against his leg before anybody could say anything about it, and took the coat off.

Without it he looked less like Pathwell. Elizabeth hadn't realized how much of the likeness had been the coat. Without it, he was only a man with a face she knew.

He held the coat a moment. One cuff was torn. There was a dark mark on the collar from the brass rail. A button hung by two threads. He put it in the basket.

"I can mend that," said Mama Baga, "if you leave a note."

"No," said Shade. "Thank you."

He stood in front of the washing line for a long time, with his hands at his sides, like a man in front of a menu in a language he only half reads. Then he took down a green canvas jacket, patched at both elbows, with pockets on the chest and pockets at the hips and a pocket on the sleeve and two more inside, and put it on. The sleeves stopped short of his wrists.

"That's Hamid's," said Mama Baga. "From before he got fat. Hamid has short arms and a great many opinions about where to keep things."

Shade put his hands in the hip pockets. He took them out. He put one in the chest pocket. He took it out and looked at the sleeve pocket for some time.

"I like it," he said, as if he'd been surprised by it.

Mama Baga went on with wherever she'd been going.

Shade took the notebook out of his old coat in the basket, and the pen, and stood a moment deciding, and then put them in the left inside pocket of the green jacket, the one over his heart, the one his hand didn't pull toward. He buttoned the pocket shut.




Elizabeth had a debt to pay.

It took some arranging. She had one arm, and a healer who had looked at her in a particular way, and a great many people at Camp who had decided, collectively and without discussing it, that she was not to lift anything. But the cooking wagon had eggs in a basket, and a black pan, and a fire under it, and Milo, who for the price of being allowed to crack the eggs himself would do almost anything.

"You hit it on the edge," said Elizabeth. "Not too hard."

He hit it too hard.

"That's fine. Fish the shell out. That's what fingers are for."

The second one he did right. The third one he did beautifully, one-handed, showing off, and looked at her to see if she'd noticed, and she had.

She cooked them over easy, the way she'd had them at the diner. It was strange doing it with one hand. Strange and not hard. Her body knew the pan. Her body had made eggs at five in the morning in Nana's kitchen for four years, with the radio on low and the stairs waiting, and it knew exactly when to tip the pan and when to leave it alone.

She put them on a plate with two pieces of toast from the fire, and a little salt, and then she picked up the plate in her good hand.

The healer, passing with a basin, looked at the plate, and at Elizabeth, and opened her mouth.

"It's a plate," said Elizabeth. "It doesn't count."

The healer closed her mouth, and looked at her a moment longer, and went on.

Shade was on his log in his green jacket. Elizabeth walked across the ring to him with the plate held out in front of her, and it was such an old, ordinary walk, carrying a plate to somebody, that halfway there she nearly had to stop.

She didn't stop.

She held it out to him.

He looked at the plate. Then at her.

"Breakfast," she said. "I owed you."

He took the plate in both hands, the right one too, though it shook. He set it on his knees. He looked at it for a long time, at the eggs and the toast, with an expression she'd never seen on him, or on anyone, exactly. Like someone being handed a letter in their own name for the first time.

Then he ate it. All of it. Slowly, with a fork somebody had given him, in his left hand.

When he'd finished, he wiped his fingers carefully on his trousers and took the notebook out of the inside pocket and unbuttoned it and opened it to the page from the diner.

Elizabeth owes me breakfast.

He drew one straight line through it with the pen. Then he wrote, next to it, small:

Paid.

He showed it to her.

"Paid," she said.

He nodded, and closed the notebook, and buttoned it back into the pocket over his heart, and sat with his hand on the outside of the pocket for a moment, as if to make sure.




The next thing that happened was coffee.

Milo came round the end of the cooking wagon with a wooden coffee grinder nearly as big as his own head and put it in Shade's lap.

"Mama says if you're going to keep looking at the pot like that, you make it."

Shade looked down at the grinder. "I looked at it once."

"She saw."

Milo started away.

"Beans?" said Shade.

"Blue tin. Not the red tin."

"What's in the red tin?"

"Mama's money." Milo looked at him a moment longer. "You look like Pathwell."

Shade's face shut.

"But you're nicer to the buckets," said Milo, and went.

Elizabeth put her good hand over her mouth.

Shade made the coffee. He did it the way he'd done everything, as if the knowing were in his hands and he wasn't sure it was his to use. He'd start a thing, grinding, pouring, and then stop, and look at what his hands were doing, and only then go on. He knew how fine to grind. He knew to wet the grounds first and wait. He knew to set the pot at the edge of the coals and not in them. Each time he knew a thing, she watched him not trust it.

What came out of the pot was black. Not dark. Black, the way a well is black.

He poured some into a tin cup and drank it, and his face did something complicated.

"Good," he said.

"That was not a good face."

"It's my face. It's still learning." He drank more. "I like it."

"Let me try."

"You've had a hard few days."

"Cup."

He gave it to her.

It tasted like someone had boiled a fence post, and then boiled the water from that, and then left it overnight in a hot car.

She handed it back.

"That's terrible," she said.

"I know," said Shade.

She stopped with her hand still out.

He looked at her. "What?"

"Nothing."

"It was something."

"Pathwell said that. Just like that. At the bookstore, when he gave me the coffee there. 'That's terrible.' 'I know.'"

Shade went still. "When?"

"Thursday night. Friday morning. After you were already—" She stopped. "After you started."

"Then I don't have it." He looked into the cup. "He was right about one thing, then."

"Which?"

"Knowing when it's terrible." He drank. "Mine's worse than his."

"How would you know?"

"I don't. I've decided."

"Then it's yours," said Elizabeth.

He thought about that. Then he nodded, and finished the cup, and poured another.




Hearts came after the coffee, because Milo said so.

"We've got three. We need four."

"Four what?" said Shade.

"People." Milo sighed, enormously, the sigh of the young for the old. "For Hearts. Are you a person or not?"

Shade looked at him for a moment.

"Yes," he said. "I think so."

"Then sit."

The table was a door laid across two barrels at the edge of the fire. A woman in a knitted cap was already there, shuffling, very fast, the cards making a sound like rain. The archivist was across from her with a pencil behind his ear and a look of a man who had work to do and had been told he didn't. There was an empty stool.

Elizabeth sat on a crate nearby with her sling on her knee, where she could watch.

"I know the rules," said Shade, picking up his cards. Then, "I don't remember ever playing."

"Then you're playing now," said the archivist, and led.

Shade was terrible at Hearts. Not a little bad. Wonderfully bad. He knew all the rules, every one, and he played as though the rules were the game, and every time he made a mistake he saw exactly what it had been about two cards too late, and said "Ah," very quietly, to himself. The woman in the knitted cap won the first hand without ever appearing to look at her cards.

The second hand he did better.

The third hand, Milo, who had been quietly setting something up since the first trick, sprang it, and the queen of spades landed in front of Shade like a dropped plate.

Shade stared at it. "He did that on purpose."

"Yes," said the archivist.

"For six tricks."

"Seven."

Shade looked at Milo. Milo had his hands behind his head and was gazing at the sky.

"I'd like to know how," said Shade.

"Lose some more," said Milo, "and I'll show you."

Elizabeth laughed. It went into her shoulder, and she let it.

By the fifth hand, Shade had stopped playing the rules and started playing the table. She watched it happen. He learned that the archivist touched the pencil behind his ear whenever he had a heart he didn't want. That the woman in the knitted cap went very calm and pleasant when she was about to do something terrible. That Milo talked when he was bluffing, which meant Milo talked all the time, which meant you had to listen to what he talked about.

Shade lost the fifth hand by less.

He took out his notebook and wrote something, and put it away.

"Again?" said the archivist.

Shade looked up, surprised to be asked. "Yes."




Somewhere in the sixth hand, the hand went.

He'd had it under control all morning. He'd sat on it and held it and buttoned it into pockets. But it came open now on the table, all on its own, and turned, and pointed past the wagons at the trees, at nothing, at somewhere, and the cards in it went everywhere.

Shade grabbed it with his left and held it down flat on the table.

"Stronger," he said, to Elizabeth, before she could ask. "Closer."

"Him?"

"Him. Or near him."

Down at the far end of the ring, by the path to the stream, someone shouted.

A girl came running up the path. She was eight or nine, and Elizabeth had seen her at breakfast, carrying two buckets and spilling most of both. She didn't have a bucket now. She was holding her right hand out away from her body, as if it belonged to somebody else, and from the fingertips to the wrist it had gone gray, the gray of dishwater, and the smell came up the path in front of her.

Low tide.

"It was in the stream," the girl said, to everyone and no one. "Not in it. Under it. Under the water. It went right by me."

The healer was there before she'd finished, and then Mama Baga. The healer took the girl's hand in both of hers and didn't flinch at it.

"Can you feel that?"

"No."

"That?"

"A little bit."

"Good," said the healer. She said it as if it were the best news she'd had all week, and put an arm round the girl and walked her to the wagon, not fast, because fast would frighten her.

Nobody at the card table looked at Shade.

That was the worst part. Elizabeth saw him notice it. Every single person there not looking at him, carefully, kindly.

He looked down at his hand, flat under his other hand on the table, still trying to point.

"It wasn't after her," he said, very low. "She was in its way. That's all she was."

Mama Baga came back down the steps of the healer's wagon. She didn't come to the table. She stopped about twenty feet off, in the middle of the ring, where everyone could see her, and folded her hands.

"Do you want to go?" she said to Shade.

He looked up at her, surprised. "Would that be better?"

"I didn't ask you what would be better. I asked you what you want."

Shade looked at the trees.

He looked at the card table, at Milo, at the woman in the knitted cap, who was gathering the dropped cards very slowly so as not to seem to be listening. He looked at the three buckets by his log, and the green sleeve on his own arm.

"I want to stay," he said.

"Then stay."

That was all. She turned round and went back toward the healer's wagon.

Shade sat there.

"People here keep doing that," he said to Elizabeth.

"What?"

"Asking me."

"It's a bad habit," said Elizabeth. "You'll get used to it."

He looked at his hand, under his hand. Then he picked up his cards again, one at a time, from where the woman in the knitted cap had stacked them neatly in front of him.




An hour later the girl from the stream was sitting on the healer's steps opening and shutting her fingers, and the gray had gone out of them everywhere except under the nails. Her name was Posy. Somebody had gone down to the stream for her buckets. Nobody had wanted to go alone.

Milo insisted on one more hand. The archivist said he had work. The woman in the knitted cap dealt.

Shade won the first four tricks.

Milo narrowed his eyes. "You've got the queen."

Shade said nothing.

"You hold your cards closer when you've got it."

"I do not."

"You just did it more."

The archivist looked absolutely delighted.

Shade looked at Elizabeth over his cards. "This is a hostile place."

"You're still sitting in it."

He looked back at his hand. Something in his face eased.

"Yes," he said.

His right hand snapped open.

It went so hard this time that his whole arm went with it, and his left couldn't hold it, and the cards went flying off the table into the dirt. His arm stood straight out toward the far side of the ring, toward a gap between two wagons, toward nothing at all.

And in the nothing, between two trees at the edge of the clearing, a line of gray light appeared, standing up straight, like a door seen edge-on.

Milo looked down at the cards in the dirt. "Do those count as played?"

"They do not," said the woman in the knitted cap.

Shade wasn't listening. He was staring at the line of light. His hand was open toward it, and he closed it, and it opened again at once and stayed open, though she could see the cords standing out in his wrist with the effort of holding it shut.

The line got wider.

Through it, very faintly, from a long way off, Elizabeth heard a broom on a wooden floor.

Shade looked down at his cards in the dirt. The queen of spades was lying face up between his boots, next to an onion that had got out of a sack somewhere and come looking for company.

He bent and picked up the queen, and looked at it, and put it in one of the green jacket's many pockets.

"I'd like to finish that game," he said.

Then the door opened.
