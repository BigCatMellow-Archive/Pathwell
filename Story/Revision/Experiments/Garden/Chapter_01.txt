Chapter One

It was the crash that woke her.

She lay there a moment trying to decide if it had been a real crash or the kind you dream right before you fall off something. Then came a second, smaller one, the sound of somebody being careful about the first.

There was a man in her living room. He was sitting on the rug in the middle of a fallen stack of boxes, with her winter sweaters all around him like he'd been dropped into a nest, and he had one of her notebooks open on his knee.

Nobody had cleaned up after the party. Every flat surface had a plastic cup on it, and some of the cups had other cups in them. The WELCOME ELIZABETH banner over the kitchen doorway had given up at one end. On the counter sat what was left of the sheet cake. Somebody had cut into it from the far side, so it said ELIZ now, and everybody after had politely kept going the same way.

She sort of knew him. He'd spent most of the party standing by her boxes with a plate of cake he never seemed to get any further into, and when she'd told her landlord story he was the only one who'd laughed. She'd assumed somebody from accounting had brought him.

Somebody from accounting had apparently left without him.

"Hi," she said. "Do you need me to call you a cab?"

He turned a page. "No, thank you."

"Some water?"

He thought about it. "Water would be nice."

She was halfway to the kitchen when she stopped, because the notebook on his knee was her diary. He was a third of the way through it, and he didn't even have the decency to look caught.

She came back without the water.

"That's my diary."

"It is," he agreed, and kept reading.

Something hit the front door. Not a knock. It was wet, and it was heavy, like someone had thrown a bag of laundry at it from across the hall.

He looked up at the door, and then at her.

"Expecting someone?"

"It's four in the morning."

"So, no."

She went and looked through the peephole. It was dark out there, which it never was, the hall light stayed on all night. She turned the lock and opened the door against the chain to see why.

The smell came in first, low tide on a hot day. Then something dark and wet sagged into the gap and pressed there, feeling along the edge of the door like it was looking for the rest of the opening.

He was across the room before she'd finished stepping back. He put his shoulder into the door and it slammed, and the chain jumped, and he shot the bolt.

The door bulged once, like something breathing in, and went flat again.

He was looking at it the way she'd seen people look at a smoke alarm going off in a building where they didn't live. It wasn't fear. It was more like he'd been planning on a quiet night.

Then he was back down among the boxes. "Where's the cookbook?"

"What?"

"The old one. Handwritten." He shoved the sweaters aside and pulled the tape off a box marked BATHROOM, looked in and closed it again, as if it had let him down. "Somebody's been writing in it a long time. Where is it?"

"Kitchen," she said. She heard herself say it. "The box that says KITCHEN. The other one."

The door took another hit, harder. Plaster dust came sifting down off the frame.

He was in the kitchen before it landed. Tape ripped, and he came back with it in his hands.

Nana's cookbook. It was fat with things that weren't pages: recipe cards, a church bulletin, a folded electric bill with a recipe on the back. Three rubber bands held it shut, and a blue marker rode under the top one, the same marker that had written CASSEROLES & SUCH across the cover in Nana's tall, leaning capitals. It had been dry for years. Nana had kept it there anyway.

He snapped the rubber bands off one after another, and the marker went bouncing away under the couch.

"Favorite recipe," he said. "Don't think."

"I don't—"

"Don't think. Favorite."

The door groaned.

"Sugar cookies?" It came out as a question. "My Nana's sugar cookies?"

"Good. That's good."

He flipped through without being gentle about it, past a pie, past something with Jell-O in it, and stopped. NANA JONES SUGAR COOKIES. Under that, smaller: *2 sticks butter, NOT margarine, I don't care what the box says.*

He laid his hand flat on the page.

"You might want to get behind something."

Gold came up out of the ink. It ran along Nana's letters one stroke at a time, the way she'd written them, and stopped where she'd stopped and went on where she'd gone on. It found the place where she'd crossed out a teaspoon and written a tablespoon, and lit both.

The door started to split. Something dark pushed in through the cracks in the wood and spread out, feeling for the room.

Light pooled under his hand.

"What are you doing?" She didn't remember crossing the room. She had the cookbook by the spine. "That's my grandmother's."

She pulled, and he didn't let go fast enough, and the sugar-cookie page tore loose and stayed under his palm.

Everything went white, and smelled like butter.

She was small enough to sit on the counter. Nana had let her, which her mother never did, and she was sitting there with her heels knocking the cabinet while Nana creamed the butter and sugar by hand, because the mixer was for people in a hurry. The window was open. It had just rained. Nana held out the wooden spoon for her to lick and said, "Don't tell your mother," and Elizabeth never had.

Then her back hit the wall, and she was in her own living room with the cookbook in her arms and her heart going like she'd run up stairs.

The light in his hand had gone wrong. It sputtered like a bad bulb, and loose threads of gold were dripping off the edge of the page and onto the rug.

The door gave all at once. The thing came through it, bringing some of the frame along, and now she could see the whole of it, big and dark and wet and taller than he was, with no front to it that she could find. It slid across the threads of light on the rug, and they went out underneath it like matches.

Then it went for his hand.

He brought the glowing page up to meet it, and when they hit, the sound was one clear note, like a wet finger run around the rim of a glass.

The thing shuddered back, and the smell came off it in a wave. Elizabeth's stomach made its own decision. She did what he'd said and got behind something, which turned out to be the kitchen counter, and threw up in her own sink.

She stayed down there on the linoleum with the cookbook against her chest.

From the living room came thumping, and something tearing, and him saying "No. No. Absolutely not," in the voice of a man refusing a second helping. Something heavy hit the wall hard enough to knock the clock off it.

Then it was very quiet.

She held her breath. The quiet went on. Under it, so low she felt it in the floor before she heard it, something began to hum, and the hum grew until the cabinet doors rattled against her back.

Light came around the end of the counter, white and bright. Then a pop, like a cork.

"All clear," he called. "Well. Clear-ish."

She came up over the counter an inch at a time.

Her living room was covered in it. Dark, wobbling, already starting to smell worse. It was on the couch and up the walls and inside the boxes, and the banner had dropped into it face down, all but the first word, so that the room just said WELCOME.

He was standing in the middle of all of it with her diary under one arm and a plate of cake in his other hand.

"Somebody left it on a box," he said, when he saw her looking. "It made it."

She came around into the living room. She picked up a cup off the floor, which was the wrong thing to do, and did it anyway.

"I wouldn't," he said.

"It's my apartment."

"It's mostly blob." He ate a bite of the cake. "That's what that was, a blob. They clean up. That's all they do."

"Clean up what?"

He opened his mouth to answer that, then stopped, and looked at her properly.

"You really don't know?"

"Know what?"

"Huh." He looked at her, and at the broken door, and back at her, like a sum that hadn't come out right.

He set the plate down on a box, the only clean surface left, went to the doorway, stepped around the worst of it, and leaned out and checked up and down the hall. When he came back, whatever he'd been working out had been decided.

"Right. Here's the thing. Your apartment's been marked." He nodded at the stuff dripping off the door frame. "Magically speaking. So we should probably be somewhere else."

He said it the way people say a restaurant is closed when they haven't actually checked.

"I'm not leaving my apartment with you. I don't even know your name."

"Very reasonable. Where are your shoes?"

"By the door, but—"

"Good. Shoes."

She was still holding the cookbook. She looked down at it, at the torn edge where the sugar cookies had been, and her thumb found it on its own.

"I heard her," she said. "Just now. My Nana."

He was quiet for a second, which she'd later learn was rare.

"She wrote it," he said. "That's all. She wrote it."

"That doesn't mean anything."

"It means a lot, actually. Shoes."

"Look at my apartment." Her voice was going up and she couldn't stop it. "Look at it. My landlord is going to kill me. I've been here three weeks. How do you even clean this? What do you clean it with?"

"Fire, usually," he said. "Shoes, please."

From somewhere down the hall came another wet thud.

They both looked at the doorway.

"I did say please," he said.

She got her shoes on without untying them and pulled her coat on over her pajamas. When he held out his hand for the cookbook, she gave it to him before she'd thought about whether she should. He tucked it under his arm with the diary.

On her way past the counter, she picked up the cake.

She didn't decide to. Her hands just did it. It was in its box with the lid half on, and it said ELIZ, and she wasn't going to leave it there to get blob on it.

He looked at the cake, and then at her, with something that might have been respect.

"Ready?"

"No."

"Perfect."
