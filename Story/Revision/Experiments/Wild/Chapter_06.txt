Six: Hot Chocolate from a Machine

The bowling alley was called the Starlite, and the sign on the roof had a star on it that had once lit up and now just leaned.

Inside it was the same as every bowling alley Elizabeth had ever been in, which was two: the carpet with the planets on it, the long shining lanes, the crash and rumble of somebody's ball going down and somebody's pins going over, the smell of floor wax and fried things and feet. It was eleven in the morning and there were three lanes going. A league of very old men in matching shirts. A birthday party of very small children with the bumpers up. And one woman on her own on lane nine, bowling in silence, with enormous concentration, as if she were trying to get something out of her system.

There was a bar along the back, under a row of tellies all showing different sports with the sound off.

Stansbury went straight to it.

"I'm going to bowl," said Pathwell.

"You can't lift your arm."

"I'll bowl with the other one."

"You've never bowled with the other one."

"Then it'll be very exciting," said Pathwell, and went to get shoes.

Elizabeth stood between the bar and the shoe counter and didn't know which way to go.

The man behind the bar looked at her. "What can I get you?"

She looked at the bottles behind him and found she had no idea. She couldn't remember the last time somebody had asked her what she wanted and meant only her. For two years it had been what does she want, and could you, and is she, and would she. Before that, she couldn't remember.

"She'll have—" said Pathwell, from the shoe counter.

"No," said Elizabeth.

He stopped, with one bowling shoe in each hand.

"I hadn't said anything."

"I know." She turned back to the man behind the bar. "Do you have hot chocolate?"

He looked at her as though nobody had ever in the history of the Starlite asked for hot chocolate.

"Out of a machine," he said.

"That's fine," said Elizabeth. "That's perfect. Thank you. Sorry. No, not sorry. Thank you."

It came out of a machine. It came in a paper cup with a plastic lid, and it was too hot and too sweet and tasted of powder, and it was the best thing she'd had in a week.

Stansbury, on the stool next to her, looked at her cup, and at her, and down at his own glass, and the corner of his mouth went in.

"Beer," he said to the barman. "And whatever he's having, put it on his account."

"I don't have an account," called Pathwell.

"Open one," said Stansbury.




Pathwell bowled with his left hand.

He was magnificent and terrible. He held the ball up in front of his face and sighted down it like a man aiming a cannon, and took four long, stately steps, and let go, with a flourish, and the ball went exactly where he'd aimed it, slowly, and then, about two-thirds of the way down, changed its mind and went into the gutter.

He watched it go all the way to the end.

"The lane is crooked," he said.

"The lane is not crooked," said Stansbury, without turning round on his stool.

"You haven't looked."

"I don't need to look. I've watched you bowl."

Pathwell bowled again. Gutter. Again. The ball hit one pin, at the very edge, and the pin wobbled, and thought about it, and stayed up.

"You try," he said to Elizabeth.

"I don't know how."

"Nobody knows how. You just throw it at them and apologize."

She didn't have bowling shoes. The man at the shoe counter, who had a great deal of gray hair in a ponytail and a name badge that said RON, looked at her boots, and said "Size?" and she told him, and he looked at her a moment and then reached under the counter and brought out a pair that were nearly new. Red and blue. "These ones are nice," he said. "Nobody's been sick in them."

She put them on. She picked up a ball, a light one, a pink one, and stood at the line and looked down at the pins, far away, white, standing there in their triangle like people waiting for a bus.

She didn't sight down it like a cannon. She didn't do four stately steps. She just walked up and let it go, the way you'd let go of something you'd been holding too long.

It went straight. It went down the middle of the lane, quite slowly, not doing anything clever, and it went into the pins right in the middle of the triangle, and every single one of them fell over.

All ten. With a crash.

"Sorry," said Elizabeth, to the pins.

There was a silence along the back of the Starlite.

Then the very old men on lane two, in their matching shirts, began to clap.

Stansbury had turned round on his stool. He was looking at her as though she'd done a magic trick.

Pathwell was looking at the pins. Then at her. Then at his own left hand.

"Beginner's luck," he said.

"I apologized to them," said Elizabeth. "I apologized to the pins."

"I heard."

"Why would I—"

"You knocked them down," said Pathwell. "You felt bad." He picked up his own ball. "It's very sweet. It's going to get you killed one day."




She bowled four more strikes and two gutters, and stopped apologizing to the pins somewhere around the third frame, and nobody said anything about it, and she noticed.

Pathwell bowled with his left hand all morning and never knocked over more than three. Stansbury didn't bowl at all. He sat on his stool with his beer and watched them both and every so often said "Lift your feet," or "Your elbow," in the voice of a man who had once taught somebody to swim and was still not happy with how it went.

At some point Pathwell stopped, in the middle of a frame, with the ball in his hand.

He stood very still at the line. His head tipped one way, then the other, like a dog hearing its name from two rooms off. Then he put the ball back on the rack, carefully, and said, "Back in a minute," and went off toward the toilets, past the shoe counter, round the corner where there was a row of lockers and a long mirror on the wall.

Elizabeth bowled a seven. Then she took her shoes off, because her feet hurt, and went to give them back to Ron.

Round the corner, by the lockers, Pathwell was standing in front of the long mirror.

He hadn't heard her. He was standing with his feet apart, quite still, and he was turning, very slowly, the top half of him. Left. Then right. Then left again. Like a man trying to find where a draft is coming from. He was frowning.

She looked at the mirror.

In the glass, the other him frowned back. Head left. Head right. It kept up perfectly.

Then he stopped turning, and stood still.

And his reflection's mouth moved.

His didn't. His real mouth was shut, pressed tight, the way it was when he was thinking. But in the mirror, his face opened its mouth and said a word. One word. No sound came out. She saw it, though. She saw the shape of it, clear as anything, the way you'd see someone say something to you through a window.

You.

Then it was only his face again, frowning, with its mouth shut.

She must have made a sound. He turned round.

He gave her the look he'd given her front door at two in the morning, while the door was bulging.

"Did you see that?" he said.

She could have said no.

"It said something," she said. "In the mirror. Your mouth. It said you."

He stared at the mirror a while longer. There was nothing in it but a tired man in a long coat, a row of lockers, and a poster saying that bowling shoes remained the property of the Starlite at all times.

"Huh," he said, very quietly.

He went past her, back round the corner. The corridor was so narrow he had to turn sideways, and he did, so that he didn't touch her.

Ron, at the shoe counter, held out his hand for the bowling shoes. She gave them to him.

"He all right?" said Ron.

"I don't know," said Elizabeth.

"That mirror does that," said Ron, putting the shoes on the shelf. "Some days. I keep meaning to have it seen to."




Pathwell was back at the bar when she came round. He was sitting on the stool next to his brother. He had a glass of something in front of him that he wasn't drinking, and his right hand was in his coat pocket.

Stansbury was looking at him. "What?"

Pathwell looked at the tellies. "Later."

Stansbury looked at Elizabeth. She didn't know what her face was doing. Whatever it was, Stansbury read it, and his own face went still.

"This morning," said Elizabeth, sitting down on the stool on Pathwell's other side. "Your brother said he didn't like the first part. The door."

"He did."

"Do you like it any better now?"

Pathwell turned the glass round on the bar.

She watched him reach for a joke, the way you reach for a light switch in a room you know in the dark. His hand found the wall where it ought to be.

It wasn't there.

"It points somewhere I don't like," he said.

"Where?"

He looked at his hand, in his pocket, through the cloth of his coat.

"Me," he said.

Stansbury put his beer down on the bar so carefully it didn't make a sound.

Behind them, on lane two, one of the very old men bowled a strike, and the others cheered.

"What does that mean?" said Elizabeth.

"I don't know yet."

"Tell me," said Stansbury, low.

Pathwell looked at Elizabeth. Then at his brother. "Not here."

He hadn't said not in front of her. He'd said not here. She held on to the difference.




Stansbury paid. A beer, a glass of something nobody drank, one hot chocolate from a machine, and six frames of bowling, five of which had been tragic.

Outside, the day had gone gray and blowy. The van was a small green one with STARLING COUNTY LIBRARIES on the side in white letters and a picture of an owl reading a book.

Stansbury took his keys out of his pocket. He looked at them. He looked at his brother, who had stopped at the edge of the car park and was looking past the van, at the scrubby bit of grass and bushes between the car park and the road.

Then Stansbury held the keys out to Elizabeth.

"Why me?"

"Because I want to sit next to him."

He said it straight, without dressing it up, the way he'd said everything. He didn't look at Pathwell while he said it.

"Can you drive?"

"Yes."

She took the keys. They were warm from his pocket, and there was a little rubber owl on the keyring.

"For the record," said Pathwell, without turning round, "I'm an excellent driver."

"You failed your test four times," said Stansbury.

"The examiner had it in for me."

"The examiner was in tears."

Elizabeth closed her hand on the keys, and the little rubber owl.

Behind them, the door of the Starlite swung shut, and for a moment the car park was quiet enough that she could hear the wind in the bushes, and the traffic on the road beyond them, and a bird.

Then, from the bushes, something heavy and wet landed on the ground.
