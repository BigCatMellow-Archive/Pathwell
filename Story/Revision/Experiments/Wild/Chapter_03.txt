Chapter Three

She caught him up at a bus shelter at the end of the narrow street. He was standing in front of the route map behind the scratched plastic, studying it, as if he were planning a holiday.

"There's no bus," said Elizabeth. "Not till five-forty."

"No," Pathwell agreed. "Not that kind."

On the map, all the colored lines of the bus routes wandered over the city, red and blue and green, the way they do. He put one finger on the plastic, at a place where none of the lines went. A blank bit, between two streets.

Then he drew a line with his finger. A short one, from the nearest green route to the blank place. As if he were adding a stop.

The line stayed there after his finger had gone. A thin gold one, on the map, under the plastic.

And behind the bus shelter, in the brick wall where there had been nothing but brick, there was a door. It was green, with a brass knob, and a little bell on a spring above it, and a card in the window that said OPEN in somebody's handwriting.

"What is that?"

"A shop," said Pathwell. "Books, mostly. Other things. They do a very bad coffee." He took her diary out of his pocket and looked at it, and put it back. "I have to settle up. Come in, or don't. It's warm in there. You've got no shoes on."

"Is this a trap?"

He actually stopped and thought about it.

"Not for you," he said, and opened the door, and the bell went, and he went in.

She stood on the pavement in her wet socks with one shoe in her hand.

A piece of paper blew out through the open door on the warm air and turned over once and landed on her foot. It was a page of a letter. Pencil. Nearly all faded. She could make out My dear, and lower down, the hens are laying again, and at the bottom, a name she couldn't read, and a smell came off it of dried lavender.

She picked it up, because you don't leave a letter lying in the street, and went in.




It was the quiet first. Out on the street there'd been a city, wet tires and a siren somewhere and the hum of the lights. In here there was nothing. Not even the sound a building makes. Her own wet socks were too loud on the floorboards.

Then the shelves.

They went up further than the light did, and they went back further than she could see. They weren't really full of books. Some books. Mostly it was other things. Journals, with the spines cracked. Letters, in bundles, tied with string and ribbon and, once, a shoelace. Recipe cards in tins. School exercise books. Address books, swollen with crossings out. A whole shelf of what looked like birthday cards, every one of them opened.

"Shut the door, would you," called Pathwell, from somewhere off to the left. "It lets the warm out."

She turned and shut it. The door went into the wall and the wall was just shelves, as if it always had been.

"Pathwell?"

Nothing.

Somebody, a long way off between the shelves, said "Shh."

She put the lavender letter down on the nearest shelf, where it would be safe, and went the way his voice had gone.

The first row was only dusty.

The second row, a woman she'd never met was in love. It came off the shelf at Elizabeth like heat off a stove, the whole of it, giddy and frightened and sick with it, a hand that might be held on a walk home, and Elizabeth stopped dead and put her own hand over her chest, because her heart had gone light and quick and stupid, and it wasn't hers. It wasn't hers.

She got through it. She told herself, not mine, not mine, the way you tell yourself it's only a film.

The next row was somebody's father dying slowly in a hospital bed by a window, and that one she knew. That one she knew too well. She had to stop and hold the shelf.

And then the next row, and the one after that, all of them at once, a hundred people, a thousand, all leaning off their shelves at her, so close, so certain that she of all people needed to hear them. And her heart went wrong, and kept going wrong. And she couldn't get a breath into the bottom of her chest, and the shelves were coming in, and the aisle was going narrow, and the light was going gray at the edges.

She sat down on the floor, because it was the only direction left.




Nana had slept in the front bedroom, at the end, because she couldn't do the stairs anymore. Elizabeth had slept next to her on a camp bed, from the hardware store, that creaked when you turned over, so she'd learned not to turn over.

It was meant to be a few months. That was what she'd told the agency where she temped. A few months. It was two years. She learned the pill box, with the days on the little lids. She learned which pharmacist would let her pick up early. She learned to cut toast into soldiers, and that Nana would eat soldiers when she wouldn't eat toast, and that you never, ever said that was why you'd cut it like that.

Nana did the crossword every morning in pen. Toward the end, she did it in her sleep. Elizabeth would wake up on the camp bed at three in the morning and Nana would be saying, quite clearly, with her eyes shut, Seven letters. Something you can't take back. And Elizabeth would lie there in the dark trying to think of it, and never could, and in the morning Nana didn't remember asking.

She never did find out what it was.

After the funeral there was nobody to cut toast for. So she took the house apart instead, a bag at a time, a box at a time. She kept the cookbook, and the spoon, and her diary. The rest went to the curb.

Then a new place, on the far side of the city. And three weeks of boxes. And Mrs. Okafor and her cake pan.

It had been a nice party. She'd been good at it. She'd forgotten she knew how to be good at a party. She'd found the extra chairs, and made sure the old man from 2A had somewhere to sit, and kept everyone's cup full, and said sorry about the boxes eleven times. And at the very end, when everyone had gone, she'd stood in the kitchen with the leftover lemon cake, and without thinking about it at all, she'd cut it into soldiers.

She'd stood there looking at it. Long thin pieces of cake, all in a row, on the plate.

Then she'd covered them with plastic wrap and gone to bed.




She woke in an armchair with a blanket over her knees.

There was an orange cat on the floor in front of her, sitting very upright, looking at her socked feet with great interest.

A cup came into view, held out. Pathwell was behind it.

"Drink that."

She took it, because somebody was holding it out, and drank.

It was dreadful. Thin and burnt and stewed, as if it had been kept warm since sometime yesterday. It tasted like the coffee from the machine in the hospital corridor, the one by the lifts, that she'd drunk at four in the morning more times than she could count.

"That's terrible," she said.

"I know."

She drank the rest of it. Her hands stopped shaking.

"Why does that help?"

"Because you've had it before," said Pathwell. "It's a lot, out there, if you're not used to it. I should have warned you. I'm—"

"If you say sorry—"

"I wasn't going to." He sat on the arm of the other chair. "I was going to say I'm not used to people coming in with me."

The room came together round her. A long wooden counter, worn pale. Lamps that weren't trying very hard. A brass cash register the size of a stove. And behind the counter, a small, round man in a waistcoat with half-moon glasses, writing in a ledger with a dip pen.

The cat got up, and came over, and rubbed its whole length along her shin, purring like a motor. She reached down to scratch its ears.

It bit her toe.

Not hard. Very precisely. Through the sock, on the big toe, and then let go and sat back down and began to wash.

"Ow! Sorry—" Elizabeth stopped. "Why did I say sorry? It bit me."

"Boots takes a toll," said the man behind the counter, without looking up. "From everybody, the first time. He'd say it's only fair."

"Is he bleeding me?"

"Only a little," said the man. "He's a cat of very moderate appetites."

There was a small bright bead of red, coming through the gray wool of her sock.

"Here." Pathwell had a strip of paper in his fingers, soft and folded and refolded, and he opened it and read it before he did anything, as though it were good manners. "Will you go to the dance with me," he read out. "Yes or no. Circle one."

Somebody had circled yes, and then a whole lot of exclamation marks after it, in a different pen.

He wrapped it round her toe, through the sock, gently, and held it, and said something under his breath, and the paper went warm, and then it wasn't there. And for a second she was fourteen and it was the end of a school day and someone had said yes, and her whole face went hot.

Then it was just her foot again. There was a tiny pale line on the toe, under the hole in the sock.

"Scars," said Pathwell. "Not really my department. Sorry."

"You said it."

"That one's allowed. It was a good note."




She got up and went to the counter.

At one end of it there was a pile. Pathwell must have made it while she was out. Bundles of letters, tied. A stack of blank paper, thick and cream-colored and lovely. A box of pencils. Three bottles of ink. A wooden crate full of notebooks, the plain kind, brand new. And on top, folded, a child's drawing of a horse with too many legs, done in crayon.

The man with the half-moon glasses looked at the pile, and then at Pathwell, over his glasses.

"For Camp, Mr. Pathwell?"

"For Camp."

"Did they ask you for it?"

Pathwell looked at the horse. "They need it."

"That wasn't my question."

Pathwell didn't answer it.

The man turned the ledger a page back. And another. And another, a long way back, running his pen down a column of figures with a little noise in his throat at each one.

"Your account," he said. "I'd like that settled. Tonight. Along with this."

"I'm good for it."

"You've been good for it for a very long time," said the man. "You've been good for it so long that you've very nearly been good for all of it." He took off his glasses, and looked at Pathwell without them, which seemed to be worse. "There isn't much left in you, Mr. Pathwell. I'm saying that as a man who keeps accounts."

"Then take what there is."

"Or," said the man, and put his glasses back on, and looked past Pathwell, at Elizabeth.

At her coat. At the place where it was buttoned up over the cookbook.

"The young lady's carrying something," he said, "that would clear the whole of it. Twice over, I should think."

Pathwell turned round and looked at her.

She saw him think it. She saw it go across his face, the sum, all at once, very fast. A book, worth more than everything on the counter. A debt he couldn't pay. A woman who said sorry to banisters.

And then she saw him not say it.

He turned back to the man. "No."

"It's not your no to give, Mr. Pathwell," said the man mildly. He looked at Elizabeth. "Miss?"

Everybody looked at her. Pathwell, and the man, and the cat.

Every part of her wanted to say sorry. Sorry, I can't. Sorry, it's my grandmother's. Sorry, I know it would help, I'm so sorry. She could feel it in her mouth, already shaped.

"No," said Elizabeth.

Just that. Nothing after it.

It sat there in the quiet of the shop, all on its own, and nobody picked it up.

The man nodded, as though she'd told him the weather. "Then it's yours, Mr. Pathwell. As it always is."

"As it always is," said Pathwell.

He didn't look at her again.

The man reached under the counter and brought up a book. Big, black, old, with silver on the edges of the pages and a little mark pressed into the leather of the cover, a dot with rings round it, the way a stone makes rings in a pond. He put it down on the counter between them with both hands.

Pathwell put his right hand flat on the cover.

"What I might have been," he said, quietly, as if he'd said it many times. "For this."

The silver edges of the pages lit. Little roots of light came up out of the book, thin and blue, and found his fingers, and went round his wrist, and up, under his sleeve. And then they went in. She saw them go in, under the skin.

He didn't make a sound. He shut his eyes.

Light came up out of the book like steam off a bath. It stood up in a wobbly line, as tall as his chest, and then it split, and split again, into something like a tree drawn by a child who'd run out of patience halfway. A few of the branches were thick and gold. Most were thin. And all over it, everywhere she looked, there were little black stumps, dozens, hundreds, where branches ought to have been and weren't, the way an old hedge looks when the council's been at it.

"There," said the man, very softly, almost sorry. "You see."

Then it burned.

Then the ends of the branches caught. Orange crept in along every one of them, thick and thin together, toward the trunk, toward his hand, eating the gold as it went. He made a noise then. It wasn't one noise. It was dozens of him, all talking over each other, as if a whole room full of Pathwells had been startled at once, and one by one they went quiet, until there was only one voice, and then not even that.

When it was over there were a few stubby branches left, near the trunk. And a lot of black. And Pathwell was down on his knees on the floorboards with his arm still hooked up in the roots, like a commuter who's dozed off hanging from the strap.

The roots let go. He fell against the counter and got hold of it, and missed, and got hold of it again.

Elizabeth was round the end of the counter before she knew she was going to be.

"Thank you, Mr. Pathwell," said the man, picking up his pen. "That's everything. Settled. Quite clean." He blotted the page. "Would you like it wrapped?"

"God, no," said Pathwell, from the floor.

He sat down there, with his back to the counter. His right arm was shaking from the shoulder down, and he held it against his chest with the other one. There wasn't a mark on it anywhere.

The man came round the counter. He had her diary in his hand. Elizabeth hadn't seen him take it; it must have come out of Pathwell's coat when he went down. The man held it out to her across the corner of the counter.

"I believe this is yours, miss," he said. "It was never his to spend."

She took it.

She stood there holding it, the diary in one hand and her one shoe in the other and her grandmother's cookbook buttoned inside her coat, looking down at Pathwell on the floor.

"You just sold—" She didn't know the word. "Some of you."

"A bit," he said. He flexed his fingers, or tried to. They didn't go very far. "There's not much call for the rest."

"Which bit?"

"No idea. You don't get to pick." He let his head go back against the counter. "That's rather the arrangement."

She looked at him. Gray in the face, on the floor of a shop that wasn't a shop, holding his own arm as though it belonged to somebody else, because he'd wanted some notebooks for some people who hadn't asked for them.

She knew that look. She'd sat beside it on a camp bed for two years.

She put the diary in her coat pocket, and her shoe on the counter, and got down on the floor next to him, and got her shoulder under his good arm.

"Up," she said.

"I'm fine."

"You're not. Up. On three."

He looked at her.

"One," said Elizabeth. "Two."

He got up on three. She took most of him.




The man in the waistcoat brought out a handcart from behind the counter. It came out of a space much too small for it, and unfolded itself twice, and stood there.

Pathwell, leaning on the counter, stared at it.

"How long," he said, "have you had that?"

"Always."

"I have carried things out of this shop in my arms. For years. Up those stairs. In the rain."

"I know. I watched you."

"You never said."

"You never asked," said the man, and began to load it.

Elizabeth laughed. She didn't mean to. It just got out, like a hiccup at a funeral, and the two of them turned and stared at her, and she clapped a hand over her mouth.

"Sorry," she said, through it.

"No," said Pathwell, and he was smiling, gray, holding his arm. "No, that one doesn't count."

The man finished loading the cart, the letters and the paper and the ink and the crate of notebooks, and last of all the crayon horse, on top. Then he looked at her.

"I can put you back on your own street, miss," he said. "Where you came in. It's a quarter past three. You'd be home by half past."

Home. With no door, and black on the walls, and something moving across the kitchen light.

The meeting at nine, with no shoes.

She looked at Pathwell, leaning on the counter, holding his arm against his chest, and pretending to look at the handcart.

"Where's he taking it?" she said.

"Camp Cunnan," said the man. "Some people who live in the woods. They write a great deal. They're always short of paper."

"Is it far?"

"Not from here."

"Can he push it?" she said. "Like that?"

The man looked at Pathwell. Pathwell looked at the ceiling.

"Not very well," said the man.

Elizabeth picked her shoe up off the counter. She put the diary deeper in her pocket, and did up the button, and went and stood by the handle of the handcart.

"I'll push," she said. "You walk. Tell me where."

Pathwell opened his mouth.

"Don't," said Elizabeth.

He shut it again.

And the man in the waistcoat, writing in his ledger, very nearly smiled.
