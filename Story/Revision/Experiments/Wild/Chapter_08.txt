Chapter Eight

She didn't speed up. That was the thing they talked about afterward, the two of them, when they thought she couldn't hear. She didn't speed up and she didn't swerve. She took the van down the hill toward the village green at exactly the speed limit, with both hands on the wheel, the way she'd been taught.

At the bottom of the hill, the road went left, round the green.

She didn't.

"Elizabeth," said Stansbury, who had seen it first, quite calmly. And then, much less calmly, "Elizabeth—"

"Seatbelt," she said.

The van went off the road and over the curb with a bang that threw every library book in the back up into the air at once. It went across the grass, bouncing, scattering ducks. It went down the little slope at the edge of the pond.

And then it went into the pond.

It went in nose first, quite slowly, with a great brown wave going up on both sides of the windscreen, and a sound like a very large man getting into a very cold bath. The engine coughed, and gurgled, and stopped. Water came in round her feet, cold, and then up to her ankles, and stayed there.

The van settled, gently, with its front end in the pond and its back end on the bank, like a dog that has decided to lie down in a puddle.

Ducks were shouting at them from every direction.

It was the crash that woke her.

She sat with her hands still on the wheel and the pond water round her ankles and felt it. Not fear, and not sorry. Something she didn't have a name for. Awake. Everything was very loud and very clear, the ducks and the ticking engine and the water, and for the first time since two o'clock that morning, since the crash in her living room, she knew exactly where she was.

She was in a pond. She'd put herself there.

Stansbury turned his head and looked at her.

"That," he said, "was a library van."

"I know."

"The county's van."

"I know."

From the back, from under a landslide of returned library books, Pathwell said, "You drove into a pond."

She turned round in her seat. He was lying flat on the bench under a heap of hardbacks, with a large-print Agatha Christie on his chest, looking at her.

"Yes," she said.

"On purpose."

"Yes."

He stared at her. She'd been ready for him to be angry. She'd been looking forward to it, a bit. He wasn't angry. He looked at her the way you'd look at someone you'd sat next to on a train for a long time, if they turned to you and said something in a language you hadn't known they spoke.

"Well," he said. "You've got our attention."

"Good." She undid her seatbelt. "Who's Wren?"

Behind her, Stansbury had opened his door and was looking down at the water.

"The books," he said. His voice had gone very strange. "Elizabeth. The books are getting wet."

"I'm sorry about the books."

"Are you? The books? Are you sorry about the—" He stopped. He looked at her. He looked at the water coming in over the floor of the van, and the paperbacks starting to float. Then he did something she hadn't expected. He started to laugh. He put his good hand over his face and laughed until he had to hold his ribs, with the pond going over his shoes.

"Two hundred years," he said, through it. "Two hundred years I've wanted to do something like that to him."

"Then why didn't you?"

He stopped laughing. He looked at her over his hand.

"I don't know," said Stansbury. "I really don't."




She climbed out. The bank was steep and muddy, and the pond was cold, and she went in up to her knees getting round the open door, and the ducks were furious about it.

"Who's Wren?" she said again, over the ducks, to Pathwell, who was crawling out of the back of the van with the Agatha Christie still in his hand.

He looked at her. He looked at Stansbury.

"You can't do it, can you?" said Elizabeth. She was standing in a pond, shouting. She had never in her life stood in a pond and shouted. "You can't even look at me while you don't tell me."

"I'm thinking."

"You always think in his direction."

Pathwell stood on the bank with the water running out of his coat. He looked at the van, and at the ducks, and at her.

"Somebody asked me for help," he said. "A long time ago. Not that long. Her name was Wren. And I helped, the way I help. I paid for it with—" He held up his right hand. "With some of what I might have been. Most of what I paid went where I sent it."

"And the rest?"

"I never found the rest."

While he talked, his right hand had come away from his side. It had come up, a little, and turned, palm out, toward the green. Toward the bench by the pond.

She saw it. He saw her see it.

He put the hand flat on his chest and held it there. And he went very still, and he wasn't looking at her anymore.

He was looking at the bench.

There was a man sitting on the bench.

He'd been there all along. She'd seen him, without seeing him, coming down the hill: a man on a bench by a duck pond, the most ordinary thing in the world. He had a paper bag on his knee. He'd been feeding the ducks, in spite of the sign, and he'd stopped, with a piece of bread in his hand, to watch a library van drive into the pond.

He stood up now. He put the bag down on the bench.

It was Pathwell's face.

It wasn't. It was the face Pathwell's face would have been if someone had taken it apart and put it back together very carefully from a diagram, and not quite trusted the diagram. The same height. The same long coat, almost. But the coat was buttoned all the way to the top, every button in its right hole, and the hair was combed flat, and he stood the way a man stands at the edge of a road waiting to be sure it's clear.

And his right hand came away from his side.

It came up, and opened, and turned, palm out, toward Pathwell on the bank.

The man looked down at his own hand as if it belonged to somebody rude. He took hold of his right wrist with his left and pulled it back down to his side, and held it there.

Then he came down the bank to the edge of the water, where Elizabeth was standing in the pond in her coat with the ducks going round her, and he held out his other hand. His left.

"You'll want to come out of there," he said. "It's very cold. And they bite."

She looked at his face. There was nothing in it that knew her. Nothing at all.

She took his hand. He pulled her out.

"Thank you," she said. She didn't say sorry. She noticed she didn't.

He let go of her hand. He looked past her, at Pathwell. Only at Pathwell.

"You," he said.

It was the word in the mirror.

Pathwell's face had gone completely still. "Apparently," he said.

Stansbury had come round the back of the van, slowly, with his hand pressed to his side. He stopped beside Elizabeth. Not in front of her. Beside.

"You know him?" said Stansbury.

"No," said Pathwell.

"Not exactly," said the man, at the same time.

Elizabeth looked from one to the other. "That's a terrible start."

The man looked at her properly then, for the first time. At her wet coat. At the van in the pond. Back at her.

"Is anyone hurt?" he said.

"No."

"Bruised," said Stansbury.

"Wet," said Pathwell.

The man nodded, as if adding them up.

"How did you find me?" said Pathwell.

"I didn't." The man's voice was quiet and very even, each word put down exactly where he wanted it. "This finds you." He lifted the right hand a little, and it strained toward Pathwell, and he put it down. "Not where. Only which way. I can sit still for a while. Then I can't. So I came this way. And then I couldn't any longer, so I sat down on that bench and fed the ducks, to have something to do with my hands." He looked at the sign. "I know you're not supposed to. I wanted to see what would happen."

"What happened?"

"They were very pleased," said the man. "And then you drove into the pond."

"Do you have a name?" said Stansbury.

The man looked at him for a while, as if names were something he'd had to go and fetch.

"Shade," he said.

"Is that your first name, or—"

"It's the one I picked." He looked at Pathwell. "You don't like it."

"I didn't say anything."

"You thought it very loudly."

Elizabeth looked at them both. Standing on the green by a duck pond, the two of them, in their long coats, with nearly the same face. The more she didn't look straight at it, the more alike they were. The same quick hands. The same tilt of the head when they listened. But everything sat differently on Shade. Pathwell filled up a quiet as soon as one started, as if a quiet were a door someone might come through. Shade let a quiet sit, and get uncomfortable, and didn't help it.

"Who are you to him?" said Elizabeth.

Shade took a long time over it.

"I remember things," he said, "I didn't do. A lighthouse I've never stood in. A woman's kitchen, with a blue oilcloth on the table, and her hands round a cup. A song in a language I don't know. How to tie a knot nobody showed me." His hand pulled at his side. He held it. "They're his. They were in me when I started. Nothing since. I don't know anything about him after that. I don't know you."

"Started," said Stansbury.

"There isn't a good word," said Shade. "I've looked."

"Wren," said Elizabeth.

Shade turned his head and looked at her. Something moved in his face.

"Wren," he said, slowly, as if trying it in his mouth. "Yes. That's her name. I didn't know her name." He looked at Pathwell. "Wren."

Pathwell said nothing at all.

The cold was coming into Elizabeth now, from her wet legs up, and her hands had started shaking, and she put them in her pockets, and they shook in there instead.

Shade looked at her pockets.

"There's a launderette," he said. "In the village. Behind the church. It's open all night. I sat in it, the night before last. It was warm." He looked at her wet coat, her soaked trousers. "You could dry."

"Is that an invitation?"

He thought about it, properly.

"I don't know," he said. "I've never made one."

Pathwell came up the bank, fast, toward her, with his arm round his ribs.

"You're not going with him."

He said it the way he'd said shoes. The way he'd said drive. And for half a second her body very nearly did what it was told, out of plain habit.

Then she heard it.

"No," said Elizabeth. "You don't get to say that one to me anymore."

"You don't know who he is."

"Neither do you. That's why somebody ought to ask him."

"Then I'll come."

"No," said Shade. Not unkindly. Just a fact. "Not you. Not tonight."

Elizabeth looked at Stansbury. He stood by the van in the pond with his hand on his side, watching her, and he didn't tell her what to do. She could see it was costing him something, not to.

She took the foam dagger out of her coat and held it out to him, handle first.

He looked at it. Then at Shade, and at her.

"Keep it," he said.

She put it back.

"You had all day," she said to Pathwell. "All day, and the whole night before that."

"I don't know what he knows."

"Then I'll go and find out."

She turned round and walked up the green, toward the church, in her wet boots, with the water squelching. Shade picked up his paper bag off the bench and folded the top of it over, carefully, twice, and put it in his pocket. Then he came after her. Not beside her. A little behind.

At the top of the green she looked back.

Pathwell was standing on the bank of the pond, beside a library van with its nose in the water, with one arm round his ribs and an Agatha Christie in his other hand. Ducks were walking round his feet, complaining. He didn't look angry. He looked frightened.

Next to him, Stansbury lifted one hand. She lifted hers.

It was only after, turning back toward the church, that she noticed what she'd done. She was following another man with a long coat and an answer, out of one bad night into the next.

It was a bit late to call it an accident.
