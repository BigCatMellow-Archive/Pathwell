Chapter Sixteen

On Tuesday Elizabeth went into the town with Dot and the flour and rang work from the telephone on the wall in the bakery, with a roll in her good hand, because the woman behind the counter said she looked as if she needed one.

Gary answered. Gary always answered. He said, "Elizabeth! We thought you'd died," in the voice of a man who had mainly been worried about the Monday rota.

"I dislocated my shoulder," said Elizabeth.

"Oh no. How?"

She thought about how.

"I fell down some library steps," she said. "Catching somebody."

"Oh no," said Gary again. Then, after a pause, carefully, "Will you be in tomorrow?"

"No."

There was a silence on the line. She could hear him waiting for the sorry. He'd known her three years. There had always been a sorry. She'd said sorry to Gary for having the flu. She'd said sorry to Gary on the morning of Nana's funeral, on the phone, from the car park of the crematorium, for the short notice.

She ate some of her roll.

"Not this week," she said. "I'll ring you next week. When I know."

"Right," said Gary, sounding faintly frightened. "Right. Well. You look after yourself."

"I am," said Elizabeth, and hung up, and the woman behind the counter, who had been listening to every word, gave her another roll for the road.




Pathwell spent the week at the library.

Stansbury said so, at the fire, each evening, when Dot brought him back. He said it in the tone of somebody reporting the weather in a country he'd been told was always sunny.

Pathwell swept. Pathwell held boards while a man from the town nailed them over the windows. Pathwell stood in the street with a clipboard and Mrs. Patel's insurance form and wrote down every book that had been fired out of the book-return slot onto the pavement, by title, by author, by condition, in his small neat hand, three hundred and twelve of them. He found a girl's lost library card in the gutter and posted it through her door with a note. He slept on the floor of the cupboard where the paintings dried. He'd bought his own sleeping bag, from the hardware shop, with money. Mrs. Patel had made him show her the receipt.

"He asked her if there was anything else," said Stansbury, on Wednesday. "On the first morning. She said the glazier's coming Friday. He said, but is there anything else. Anything he could do."

"What did she say?"

"She said, Mr. Pathwell, there is always something else. That's the trouble with you. Hold this board." Stansbury drank his coffee. "He held the board."

Elizabeth looked into the fire.

"Is he all right?"

Stansbury didn't answer for a while.

"He's quiet," he said. "I've never known him quiet. I keep thinking he's left the room."




On Thursday, Pathwell came back to Camp with Dot.

He came across the ring to the fire. Shade was sitting on his log by the pot with Biscuit across his feet, peeling. The peel broke about one time in three now. He'd started keeping count.

Pathwell stopped in front of him. Not too close.

Shade looked up at him with that same polite, total interest. The bus-stop look.

"I said I owed you something," said Pathwell. "I said I'd tell you what. If you wanted."

Shade put the knife down.

"Is it long?"

"Quite long."

"Then sit down," said Shade. "Everybody sits down here. There's a crate."

Pathwell got the crate. He put it down on the other side of the pot, and sat on it, and told him.

Elizabeth didn't listen. She got up and went away to the healer's wagon and sat on the step with Tamsin's baby on her good arm, because that wasn't hers either, and because she thought that if she listened she'd want to help it along.

She watched, though. She couldn't help watching. She watched him tell it. She could see him going carefully, in order, with his hands open on his knees. She could see him not do the voices, and not do the clever bits, and not stop to see how it was landing. She could see Shade sitting across the pot from him, listening, all the way through, with the potato in his hand, and not peeling it.

It took a long time. It was dark by the end.

When Pathwell stopped, Shade sat for a while.

Then he said something Elizabeth couldn't hear. Pathwell answered. Shade said something else.

Then Pathwell put his face in his hands again, for a moment, the way he had on the night of the cutting.

Then he laughed. Elizabeth heard that. It came across the ring, cracked and surprised, like something that hadn't been used for a long time.

She found out what it was later. Dot told her. Dot had been listening behind the truck, because Dot listened to everything.

"He said, what do I owe you," said Dot. "And the other one, Shade, he thought about it for ages. And then he said, I don't know what you owe me, I don't remember any of it. It sounds as if it happened to somebody else. And your brother said, it did, in a way. And Shade said, then I don't think you owe it to me. I think you owe it to her. The one in the kitchen."

"Wren," said Elizabeth.

"Yeah. And your bloke said yes, I know, and that it was too late, she'd died, years ago, at her sister's. And Shade went quiet. And then he said, well, then I think you just owe it." Dot shrugged. "And then he said, but if you want something to do, you could play."

"Play what?"

"Dominoes," said Dot, and grinned. "He's rubbish. Your bloke. He's worse than Shade was. He lost four games. I didn't even have to cheat."

Elizabeth looked across the ring.

They were at the board, on its two crates, by the fire. The two of them. Shade with his still right hand, putting down a domino, and Pathwell on the other side, looking at the dominoes left in front of him with an expression she'd never seen on that face. Not on either version of it.

He'd lost. He looked as if he couldn't believe it was allowed.

"He's not my bloke," said Elizabeth.

"Yeah, yeah," said Dot.




On Saturday morning, at a quarter to ten, the children's room of the Starling County Library smelled of new putty and floor polish and wet paint, and Mrs. Patel was at the desk, with her glasses on their chain, stamping books as though nothing had ever happened.

The windows along the front were new. The glazier had done them on Friday. The glass in the front door was new too, clean and empty, without so much as a smear on it.

There were nine children on the map rug. There'd usually be eleven, Mrs. Patel said; one had chicken pox and one had gone to Spain.

There was Kevin.

He was sitting at the front, cross-legged, with his hand already up, though nobody had asked anything. His nan was on a chair at the back with her handbag on her knee. Elizabeth was on the chair next to her, in Mama Baga's girlhood boots and her sling. Shade was in the corner by the picture books, reading the backs of things, one after another, very slowly, as though he were choosing from a menu in a restaurant he'd been told was expensive.

Stansbury was on the story-hour chair at the front, with his burned hand in its sling and the bear book on his knee. He couldn't hold it and turn the pages. He'd tried, in the cupboard, and dropped it twice.

Pathwell was standing in the doorway of the cupboard.

He'd been standing there for some time.

At ten o'clock exactly, Kevin put his hand up higher.

"Mr. Stansbury," he said. "Where's my bear?"

The room went quiet. Even the girl who was clearly in charge went quiet.

Stansbury didn't answer. He looked at the doorway.

Pathwell came out of it.

Elizabeth watched him come across the children's room. He wasn't wearing the borrowed coat with the library badge anymore. He was in his shirtsleeves. He looked like somebody who'd been sweeping for a week. He came across the room and he didn't look at her, at the back. He didn't look at anyone. He came all the way to the front, to the map rug, and stood in front of Kevin.

Then he sat down on the floor. On the rug, cross-legged, among the children, so that his face was level with Kevin's face.

Kevin looked at him with deep suspicion.

"Who are you?"

"My name's Pathwell," said Pathwell. "I'm Mr. Stansbury's brother."

"You don't look like him."

"No. I'm the younger one."

"You look older."

"I've had a bad week." He sat with his hands open on his knees. "Kevin. Your bear. The one in the hat. It was on the glass in the door. I took it. I used it to do something I shouldn't have done. And the windows broke. And your bear is gone. It's not anywhere. I can't get it back."

Kevin looked at him.

Pathwell didn't say anything else. Elizabeth could see him not saying it. She could see all the next words lined up behind his teeth, the explaining, the making up, the I'll get you a better one, and she could see him leave them there.

He waited.

Kevin's face did several things, one after another, and Elizabeth's whole body wanted to get up off the chair and go to the front and help. She held on to the bottom of the chair with her good hand. Beside her, Kevin's nan had gone very still too, with her handbag.

"Was it a good thing?" said Kevin at last. "The thing you did?"

"No," said Pathwell. "It was a stupid thing. I was angry and frightened and I didn't listen to anybody."

Kevin considered this.

"I do that," he said.

"Yes," said Pathwell. "Most people do. I'm very old. I should have stopped by now."

Kevin looked at the door. At the new glass, clean and empty.

"I spent ages on that bear," he said. His voice had gone wobbly round the edges. "Ages. I did the hat four times."

"I know."

"You don't know. You weren't there."

"No," said Pathwell. "You're right. I wasn't."

Kevin wiped his nose on his sleeve. His nan, at the back, started to get up, and Elizabeth, without thinking, put her good hand on the woman's arm, and the woman looked at her, and sat back down.

Kevin thought for a long time. Nine children waited with him.

"You have to do me another one," he said.

"It won't be your bear."

"I know," said Kevin, scornfully. "It'll be your bear. It'll be rubbish. But you have to do it. Now. With my crayons. And it goes on the door. Where mine was. So everybody knows."

"Knows what?"

"That you did it," said Kevin.

Pathwell looked at him for a long moment.

"All right," he said.

Kevin had his crayons in a tin, in his coat. He got them out. Mrs. Patel brought a sheet of paper from the desk without being asked, and Pathwell lay down on his front on the map rug, among the children, and drew a bear.

It was terrible.

Elizabeth had seen him do things she still didn't have words for. She'd seen him hold a monster off her door and open a green door out of a bus shelter and set his own branches alight to pay a shopkeeper in half-moon glasses. He couldn't draw a bear. The head was too big. The legs were different lengths. The hat looked like a saucepan.

Kevin lay on his front next to him and watched, with his chin on his fists, and gave notes.

"The hat's wrong."

"I know."

"It's a saucepan."

"I know. I'm trying."

"Do the ears again."

He did the ears again.

When it was done, Kevin took it and looked at it for a long while. Then he took a black crayon out of the tin and wrote at the bottom, in big uneven capitals, the way he'd written his own name on the other one:

BY PATHWELL.

He gave it back.

Pathwell got up off the floor. He went to the front door with Mrs. Patel's roll of Sellotape and put the bear up on the inside of the new glass, where the other one had been, facing out into the street, so that anybody coming up the steps would see it. A bad bear, in a saucepan, in crayon.

BY PATHWELL.

He stood and looked at it.

"Now," said Kevin, from the rug, "Mr. Stansbury has to read."




Stansbury couldn't hold the book.

So Pathwell went and stood beside the story-hour chair, and held it for him. He held it open, at an angle, so his brother could see the words and the children could see the pictures, and when Stansbury nodded, he turned the page.

Stansbury read.

Elizabeth had heard him read before, in that room. He'd read the way you'd carry a full bowl of soup up a staircase, slowly, flat, holding very still in case anything spilled. He'd told her why. He took from the children when he read. He couldn't help it.

He didn't read like that now.

She didn't know what was different. Maybe it was his hand, or the week, or his brother standing beside him holding the book. Maybe it was that the bear's hat on the door was a saucepan. He did the bear's voice. He did the owl's voice. He did a voice for the bear's mother that made the girl who was clearly in charge laugh so hard she lay down. Kevin knew every word and said them along with him, a beat early, and Stansbury let him.

And at the end, when the bear found his hat, the children all cheered as if they hadn't known he would, and Stansbury sat back in the story-hour chair and looked, for a moment, astonished. He didn't look tired at all.

He looked across the room, over the children's heads, at Elizabeth.

She remembered asking him. In the cupboard, a week ago, with the paintings drying. Isn't that the wrong way round?

He didn't say anything. He didn't have to.




Afterward, when the children had been collected, Shade came to the desk with his arms full.

Mrs. Patel looked at him over her glasses.

"Six books," she said, "and two DVDs."

"I know," said Shade. "I counted." He put them down, one at a time, on the desk. A book about ducks. A book called Cooking for One, which had a picture on the front of a very sad man with an egg. A book about how to play dominoes and win. A book of crosswords for beginners. A big book with photographs of lighthouses, which he put down on the desk very carefully, and looked at for a moment, and didn't explain.

And the bear book, with the hat.

He took the library card out of his coat and gave it to Mrs. Patel.

She looked at it. SHADE, in biro. The library's own address underneath, in her own writing.

"Do you want to change the address?" she said. "Now. Since you've got one."

Shade thought about it.

"Can I have two?"

Mrs. Patel looked at him for a long moment. Then she took a pen off its chain and wrote something under the library's address, small, and stamped every book, one after another, thump, thump, and gave him back the card.

He looked at it. Elizabeth, beside him, looked too.

Under STARLING COUNTY LIBRARY, MAIN STREET, in Mrs. Patel's neat hand, it said: AND CAMP.

"Due back in three weeks," said Mrs. Patel. "All of them. I'll know."
