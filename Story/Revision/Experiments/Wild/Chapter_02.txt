Chapter Two

He went down the stairs two at a time. She went down them one at a time, with her shoes crushed down at the backs and the cookbook buttoned inside her coat, saying sorry to the banister.

"Why are you apologizing to the banister?"

"I'm not. I'm just saying it."

"To the banister."

"It's a habit. Sorry."

He looked back up at her from the landing below. "You said it to the door, too. Earlier. To the thing at the door."

"I know."

"That was a monster."

"I know what it was."

He seemed to find this very interesting, and went on down.

The street was empty and cold and orange under the lights. It had rained while they'd had the party, and everything was shining. Elizabeth stood on the front step of her building and breathed, because breathing was something she could do, and looked up.

Her window was the only one lit. The kitchen light. She'd left it on. On the windowsill in the kitchen, behind the glass, she could just see the shape of the wooden spoon standing up in the jar where she'd put it, three weeks ago, the very first thing she'd unpacked, before the kettle, before the bed.

She took one step back toward the door.

"I wouldn't," he said.

"There's a spoon."

"There'll still be a spoon in the morning. Spoons are very reliable."

Up in the window, something moved across the light, slow and dark, and then the light was just light again.

She came down off the step.

"Where are we going?"

"Somewhere with something to eat."

"It's two in the morning."

"That's when the good places are open."

He set off down the street, not fast. She went after him, because he had her diary in his pocket, and because the alternative was standing on her own front step with a monster in her kitchen, and because, she realized, he'd said somewhere with something to eat, and some animal part of her had heard that and was already following.

"I have work at nine," she said, at his back.

"That's very far away."

"I have a meeting. It's my first one. With everybody. I have to—" She heard her voice going up. "My landlord's going to kill me. The door is on the floor. The door is lying on the floor. How do I tell him that? What do I even say? I haven't got my phone. I haven't got my keys, I didn't lock—" There was nothing to lock. "I should call the police. Shouldn't I call the police?"

"And tell them what?"

She opened her mouth. She shut it.

"Exactly," he said. "Sorry."

She looked at him. He looked back, quite seriously.

"I thought I'd try it," he said. "See what the fuss was."

They turned a corner. Then another. There ought to have been a bus stop on that corner, she walked past it every morning, and a pharmacy after it, with a broken sign. There wasn't. There was a little shut-up shop that sold, according to the faded letters on its window, UMBRELLAS, CANES & HATS, and then a set of stone steps going down between two buildings that she'd never once noticed, to a narrow street she'd never seen.

"This isn't—"

"Your street? It is. You just haven't been down this end."

"There isn't a this end."

"And yet," he said, and went down the steps.

Halfway down, her left shoe came off.

She'd crushed the backs down to get them on in a hurry, and on the wet stone the heel just slid out from under her, and she sat down hard on the step, and the shoe went on without her. It went down three steps, end over end, and onto the cobbles at the bottom, and into the gutter, where the rain from earlier was still running, fast, toward a grate. And the shoe went into the grate. And through it.

She heard it go. A little plop, down in the dark.

She sat on the step.

He'd turned round at the bottom. He looked at the grate. He looked up at her.

"Ah," he said.

Elizabeth looked at her foot. Her one bare foot, in its sock, on the wet stone.

They were her work shoes. The good ones. The only good ones. She'd bought them the week before she started, for the job, with her first paycheck that wasn't Nana's pension spent on Nana, and she'd stood in the shop for twenty minutes deciding, and the girl in the shop had said they make you look like you know where you're going. And she'd needed that. She'd needed to look like that. She'd walked into the building the first morning in those shoes.

She sat on the step in the cold and found that she was crying.

Not a little. All of it. It came up out of her all at once, from somewhere down past her stomach, and she couldn't stop it and she didn't try. Three weeks of boxes. A whole house she'd taken down to the curb in garbage bags, one bag at a time. A new life in a new part of the city that she'd picked because it was far away, and it had felt like nothing, it still felt like nothing, she'd been waiting every single morning for it to feel like something. And now there was a monster in her kitchen and she had one shoe.

She put her face in her hands and let it come.

He came back up the steps and sat down next to her. Not too close. One step down, so he was a little lower than her.

He didn't say anything for a long time. She was grateful for that, later.

When it slowed down, he took something out of his coat and held it out. It was a handkerchief. A real one, cloth, folded in four, with a letter stitched in one corner that wasn't a P, it was an M.

"It's not mine," he said, when she looked at it. "But it's clean."

She blew her nose on it.

"Sorry," she said.

"There," he said. "That one I'll accept."

She laughed. It was a horrible, wet laugh, but it was a laugh, and it surprised them both.

"Who the hell are you?"

He considered this.

"Pathwell," he said.

"Pathwell what?"

"Just Pathwell."

"Is that a first name or a last name?"

"Yes."

"Why were you at my party?"

"There was cake," said Pathwell, "and nobody asked me who I'd come with. You'd be amazed how often that's true." He looked at the grate at the bottom of the steps. "I'm sorry about your shoe."

"They were my work shoes."

"I know. You wrote about them."

She stared at him. "You read that far?"

"It was very well written. The girl in the shop. 'Like you know where you're going.'" He looked at her sideways. "You spent twenty minutes."

She took the handkerchief away from her face. "Give me back my diary."

"In a bit."

"Now."

"In a bit, Lizzy."

The name went through her like cold water down her back.

Nobody had called her that in seven months. Nobody alive. It had been on the cookbook page, she realized. Lizzy likes the edges. He'd read it there, with his hand on it, and taken it, the same as he'd taken the page.

"Don't call me that," she said.

"I'm sorry."

"You keep saying sorry now."

"I'm getting the hang of it." He stood up, and brushed the wet off the back of his coat, and looked down at her. "Here's the thing. I have to go somewhere. Now, tonight. There's a debt I have to pay, and some things I have to pick up, for people who need them. It's not far. I'd like you to come. I think you'd be safer. I'm not certain. But I think so." He patted the pocket with her diary in it. "That's up to you."

"You're holding my diary hostage."

"I'm holding it," said Pathwell. "Hostage is a strong word."

"You're making me come."

"No. That's what I'm saying. I'm not." He started down the steps, not fast, the way people walk when they don't need to look back to know you'll follow. "Whatever you do next, Elizabeth, it's your choice."

He'd said her name right. Her whole name. She sat there with it a moment.

He went down to the bottom of the steps, and stepped round the grate, and went off along the narrow street without looking back.

She sat on the wet step with one shoe and Nana's cookbook buttoned inside her coat, and his handkerchief with somebody else's letter on it in her hand.

He had her diary. Four years of her. Every bad Tuesday. The plumber. The night Nana died, which she'd written down at four in the morning because there wasn't anybody to tell, so she'd told the diary. The girl in the shoe shop.

He was going to read all of it.

She stood up. She took her other shoe off, because there was no sense in it now, and held it in her hand.

Then she went down the steps after him in her socks.
