Four: Chicken Soup

The back door of the shop let out onto a dirt road in the woods, in the dark, which Elizabeth decided she would think about some other time.

She pushed. He walked beside the cart with one hand on the side of it, not pushing, just keeping it company, and told her when to go left. The road went downhill between trees, soft underfoot. Her socks were soaked through in about a minute. She stopped noticing in about five.

"You're very good at that," said Pathwell, after a while.

"At what?"

"Pushing things. Steering."

"I had a lot of practice."

"With carts?"

"With a wheelchair," said Elizabeth. "Up a ramp. Into the car. Out of the car. Into the doctor's. Two years." The cart went over a root, and she leaned on it, and it went on. "You learn where things are going to stick."

He didn't say anything to that. After a bit, he took his hand off the side of the cart, so he wasn't in her way.

Woodsmoke. Then light through the trees. Then music, a fiddle playing something cheerful and not very well, and a clearing opened out in front of them, full of wagons. Painted ones, faded, in a big ring, with a fire in the middle and smaller fires off it, and lanterns hanging off the ends of things, and washing on lines, and in the middle of the night, at half past three, people. Up. Sitting round the fire, mending things, talking. A child asleep across two other people's laps.

The nearest wagon's door banged open while the cart was still rolling. Out came a woman, small and old, in a shawl the color of a fire truck, and she crossed the clearing at Pathwell like a tugboat that has seen a liner it disapproves of.

She took hold of his chin in one hand and turned his face toward the lantern. She looked at it. Then she let go of his chin and took his right wrist instead, and held up the arm, shaking, without a mark on it.

"How much," she said.

"Hello, Mama."

"How much?"

"A bit."

She let go of his arm. She didn't say anything else. She didn't have to. Elizabeth had seen that face on the matron at Nana's care home every time somebody tried to discharge themselves.

Then she looked past him at the cart. At Elizabeth, in her soaking socks, holding the handle with both hands.

"And who are you?"

"Elizabeth," said Elizabeth. "Sorry. I'm just—he couldn't push it. So I pushed it."

The old woman looked her up and down, socks first.

"Mama Baga," she said. "And you are cold to the bone, and you have no shoes, and you're saying sorry for pushing a cart. Inside."

"Oh, I'm fine, really, I don't want to—"

"Inside," said Mama Baga, and Elizabeth went inside.




People were unloading the cart before she'd got up the wagon steps. Nobody asked what was on it. A man lifted out the stack of blank paper and held it against his chest with his eyes shut, like it was a puppy. A girl took the ink. The crayon horse got passed from hand to hand, very carefully, to the edge of the fire, where somebody looked at it and laughed and showed somebody else.

Mama Baga's wagon was warm and close and smelled of wool and woodsmoke and something green and bitter drying in bunches from the ceiling. There were blankets on everything. There was a curtain across the far end, on a wire, pulled nearly shut.

"Sit."

Elizabeth sat on a stool by a little iron stove.

Mama Baga knelt down, which she did slowly and without any fuss about it, and took hold of Elizabeth's left foot and peeled the wet sock off it. And then the other. Then she rubbed each foot between her hands, hard, the way you'd rub a cold child's, until it hurt, and then until it didn't.

Nobody had touched Elizabeth's feet since she was a little girl. She sat very still and didn't know where to look.

"Sorry," she said.

"For what?"

"I don't know."

"Then don't say it." Mama Baga got up, slowly, with a hand on the stove rail. She went to a chest and came back with a pair of thick gray socks, and then went to a different chest and came back with a pair of boots. Old ones, brown, laced up to the ankle, the leather gone soft and dark with wear.

"Try those."

Elizabeth tried them. They fit. They fit better than her work shoes had. They'd been walked in for years by someone whose feet had been very much like hers.

"Whose are they?"

"Mine," said Mama Baga, "when I was a girl and walked everywhere. Now they're yours." She saw Elizabeth open her mouth. "If you say sorry, I'll take them back."

Elizabeth shut her mouth.

She remembered the handkerchief. She took it out of her coat pocket, crumpled and still damp.

"He gave me this. Pathwell. On some steps. I think it might be—"

Mama Baga took it. She unfolded it. She looked at the M stitched in the corner.

"He took that out of my apron," she said, "when he was twelve years old, and I have not seen it since." She folded it again, small. "It was clean?"

"It was clean."

"Hm," said Mama Baga, and put it in her own pocket, and something in her face went soft for a second and then didn't.

Behind the curtain, somebody coughed. A thin, wet, painful cough, that went on too long.

Elizabeth looked up.

Mama Baga went and drew the curtain back a little way.

There was a bed back there. A young woman was in it, propped up on pillows, and she was very pale except for two bright red spots high on her cheeks, and her hair was stuck to her forehead. And in the crook of her arm was a baby. A very new baby, red and crumpled and furious, asleep at last, with its fists up by its ears.

A thin woman was sitting by the bed with her sleeves rolled up to the elbow, holding the young woman's wrist and looking at nothing, counting.

"Who—"

"Tamsin," said Mama Baga. "The little one came the day before yesterday. Too fast, and in the wrong place, on the road, in the back of a wagon. And now the fever's in her." She looked at the thin woman. "How is she?"

The healer let go of the wrist. "The same. A bit worse." She said it very flat, the way people do when they've already said it to themselves a hundred times. "I've used what I had. Everything. I sent to the other wagons and they've given what they had. It's not enough. It needs something with care in it. Something to hold her here." She rubbed her eyes. "I keep thinking someone will come in the door with something."

Nobody looked at Elizabeth.

That was the strange thing. Nobody looked at her at all. Nobody looked at her coat, buttoned up. Nobody asked.

The young woman in the bed coughed again, and the baby woke up and screwed up its whole face to cry, and the young woman said, "Shh, shh," in a voice with nothing in it, and couldn't lift her arm to rock it.

Elizabeth unbuttoned her coat.

She took out Nana's cookbook. It still smelled faintly of the bakery bag it had lived in for twenty years, and of Nana's kitchen, and of the black stuff from her living room. She opened it on her knees.

It fell open where it always fell open.

CHICKEN SOUP. FOR WHOEVER'S POORLY. And under it, in Nana's leaning capitals, going down the margin, MAKE MORE THAN YOU THINK. NOBODY EATS WHEN THEY'RE SICK SO YOU HAVE TO KEEP BRINGING IT.

She'd had that soup at six, with the measles. She'd had it at seventeen, with a broken heart, which Nana had said was the same thing. She'd made it, herself, from this page, every week of the last winter, and carried it up on a tray, and brought most of it back down again, and made it again the next week anyway, because you have to keep bringing it.

Her hands were shaking.

"This one," she said. "Would this one do?"

The healer looked round, and saw the book, and then at Elizabeth.

"It's yours?"

"My grandmother's."

The healer came over and knelt on the floor in front of her and looked at the page without touching it. Then she looked up at Elizabeth.

"It would do," she said. "It would do very well." She was quiet a second. "You don't have to."

"I know," said Elizabeth.

She knew. That was the thing. At the counter, in the shop, she'd said no, and it had been the first time in her whole life she'd said no without saying sorry after it, and it had been right. And this was the same book. And nobody was asking.

"Take the book," she said.

The healer looked at her. "I'll only need the page."

"Take the whole book." Her voice was quite steady, which surprised her. "If she needs more than one. If anybody does. I can't sit here holding the rest of it and wondering if I should have."

The healer didn't move.

"Make more than you think," said Elizabeth. "That's what she wrote. So."

Mama Baga took the book out of her hands. She took it the way you'd take a baby from somebody, both hands, from underneath, and held it for a moment against her chest.

Elizabeth's hands stayed up in the air, for a second, after it had gone. Then she put them in her lap.

Mama Baga gave it to the healer.

The healer opened it on the blanket at the soup and read the whole page through, slowly, her mouth moving round the words like someone getting a hymn right. Then she laid one palm on the page and rested the other, barely touching, on the young woman's breastbone, just above the sleeping baby.

Nothing flashed. Nothing glowed. The page just went warm, the way a kitchen goes warm on a winter afternoon when something's been on the stove for hours. And the smell came. Onions, and pepper, and chicken, and steam off a pot, and something else, under all of it, that Elizabeth would have known anywhere, which was Nana's kitchen on a Sunday.

Behind her, Mama Baga breathed in.

The ink went black, then pale, then began to go. It went from the top down, line after line, as if someone were wiping a window, and MAKE MORE THAN YOU THINK went last, and then the page itself thinned to nothing under the healer's hand.

The young woman's chest went up, and down, and up, deeper.

The red went out of her cheeks, slowly, as if someone were turning down a lamp.

The baby, who had been working up to a really serious scream, stopped, and looked surprised, and went back to sleep.

The healer didn't move her hands for a long while after the page was gone. When she did, she sat back and let out a breath.

"There," she said. And then, to nobody, very quietly, "There." And she put her face in her hands for a moment, just a moment, and then took it out and was a healer again.

"Will she be all right?"

"She'll sleep. And then she'll wake up, and she'll want to know what everyone's done with the baby while she wasn't looking." The healer shut the book. It was thinner by two pages now. You could see the gaps from the side. "Thank you."

She held it out, out of habit, to give it back.

Elizabeth's hand went out to take it. The way your hand goes out when somebody passes you a plate.

Then she remembered.

She put her hand back in her lap.

"It's yours," she said. "Camp's. Whoever needs it."

The healer looked at Mama Baga.

"Archive," said Mama Baga.




The archive was a long wagon at the edge of the ring, painted a blue that had been bright once, with shelves inside from the floor to the roof and drawers under the shelves. A man sat inside at a narrow desk under a lamp. He was old, and very tall, and folded, as if he'd been made for a bigger room and had decided to make do.

Mama Baga put the cookbook on his desk. He looked at it, and at Elizabeth, behind her.

"Given?" he said.

"Given," said Elizabeth.

He took a card out of a drawer. "Who wrote it?"

"My grandmother."

He waited, with his pen up. She thought he was waiting for a name. She opened her mouth to give it, and found that she didn't want to. Not here, not tonight. It was hers.

"My grandmother," she said again.

He nodded, as if that were a perfectly good answer, which perhaps it was, and wrote, in small square letters, HER GRANDMOTHER.

He opened the book and went through it, a page at a time, all the way to the end. At the two gaps, he stopped and made two marks on his card. At the sugar cookies, he stopped a moment longer, and looked at the torn edge, and at her, and didn't ask.

Then he wrapped it in clean paper and put it on a low shelf by the door.

"There," he said. "Someone will find it when they need it."

"And if nobody does?"

"Then it'll wait." He went back to his ledger. "That's mostly what we do here. Wait with things."




When she came out, the sky over the trees had gone from black to gray.

Pathwell was at the edge of the clearing with Mama Baga. They were looking into the trees together, the way people look down a road for a car that's late.

"It was already at her door," Mama Baga was saying. "Before you did anything."

"Yes."

"And you didn't bring it."

"I don't think so."

"You don't think so."

"It came for her, Mama. It went straight for her door. It's the only thing that makes sense." Pathwell's voice was very sure. "Something's following her. I don't know what or why. That's why she's with me."

Mama Baga looked at him for a long time, the way the man at the shop had looked at him over his glasses.

"Go and see your brother," she said.

"Mama—"

"Go and see Stansbury. He knows more about the cleaners than anybody I know. Take her. Ask him." She reached up and pulled his collar straight, both sides, sharp, as if he were twelve and late for something. "And eat something first."

"Who's Stansbury?" said Elizabeth.

They both turned round.

"His brother," said Mama Baga.

"Older," said Pathwell. "He'll tell you. He tells everyone."

"Is he like you?"

"No," said Mama Baga and Pathwell, at the same time, in exactly the same voice, and then looked at each other, and Mama Baga laughed, a short, surprised bark of a laugh, and went off toward the fire.

Pathwell looked at Elizabeth in her new old boots.

"You don't have to come," he said. "You'd be safe here. Safer than with me, probably. Mama would see to you."

She thought about it. She thought about staying, properly. The fire, and the man with the paper held like a puppy, and the baby, and the boots that fit.

And she thought about her front door, lying on the floor. And about a man standing in her living room telling her something had come for her, and her not knowing what, or why, or whether it was going to come again.

And she thought about him, gray, on the floor of a shop, with his arm against his chest. He wasn't any better. He'd only stood up.

"If something's following me," she said, "I want to know what it is."

He nodded slowly.

"And you're not right," she said. "On your own. You can barely lift that arm."

"I'm perfectly—"

"I pushed the cart," said Elizabeth.

He looked at her. Then he looked down at his own right hand, and closed it, slowly, and opened it again.

"Yes," he said. "You did."
