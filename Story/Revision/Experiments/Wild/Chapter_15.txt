Fifteen: A Bootlace

"It's cracked," said Stansbury.

"I know."

"If you use it again, it'll go. Not might. Will. They don't come back from a crack. That one's had more use in three days than most get in fifty years." He was looking at it on her knee, not at her. "And I don't know what it'll cut."

"The pull," said Elizabeth. "Between them."

"That's what you want it to cut." Stansbury took his hand out of the bowl of water, looked at the cloths, put it back. "It cuts what clings. You don't always know what's clinging. You only find out after." He looked across the fire at Shade. "Whatever he came with. Whatever came out of that kitchen, with him, that was ours first. The lighthouse. The red shawl. How to tie a knot. I don't know if it's the pull that's holding all that in him, or something else. I don't know if it'll stay when the pull goes."

On his log, Shade had stopped moving altogether.

"So he might forget," said Elizabeth.

"He might forget everything he didn't do himself."

Nobody said anything.

Pathwell was looking at his open hand on his knee.

"Then I should do it," he said. "If it costs something. It's mine to—"

"No," said Elizabeth.

He stopped.

"It isn't yours to spend," she said. "And it isn't mine, either." She held the dagger out across the fire, handle first, toward Shade. "It's his."




Shade didn't take the dagger.

He said he'd like to think about it. He said it very carefully, the way you'd carry something full to the brim across a room. He said he'd like to have the day.

Mama Baga said he could have the day, and that she'd have some potatoes for him in the meantime.

So it was Monday at Camp, and it was quite an ordinary Monday, if you didn't count the steam still coming off a patch of burned grass in the middle of the ring. Somebody hung washing out. Somebody went to look for the missing domino. Stansbury slept with his hand on a pillow and his mouth open. The healer looked at Elizabeth's shoulder and said it was coming along, in a voice that suggested she would be the judge of that.

Elizabeth watched Shade.

She didn't mean to. She meant to give him the day. But it was a small place, and he was the only thing in it doing anything strange.

He went round Camp.

He went round it slowly, one wagon at a time, as though he were checking a list. He stood in front of the archive wagon looking at the blue paint until Dot asked him if he was thinking of buying it, and then he went in, and came out again. He went and found the old black-and-white dog under the healer's wagon and sat down on the grass with it and talked to it, quietly, for nearly an hour. He went and stood next to the place where the washing line was tied to a tree and looked at the knot.

Then he came and sat on the log by her.

"I've been looking at what's his," he said. "The things in here that came with me. To see how much it is."

"How much is it?"

"Lots." He was looking at the fire. "Nearly all of it. The way to tie that knot, on the line. The dog's name. He gave the dog's grandmother its name, when he was nine. The inside of that wagon, how it smells, the lamp, the drawer that sticks. All the songs. Every word I know that you can't say in front of Mama." He was quiet a moment. "How to peel a potato."

"You did that," said Elizabeth. "You did the peeling. Not him. He never did."

"I knew how because of him."

"You still did it."

He looked at the potato peel in the dirt by the log. It was still there, from the day before. Long curls of it, unbroken.

"And what's mine," said Shade, "is five weeks. Walking. The other way. A launderette. A woman at a library with glasses on a chain. A paper bag of bread for ducks." He touched his coat, the left side, where the card was. "This. And you. In a pond." He thought about it. "And losing at dominoes. And the healer saying you're welcome."

"That's not nothing," said Elizabeth.

"No," said Shade. "It's not nothing."

He sat for a while.

"If I keep the rest," he said, "I'll always be a bit of him. Somebody'll look at me and see him first. Mama did. You did. Everyone did. The things that come will always come." His right hand pulled at its bootlace. "If I let it go, I'll be five weeks old. I won't know the dog's name. I won't know Mama." He looked across the ring, at Mama Baga, who was hanging up a sheet with her mouth full of pegs. "She'll be a stranger."

"She said she didn't know you yet," said Elizabeth. "When you came. She said, I know his face. I don't know you yet."

Shade turned his head.

"So she could get to know you," said Elizabeth. "Properly. And you could get to know her. From the start. Not from his." She stopped. She didn't know if she was helping. "I don't know. It's not mine to say. I'm only saying she said yet."

He looked over the washing at Mama Baga.

"Will you ask her?" he said.

"Ask her what?"

"If she'd mind."

Elizabeth thought about it.

"No," she said. "You ask her."

Shade looked at her. Then he got up and went across the grass to the washing line, where Mama Baga was pegging out the sheet, and stood next to her, and said something.

Elizabeth couldn't hear what.

She saw Mama Baga take the pegs out of her mouth. She saw her listen. She saw her put a hand on Shade's arm, his left arm, just above the elbow, and say something back, quite short. And she saw Shade nod, and nod again, and then take the other end of the sheet off the basket and help her peg it out, the two of them working down the line toward each other, peg by peg, until they met in the middle.




He came back at dusk.

The fire had been built up. Somebody had found the domino, in Dot's boot, and there had been a long and complicated trial about it which Dot had lost. Stansbury was awake, and gray, and sitting up on his crate with his hand in a sling to match hers. Pathwell was on his own crate, on his own side of the fire. He'd been there most of the day. He hadn't gone near Shade once.

Shade stood in front of the fire. He untied the bootlace from his belt, carefully, picking at the knot with his left hand, and let his right hand go.

It came up at once and pointed across the fire at Pathwell, shaking. Everyone watched it.

"Yes," said Shade.

Elizabeth got up off the ground.

She came round the fire with the dagger in her good hand. The healer had said not to use her other one for anything, and she didn't. She'd have to do it one-handed. She thought that was probably right.

"Where?" she said.

Shade held out his right hand. The pointing one. He held it out level, between himself and Pathwell, across the fire. The bootlace was still round his wrist, in its neat knot, nobody's knot.

"There," he said. "Where it pulls."

She looked across the fire at Pathwell.

He'd stood up. He was standing in front of his crate with his own right hand held out from his side, a little. It was shaking too. She hadn't noticed before. She'd never seen it from this side. It was shaking toward Shade.

"Do you want to say anything?" said Elizabeth. "Before."

Pathwell opened his mouth.

He shut it again.

He looked at Shade, across the fire, and Elizabeth watched him look for the thing to say, the right thing, the clever thing, the thing that would fix it, and watched him not find it, and watched him stop looking.

"No," he said. "I don't think it's mine."

Shade looked at him.

"It was," said Shade. "Some of it. I'll give it back now."

Elizabeth put the edge of the dagger against the bootlace on his wrist.

It was foam. It shouldn't have cut a bootlace. It shouldn't have cut a slice of bread. It went through the lace as if the lace weren't there, and through something else under the lace that wasn't there either, that she couldn't see and could feel, that went taut against the edge for one second like a guitar string and then didn't.

There wasn't a sound. She'd thought there would be.

The dagger went warm in her hand. Then hot. Then it wasn't foam anymore, it was ash, gray, soft, and it went through her fingers and down into the grass, and was gone.

The bootlace fell off Shade's wrist into the fire.

Shade's right hand stayed where it was for a moment, out over the fire, level.

Then he brought it back. Slowly. On his own. He turned it over and looked at the palm, and at the back, and opened it, and closed it. It wasn't shaking.

On the other side of the fire, Pathwell sat down on his crate quite suddenly, as though somebody had cut a string in him too.

He put his right hand flat on his knee. It lay there. Still.

"It's quiet," he said, to nobody. "It's very quiet."




There is a fire. He is standing in front of it.

There are wagons round it in a ring, painted, and the paint is old. There's washing on a line. A sheet, white, moving a little. He doesn't know whose it is. He would like to know.

There is a dog. It has a gray face. It is looking at him with its head on one side, the way you look at something that has changed.

"What's his name?" he says.

Nobody answers. A girl with a long plait looks at him as if he's said something unkind. He goes back over it. It was a question. He thinks it was all right.

"Biscuit," says the girl. Her voice has gone odd.

Biscuit. He gets down, slowly, because he doesn't know how fast you're allowed to get down to a dog, and holds out his hand. The right one. It's very quiet, the hand. He hadn't known it wasn't quiet before. The dog smells it, and smells it again, longer, the way you'd check a list, and then puts its head under it. The head is warm and hard and the ears are soft. He didn't know about the ears.

He looks up.

There's a woman by the washing line. Small. Old. A shawl round her the color of a fire truck. He knows fire trucks. He knows red. He knows a great many things. It's only people he doesn't know. She is looking at him the way the dog did.

He stands up.

"Hello," he says.

"Hello," she says.

"I don't know you," he says. He can tell from the faces that this is a sad thing to say, and he's sorry, so he says that too. "I'm sorry. I don't know who you are."

"No," says the woman. She comes across the grass to him. "That's all right. I'm Mama Baga. I've been here a long time. That's my wagon, the red one. I make the coffee. Everybody says it's terrible." She looks up at him. "And you're Shade."

"Yes," he says. It's the one thing he's sure of. It's like a stone in his pocket that he can close his hand round. "I'm Shade."

He has a card. He knows that too. Inside his coat, on the left. He takes it out. White, plastic, bent at the corner. An owl reading a book. SHADE, in capitals. He wrote those. He remembers the biro, and a woman with glasses on a chain who said well, you're here now, aren't you.

That's his. All of it.

By the fire there's a woman with ash on her fingers and her arm in a sling. He knows her. He knows her the way you know your own handwriting.

"You were in a pond," he says.

"I was."

"With ducks."

"Yes."

"And you didn't let go. On the steps." He checks. It's there. The steps, and the falling, and her arms, and the bottom step. "You didn't let go."

"No," she says.

"Good. That's still there. That's mine." Something is missing from it, though, like a stair that isn't where your foot thought it would be. "I fell. I remember falling, and you. I don't remember why I fell. There's a gap where the why was."

Across the fire, a man has put his face in his hands.

He has a long coat and flour in his hair, and he's making a sound. Shade looks at him carefully, because the man seems to need looking at carefully, and because there's something about his face. Shade can't say what. It's a face like a word you're sure you've seen written down somewhere.

"Who's that?" he says.

Nobody can answer. He watches the woman with the ash try, and not be able to. He watches the big man with the bandaged hand try.

The man takes his hands away from his face.

"I'm Pathwell," he says. "I'm—" He stops. He stands up. "I owe you something. I'll tell you what. If you want. Not tonight."

It's a strange thing for a stranger to say. Shade thinks about it, and finds he doesn't mind.

"All right," he says. "Not tonight."

He sits down on a log by a pot, because the log seems to be for sitting on and the pot seems to want watching. After a while the dog comes and lies across his feet. It's heavy. He didn't know dogs were heavy.

After a while longer, the woman in the red shawl brings him a sack and a knife and puts them in his lap.

"Peel," she says.

He looks in the sack. Potatoes. He knows potatoes. He picks one up and turns it round in his hand. He knows exactly what it is. He doesn't know what you do to it.

"I don't know how," he says.

"I know you don't," says Mama Baga. "Here."

She sits down on the log beside him, and from the way all their heads come up round the fire, he understands that this is something she doesn't do. She puts her hands round his hands, round the knife and the potato, and shows him. The thumb. The angle.

The first peel breaks after an inch.

So does the second.

The third goes nearly all the way round.
