Chapter Fourteen

Afterward, Dot said the worst thing about it was the smell, and Stansbury said the worst thing about it was the size, and Mama Baga said the worst thing about it was that somebody had kicked over the dominoes, and she'd find out who.

At the time, nobody said anything much.

It came out of the trees like a sheet off a line in a high wind. Gray. Wet. Bigger than the one in her flat, bigger than the one in the car park, so big that the edges of it went out past the edges of the firelight and she couldn't see where it stopped. It didn't hurry. It came across the grass the way the tide comes in, not fast, just all of it, everywhere, at once.

The dog stood its ground and barked until Mama Baga picked it up and threw it into a wagon and shut the door.

Elizabeth was already moving.

She didn't think about it. Her body did it the way it had done Nana's stairs, without asking her. She went up the healer's steps one-handed and in through the door. Tamsin was sitting up in the bed with the baby against her, white, trying to get her legs out from under the blanket.

"Don't," said Elizabeth. "Stay there. Give her to me."

"You've only got one arm."

"I only need one."

Tamsin looked at her for half a second. Then she gave her the baby.

It was the lightest thing Elizabeth had ever held. It was furious. She put it against her chest, inside her coat, against the sling, and held it there with her good hand, and it stopped shouting, quite suddenly, as if the coat were a decision it agreed with.

She turned round in the doorway.

From up on the wagon steps she could see the whole of Camp. The fire in the middle. The wagons in their ring. The great gray sheet of the thing coming in over the grass from the trees, slow. Stansbury, in front of the fire, with his baton out, glowing a dull red at the end. Mama Baga beside him, with a burning branch out of the fire in each hand. Dot running for the truck.

And Pathwell, running the other way.

Running for the archive wagon.

She knew what he was going for before he got there. She watched him go up the archive steps two at a time and in through the door, and she knew what was on the low shelf just inside it, wrapped in clean paper. Two pages used. There were a great many pages left. Christmas. Birthdays. Every soup there was. Nana's whole kitchen, every winter of it.

She stood on the steps with the baby inside her coat and didn't shout.

She waited.

He came back out onto the archive steps.

He came out empty-handed.

He stood there. He looked across the ring at her, past the fire and the gray thing coming and his brother with the glowing baton. He looked straight at her.

"Elizabeth," he shouted. "Can I?"

All of Camp heard it. She knew they did.

She thought about the kitchen. Edges for you. Middles for me. Fifteen years of Sundays. Two pages used, and the card in front of it in small square letters: ASK BEFORE USING.

He'd read the card.

"No," said Elizabeth.

It went across the ring like a stone across water.

Pathwell stood on the steps for one moment longer. Then he nodded, once, as if she'd told him a time a train was leaving.

"Right," he said.

He came down off the steps.




"It can't tell," said Elizabeth.

She was saying it to nobody. Then she was saying it loudly, to all of them.

"It can't tell which one it wants! He said so. Shade said. It's like two people with the same name in the phone book. It came for both of you. It doesn't know which." She was shouting now, from the wagon steps, with a baby in her coat. "So don't be in the same place!"

Shade, by the kicked-over dominoes, turned his head and looked at her.

Then he looked across the fire at Pathwell.

His right hand was still tied to his belt with its bootlace. It wasn't pointing at Pathwell. It was pointing at the gray thing, and shaking.

"Will you?" said Pathwell. Across the fire. To him. "Help."

Shade looked at him for a long moment.

"Which way?" said Shade.

"I'll go left."

"Then I'll go right," said Shade. "As long as I can."

And they went.

Pathwell went one way round the ring, toward the trees on the left, running with his arm round his ribs. Shade went the other, toward the trees on the right, with his tied hand pulling and his left hand holding his right wrist and his long coat buttoned to the neck.

And the thing stopped.

It stopped in the middle of the ring, between the fire and the wagons, between the two of them. It had been coming in like the tide, all of it, everywhere. Now it bunched. It reared up in the middle, a great gray lump of it, and leaned left, toward Pathwell, and then leaned right, toward Shade, and then left again, and couldn't. It made a sound she'd never heard anything make, a long wet stretching sound, like a sheet being pulled from both ends. It got thinner. It went gray to pale gray. In the firelight she could see through it in places, to the wagons on the other side.

"Now," said Mama Baga.

Stansbury went in with the baton.

He put the glowing end into the thin part of it, the stretched part, the middle, where it was trying to go two ways, and held it there, and the thing screamed like a kettle, and Mama Baga came round the other side with her two burning branches and put them in too.

Dot put the truck lights on. Full. Every light the truck had, all at once, and the horn, which played the first four notes of something and then gave up.

The thing came apart.

It didn't burst. It didn't go out. It came apart the way wet paper comes apart when you pull it slowly, in long gray strings, and the strings went thin, and pale, and then there wasn't anything in the middle of the ring but steam and a very bad smell and Stansbury standing in the steam with his baton held out in front of him.

The baton was white-hot now, the whole length of it, all the way down to his hand.

He dropped it.

He looked at his hand.

"Oh," said Stansbury, in a small, reasonable voice, as if he'd remembered something he'd left on the stove. "That's not good."

Then he sat down on the grass.




Tamsin took the baby back. She had it out of Elizabeth's coat and against her own chest before Elizabeth could even begin to say anything, and she held it with her eyes shut, rocking, and said into its hair, "Thank you, thank you, thank you."

"She was no trouble."

"She's always trouble," said Tamsin. "That's how you know."

The healer was down the steps and across the ring with her bag before anyone had called her. Stansbury was sitting on the grass with his burned hand held out in front of him, very still, looking at it with interest, the way Shade had looked at the cut on his.

Pathwell came back out of the trees on the left.

Shade came back out of the trees on the right.

They came back across the grass toward the fire, from opposite sides, the two of them, slowly, both looking at the place in the middle of the ring where there was nothing now but steam.

They stopped. Not near each other. With the whole of the fire between them.

Shade's right hand had come round again. It was pointing at Pathwell, pulling at its bootlace.

Pathwell's right hand was in his pocket. Elizabeth could see the pocket moving.

"Thank you," said Pathwell, across the fire.

Shade looked at him.

"You're welcome," said Shade. He said it the way the healer had said it. Exactly the way. As though he'd been keeping it somewhere safe for when it was needed.




It was nearly dawn before anybody went to bed, and nobody did.

They sat round the fire. Stansbury's hand was in a bowl of cold water, wrapped in the healer's cloths, and he had stopped saying it wasn't bad. Dot had picked up all the dominoes and counted them twice. One was missing. She was going to find it if it killed her.

Mama Baga made coffee. It was worse than usual. Nobody complained.

Pathwell sat on his crate on one side of the fire. Shade sat on his log on the other. Elizabeth sat on the ground between them, but not in the middle. A bit nearer Shade.

It was Pathwell who said it. She'd known somebody would. She'd known it would be him.

"It'll come again," he said.

Nobody said anything.

"Not tonight. But it'll come. Another one. Bigger. As long as there are two of us, in the world, this close, they'll keep coming." He was looking at the fire. "We can't keep running in opposite directions. We can't keep doing it in a field full of people with a baby."

"So what do you want to do?" said Mama Baga.

He didn't answer straight away. His thumb was pressing into his palm.

"I know how it went out," he said. "So I know how it'd come back. I made the spill. I could gather it. I could take it back." He looked up, across the fire. "Take him back. Then there'd only be one of us."

Shade didn't move.

"No," said Shade.

"I know what it sounds like—"

"No."

"Stop," said Mama Baga. Not loudly. She didn't need to say it loudly. It was the voice she'd used on the dog. "Stop there. Not another word in that direction."

Pathwell stopped.

He sat on his crate in the gray light, with flour still in his hair from the truck and his right hand in his pocket, and Elizabeth watched him do the thing he always did. She watched him look for the reasons. She could see him find them. She could see him getting them in a row, the way he'd got them in a row at Wren's kitchen table. A baby in a wagon. A burned hand. A thing as big as a field.

She watched him, and she knew he was going to start again, and she knew what it was going to sound like when he did.

"Ask him," said Elizabeth.

He looked at her.

"You don't decide and then go and find the reasons," she said. "Not this time. You don't get to do the sum. Ask him."

The fire was very quiet.

Pathwell looked across it, at the man with his face, on a log, with his hand tied to his belt with a bootlace.

"Will you?" said Pathwell. "Come back. Into—" He couldn't finish it. "Will you?"

"No," said Shade.

Pathwell nodded.

He nodded for a long time, looking at the fire. Elizabeth waited for the next thing. The but. The I understand, however. The reasons.

They didn't come.

"All right," said Pathwell.

That was all.

He sat back on his crate. He took his right hand out of his pocket and put it on his knee, open, and left it there, empty, in the firelight, where everybody could see it.

Elizabeth found she'd been holding her breath. She let it go.

Across the fire, Shade was looking at Pathwell's empty hand as though it were something he'd never seen before. Maybe he hadn't. It wasn't in anything he remembered.

"It'll still come," said Shade at last. Quietly. To the fire. "He's right about that. It will. Another one."

"I know."

"So something has to happen."

"I know."

Elizabeth put her good hand inside her coat.

The foam dagger was still there. She'd forgotten it. It had been against her ribs all night, against the baby, cracked down the middle from the point into the handle, and it was still faintly warm, the way a stone stays warm after the sun has gone off it.

She took it out and held it on her knee.

Stansbury, across the fire, with his hand in a bowl of water, saw it. His face changed.

"It cuts what clings," said Elizabeth.
