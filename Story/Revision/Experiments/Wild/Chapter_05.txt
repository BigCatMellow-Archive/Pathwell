Chapter Five

Camp had a truck.

It was very old and very green, where it wasn't rust, with a flat bed in the back with wooden sides on it, and it lived in the trees at the edge of the clearing with a chicken asleep on the bonnet. Mama Baga said it went out to the world once a week for flour and paraffin and whatever else Camp couldn't grow or make or argue out of somebody. She said it could go out today instead, and that Dot would drive.

Dot was about fifteen. She had a long plait and a short temper and she drove the way people play drums.

"Is she allowed to drive?" said Elizabeth, in the back, holding on to the wooden side with both hands as the truck went down a track that was mainly holes.

"Allowed by whom?" said Pathwell, beside her.

"The law."

"Camp isn't on any maps," said Pathwell. "So, legally, neither is the road. So, legally, she isn't driving. She's just sitting in a moving truck."

"That's not how it works."

"It has been for forty years."

He slept for most of the way, sitting up against the cab with his arms folded over his chest and his chin down. He'd eaten three bowls of porridge at Mama Baga's fire before they left, standing up, very fast, and then gone gray again and quiet. Elizabeth watched him sleep. She watched the right hand on his arm, how it shook a little even while he was asleep.

She took her diary out, and held it. She didn't open it. She just held it, on her knees, the way you hold something you've got back.

At some point the track turned into a road, and the road turned into a town. A little one. A main street with a hardware shop and a bakery and a church, and at the end of it, a long low brick building with a sign over the door that said PUBLIC LIBRARY, and under it, on a paper banner, STORY HOUR SATURDAYS 10AM — ALL WELCOME.

Dot stopped the truck outside it in a way that woke Pathwell up.

"Two hours," said Dot. "Then I'm going for the flour, and if you're not here, you can walk."

"Thank you, Dot."

"Don't thank me. Mama made me."




The library was warm and smelled of floor polish and paper and, very faintly, of crayons.

There was a children's room at the back. It had small chairs and a rug with a map of the world on it, and every wall was covered in drawings. Paper suns. Houses with smoke coming out. Dogs. A great many dogs. A family of four holding hands outside a house, with the sun in the corner, wearing sunglasses.

Pathwell stopped in the doorway and went very still.

"What?" said Elizabeth.

"They're warm," he said.

She put her hand up near a drawing of a black and white dog with a purple collar. Under it, in enormous crayon letters: THIS IS PATCH. HE IS MY BEST.

It was warm. The paper wasn't. It was something on it, coming off it the way warmth comes off a cat that's been asleep in the sun. It went up her arm and settled in her chest, and it was pride: tiny and fierce and complete, a six-year-old's pride in a dog drawn in felt-tip, the best dog, the only dog.

She took her hand back.

"Whose are they?"

"The children's," said Pathwell. "And my brother's. In a way."

"Your brother's what?"

"Excuse me," said a voice behind them. "We don't open until ten."

He was tall, and broad across the shoulders, and his dark hair was combed very flat and very neat, and he had on a cardigan with leather patches on the elbows and a lanyard round his neck with a little laminated card on it that said MR. STANSBURY — CHILDREN'S SERVICES. He looked a good deal like Pathwell would have looked if Pathwell had ever ironed anything, or folded anything, or put anything back where it came from.

He looked at Pathwell. Then he looked at Pathwell's right arm.

"You pruned," he said.

"Good morning, Stan."

"Don't call me Stan."

"It's a good morning."

"It isn't. You pruned. Again." Stansbury's eyes went to Elizabeth, quick, up and down, the boots and the pajama trousers and the coat, and back to his brother. "And you've brought someone."

"Elizabeth," said Elizabeth. "Sorry. Hello."

Stansbury looked at her a moment longer.

"Why are you sorry?" he said.

She didn't know.

"Come on," said Stansbury. "Before the children."




Stansbury's office was a cupboard behind the children's room. It had a desk in it and a kettle and a great many cardboard boxes, every one of them labeled in neat square capitals, and a long wire rack like a clothes airer, and on the rack, drying, were paintings. Children's paintings. Dozens of them. They gave off a warmth that filled the cupboard like a radiator.

On the desk was a box of foam swords.

Bright plastic handles. Soft gray blades. Underneath them, foam daggers, and short batons, and a couple of round shields with dragons on them.

Elizabeth reached for one, because it was a box of foam swords and that's what you do.

Stansbury moved the box away from her, along the desk, without looking.

"Not for playing with."

"They're made of foam."

"They're not for playing with," he said again, in exactly the same voice, the way you'd say it twice to a child who'd heard you the first time.

"Stan makes toys," said Pathwell, sitting down on a box marked PUPPETS — DAMAGED.

"I make tools that look like toys, which is a very different thing. Don't sit on that." Stansbury looked at him. "Tell me. From the beginning. In order."

"There was a party. I was there."

"Why?"

"There was cake."

"Pathwell."

"There was a box of very loud old notebooks," said Pathwell, "and I wanted to read them. All right? And then there was something at her door, a cleaner, a big one. Before I'd done anything. It was just there, at the door, trying to get in. So it was there for her. And I used a page of her grandmother's to keep it off, and she took the book back halfway through, and the working broke, and it came in after the mess."

"After the mess."

"After the waste. Yes."

"So once it was in," said Stansbury, "it did exactly what they do."

"It was very rude about it."

"They're not rude. They're conscientious." Stansbury turned to Elizabeth. "They tidy. Anything that's been spilled. A working that went wrong, bits left over. Get in their way and you'll have a bad time. Otherwise they couldn't care less about you."

"That would have been nice to know at two o'clock this morning."

"I'm sure." Stansbury drummed his fingers once on the desk. "It's the first part I don't like."

"What first part?"

"It was at your door," said Stansbury. "Before he'd done anything. Before there was any mess for it to come for." He looked at his brother. "What was it doing at her door?"

"Coming for her," said Pathwell. "Obviously."

Stansbury looked at him for a long moment.

"Was it?" he said.

Pathwell's face didn't do anything at all. It didn't do anything so carefully that Elizabeth found herself looking from one of them to the other, and opening her mouth to ask, and then not.

Somewhere out in the children's room, a door banged, and a small voice shouted, "MR. STANSBURY! IT'S STORY HOUR!"

"It's twenty to," said Stansbury, to the door.

"MR. STANSBURY!"

"It's twenty to, Kevin."

He stood up. Then he did something Elizabeth didn't expect. He looked at his brother's coat. There was something stuck to the shoulder of it: a sticker. A big round shiny one, a gold star, that said I WAS GOOD AT STORY HOUR. It must have been on the floor of the children's room. Pathwell had picked it up on his coat somewhere.

Pathwell tried to pull it off. It wouldn't come. It had stuck the way stickers stick to wool, all the little fibers grabbing it, and when he pulled, the coat came with it.

Stansbury took a foam dagger out of the box. Gray. Black handle. He touched the edge of it to the edge of the sticker.

The foam lit up, faint, a thin line of orange along the edge. And the sticker let go. Not torn. Not pulled. It just came away from the wool, clean, all at once, and dropped onto the floor, and every fiber of the coat underneath lay down flat again as if nothing had ever been there.

"It cuts what clings," said Stansbury. "Things stuck where they shouldn't be. Bindings. Things that don't come apart easily on their own."

"Does it cut people?" said Elizabeth.

"It's made not to." He turned it over in his hand. "None of these are unbreakable. If one cracks, or splits, or gets warm when it shouldn't, you stop using it. You stop at once."

"What happens if you don't?"

"Something comes apart that you didn't mean to come apart." He put the dagger down.

"MR. STANSBURY!"

He picked up a book off the desk. A big picture book, very worn, about a bear.

"Story hour," he said. "Stay here. Don't touch anything." And to Pathwell, "Especially you."

"You haven't read to them in years," said Pathwell. "You told me. You have a volunteer."

Stansbury stopped in the doorway with the bear book under his arm.

"Mrs. Patel," he said, "has a cold."




Elizabeth went and stood in the doorway of the children's room to watch.

There were eleven of them, on the map rug, sitting cross-legged and not sitting still. There was a boy with his hand up before anyone had asked anything. There was a girl who was clearly in charge. There was Kevin, who was much smaller than his voice.

Stansbury sat down in the grown-up chair at the front, which was still a bit small for him, and opened the bear book, and held it up the way you hold it up so everybody can see the picture, and began to read.

He read very badly. He read as if every word were a word in an instruction manual. The bear said "Where is my hat?" as if he were asking the time.

And the children sat and listened as if it were the best thing they'd ever heard.

Pathwell had come up behind her. He stood next to her in the doorway with his arms folded over his chest, holding his right hand still.

"He's terrible," she said.

"He's awful. He's always been awful. He used to read to me." Pathwell watched his brother turn the page. "He'd stop to correct the grammar."

"Why doesn't he do it anymore?"

Pathwell was quiet a moment.

"Ask him," he said. "He likes being asked. Nobody does."

At the front, Kevin put his hand up.

"Mr. Stansbury, is the bear going to find his hat?"

Stansbury looked at him over the top of the book, very seriously.

"I have read this book," he said, "three hundred and forty-one times, Kevin. I can't tell you."




Afterward, when the children had been collected, and Kevin had shown Elizabeth his own drawing on the wall (a bear, with a hat), and Mrs. Patel's cold had been discussed at length by three different mothers, Stansbury came back into the cupboard and sat down at his desk and put his head in his hands for a moment.

"I don't know how she does that every week," he said.

"You were good," said Elizabeth.

"I was dreadful."

"They didn't think so."

He took his hands away from his face and looked at her, as if she'd said something in another language.

"Why did you stop?" she said. "Reading to them."

He looked at her a while longer. Then at the drying rack, at all the paintings.

"Because I take a little," he said. "From them. When they make these. It comes off them while they're painting, like heat. It's gone by the time the paint's dry anyway. Nobody misses it. They take every picture home and put it on the fridge." He touched the edge of the nearest painting with one finger, not quite on it. "But it seemed wrong. To take a little off someone, and then sit down in front of them and be nice to them. So I stopped doing the nice part."

"Isn't that the wrong way round?" said Elizabeth.

Stansbury opened his mouth. Then he shut it.

From the box of puppets, Pathwell laughed, once, short and surprised, and then put his hand over his ribs.

"I'm going to want a drink," said Stansbury, eventually. He stood up, and picked up the box of foam swords, and put it under his arm. "Dot's gone for the flour. I've got the van. There's a bowling alley on the way to anywhere, and it has a bar, and I am going to sit in it." He looked at Elizabeth. "You're welcome. Both of you are welcome. Nobody is going to prune anything, or be followed by anything, or set anything on fire, while I have one drink. Is that understood?"

"Yes, Stan."

"Don't call me Stan."

He went out with the box. Pathwell got up off the puppets, slowly.

At the door, Stansbury stopped. He came back. He took the gray foam dagger out of the box and held it out to Elizabeth, handle first, the way you'd hand a teenager the car keys.

"Keep that inside your coat," he said. "Don't play with it."

"I'm not going to play with it."

"Everybody says that," said Stansbury, and went out.

She looked down at the dagger in her hand. It was soft and light and silly.

She put it in her coat, in the inside pocket, next to her diary.
