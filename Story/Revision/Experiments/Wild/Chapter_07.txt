Chapter Seven

It came up out of the bushes the way a wave comes up the beach, all at once and much bigger than you'd thought, and stood there between the van and the road, black and shining, with the gray day sliding off it.

The smell came across the car park in a wave of its own.

Elizabeth's hand went to her coat, to the inside pocket, to the soft silly handle of the dagger.

It didn't come for her. It didn't even slow down. It went past the van, and past her, the way water goes past a stone in a stream, and it went for Pathwell.

"Oh," said Pathwell. "Oh, no, you don't—"

Stansbury had the van's back doors open. He came out with a foam sword and hit the thing in the side with it, hard, like a man beating a rug, and the sword went orange all down its length and sank in, and the whole wet mass shivered and didn't stop.

Pathwell was going through his coat. Every pocket. Left, right, inside, outside. He came up with a cinema ticket, a pebble, a button and a paper bag with a bit of porridge in it from Mama Baga's.

"Paper!" shouted Stansbury. "Anything written on!"

"I gave it all to Camp!"

"All of it?"

"They were short!"

It hit him at the knees and went up him like a tide coming in. Up his legs and over his chest and over his shoulders, smooth, and quick, and quiet, and for a moment there was still a hand, his left hand, out at the side, opening and shutting.

And then not.

"Pathwell!"

Stansbury caught her arm. "Don't touch it—"

She took her arm back.

It cuts what clings.

She had the dagger out. It weighed no more than a pencil. Her hand was shaking so badly the tip wobbled like a compass needle.

Close to, the smell made her eyes stream. She couldn't keep her eyes on any part of it. Wherever she looked, it slid off to the side, like print in a dream. She could see pieces of him in there. A shoe. A bit of coat. A pale bit of wrist, and then not.

She put the dagger in where the black met the edge of his coat.

It should have bent. Foam bends. It didn't. The gray edge lit, a thin line of orange, and something gave, not like cloth tearing but like a sticker coming off wool, all at once, cleanly. And the black flinched back from it, the whole of it, in one long shiver.

She drew the dagger down. The opening came down after it. His shoulder. His jaw. His face, gray, with his eyes shut, and his mouth open, as though he'd been halfway through saying something clever.

"Come on," she said. "Come on, come on."

She grabbed his coat with her other hand and hauled. Nothing. She sat back on her heels and hauled again, and the black hauled back, and where it brushed her sleeve the cold came straight through, through skin, into the bone, and her fingers went stupid and wouldn't close.

She cut again. Across his chest. Where it was holding him tightest.

It let go of him all at once. She went over backward onto the tarmac and he came with her, on top of her, and all the breath went out of her in one go.

Neither of them moved.

Then he coughed. Once. And again. A horrible, wet, rattling sound, and she'd never in her life been so pleased to hear anything.

She wriggled out from under him and got him onto his back. "Breathe."

He opened one eye. "Trying."

"Try harder."

"I'll put it on the list," he said, and coughed for a long time.

Over by the van, Stansbury had dropped the sword and picked up something else. A short foam baton, orange from end to end and glowing, and about as frightening as a pool noodle. The black thing was gathering itself up again, slower now, as if it had been offended. Stansbury walked straight up to it and struck the tarmac with the end of the baton, once.

Heat. It hit her face like the blast when you open the oven to check on the roast. Under the thing, a white line ran out across the tarmac, and the black buckled.

Stansbury hit it again, right in the middle.

It burned from the inside out. Nothing else caught. Not the hedge, not the van's back tire a yard off. The thing sagged, and slumped, and broke into wet lumps that spat on the tarmac and spread and went thin, and then there was nothing but some black streaks on the ground and a smell that looked as if it planned to stay the night.

The door of the Starlite stayed shut. Inside, faintly, somebody bowled a strike, and the very old men cheered.

"Is it dead?" said Elizabeth.

Stansbury laid the baton on the bonnet of the van as if it might go off again. "Gone."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what I've got."

Pathwell sat up. Both his hands were shaking now. Not only the right.

She took his arm. He leaned on her, and he didn't make a joke about it, and she found that frightened her more than anything else had.

Stansbury crouched down in front of his brother. He took his chin in one hand and turned his face to the light, the way Mama Baga had done, exactly the way, and looked into one eye and then the other.

"Did you lose anything?"

"A pebble. Some porridge."

"Pathwell."

"Dignity," said Pathwell. "Mostly dignity."

Stansbury pressed his ribs, and Pathwell's breath stopped halfway in.

"Bruised. Nothing's gone. It'll hurt every time you laugh, which serves you right."

Then Stansbury sat back on his heels and looked at the black marks on the tarmac. He looked at them a long time.

"It went for you," he said.

Pathwell didn't say anything.

"It went past her," said Elizabeth. "It didn't even look at me. It went straight past."

Stansbury looked at her. Then at his brother.

"What happened at the mirror?"

"Nothing," said Pathwell.

"It said you," said Elizabeth.

They both turned and looked at her.

"In the mirror. By the lockers." She heard her own voice, careful and flat, like somebody giving evidence. "His reflection said something. His mouth didn't move. But in the mirror it did. It said you. Like that. Like it was talking to him."

Nobody said anything.

Pathwell shut his eyes.

"Thank you, Elizabeth," he said, very politely.

Stansbury didn't look surprised. That was the part she kept. He looked like someone opening a bill he'd known was coming and adding it up in his head, very quietly, without moving his face.

"What does it mean?" said Elizabeth. "Is that bad? What does it mean?"

"Van," said Pathwell, and got up.




She drove. Stansbury sat in the front with her, and Pathwell lay down along the bench seat in the back among the boxes of returned library books, with his arm over his eyes.

The van smelled of books. Old paper and plastic covers and somebody's banana. She had to pull the seat right forward. The little rubber owl on the keyring swung against her knee.

For a while nobody said anything.

Then Stansbury turned in his seat, toward the back. "How long?"

"A while."

"How long is a while?"

"Long enough I stopped thinking it was new." Pathwell's voice came from under his arm. "A pull. Not all the time. Like a thread caught on something."

"Toward what?"

"I don't know."

"Since when?"

Pathwell didn't answer.

"Since Wren," said Stansbury.

Elizabeth looked in the mirror. "Who's Wren?"

Nobody answered her.

"Maybe," said Pathwell.

"What happened with Wren?"

"Not now, Stan."

"Who is Wren?" said Elizabeth. "What happened?"

"Elizabeth." Pathwell took his arm off his eyes. In the mirror she could see him looking at the back of her head, kindly, the way you'd look at a child asking about death at a funeral. "Just drive."

Her hands went still on the wheel.

It wasn't unkind. Unkind she could have done something with. It was practical. It was what you said to the driver, so she'd get on with the driving and the grown-ups in the back could talk.

She drove.

Behind her and beside her, the two of them went on talking, low, across her, in the kind of code families use in hospitals. Half sentences. Names she didn't know. Wren. The lighthouse. The winter. Long gaps. Every so often one of them would look at her the way you'd check on a dog asleep on the back seat.

She knew this job, she thought. She'd done it for two years. Drive there. Find somewhere to park. Sit on the plastic chair outside the consultant's room with Nana's coat on her knee while they talked about Nana behind the door. Drive back.

She drove, and they went on not telling her, and the odd thing was that her hands stopped shaking on the wheel. They went quite calm.

The road went out of the town and between fields. There was a village ahead. A church. A green. A pond, on the green, with ducks on it, and a bench beside it, and a sign that said PLEASE DO NOT FEED THE DUCKS BREAD.

She watched the pond come up toward them as they went down the hill.

She looked at it the way you look at a door you've decided to go through.
