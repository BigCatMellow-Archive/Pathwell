Twelve: Peel

Dot drove the way she always drove, and every hole in the track went up through the flour sacks into Elizabeth's shoulder, and she held on to the side of the truck with her good hand and didn't say anything at all, because there wasn't anything to say that wasn't a scream.

Shade sat across from her on a sack, with his cut hand wrapped in his own handkerchief, and watched her.

At the worst hole, he leaned across and put his good hand flat on top of her good hand, on the side of the truck, and held it there. He didn't say anything about it. He didn't look at her while he did it. He looked out at the trees.

At the next hole, he let go.

"Sorry," said Elizabeth, through her teeth. "For falling on you."

"You didn't fall on me," said Shade. "I fell on you. And you didn't let go." He looked at her. "Why didn't you let go?"

She thought about it, holding on.

"You don't," she said. "When somebody's falling. You don't."

He nodded slowly, and looked back out at the trees, as if she'd told him a rule of a game and he was going to need to remember it.




Camp was awake. It was always awake.

Mama Baga was standing in the middle of the ring before the truck had even stopped, with her red shawl round her and her arms folded, and she looked at the flour, and at Elizabeth in the back of the truck holding her arm, and at Shade beside her.

She looked at Shade for a long time.

He climbed down. He stood in front of her, in his long buttoned coat, with his bandaged hand, and his right hand at his side shaking.

"You're the same," he said. "You're in here. The red shawl. The fire. You're exactly the same."

Mama Baga looked at his face. Elizabeth watched her look. She watched her see it, the face, Pathwell's face, all of it, and then watched her look past it, the way you'd look past a coat somebody was wearing to see who was in it.

"No," said Mama Baga. "I'm older. I used to be quicker." She looked at his right hand. "And you are not who's in there. Who are you?"

"Shade."

"Then I don't know you, Shade. I know his face. I don't know you yet." She unfolded her arms. "That's a hand that needs looking at. And she needs looking at worse. Inside. Both of you."




It was the healer from before, the thin one with her sleeves rolled up. She didn't seem surprised to see Elizabeth again. She didn't seem surprised by anything, but she looked at the arm, the way Elizabeth was holding it, and her mouth went thin.

"Out of the socket," she said, without touching it. "I can see it from here."

"I fell down some steps."

"Who pushed you?"

Elizabeth opened her mouth to say nobody. She closed it.

"Somebody who was trying to help," she said.

The healer looked at her for a moment. "Those are the worst ones," she said.

There was someone else in the wagon. In the bed at the back, propped up on pillows, with a baby asleep across her chest like a hot water bottle. Tamsin. Her face had color in it now. Her hair was washed. She was looking at Elizabeth with enormous eyes.

"It's you," she said. "The book. It was your book."

"It's Camp's book."

"It was your book," said Tamsin, firmly, the way people talk when they've been very ill and are not going to waste time being polite. "I had chicken soup in my dream. All night. There was a woman in it with a wooden spoon, telling me to eat more. She was very bossy."

Elizabeth sat down on the stool by the stove, quite suddenly.

"Yes," she said. "She was."

The healer knelt in front of her. "I'm going to put it back in now," she said. "It's going to hurt a great deal, and then it's going to stop hurting a great deal and only hurt a bit. Is there anything you want first?"

Elizabeth looked at her.

"No one asks that," she said.

"I'm asking."

She thought about it.

"Can I hold something?"

The healer looked round the wagon. Then Tamsin, from the bed, held out her hand. The hand that a day ago hadn't been able to lift to rock her own baby.

Elizabeth took it with her good hand.

Tamsin held on hard.

"Ready?" said the healer.

"No," said Elizabeth.

"Good," said the healer, and did it.

Everything went white. Somewhere inside her there was a deep, wooden thunk, like a drawer being shoved shut in another room, and the white faded to gray, and the gray settled into one enormous, ordinary ache.

She was still holding Tamsin's hand. Tamsin was still holding hers. The baby hadn't even woken up.

"There," said the healer. "Now you don't use it. Not for anything. Not for a cup. Not to push a cart. Not to catch anybody falling down any steps, ever again, until I say."

"I can't promise that."

The healer looked at her.

"I can't," said Elizabeth. "I'll try. But I'll catch them. If somebody's falling."

The healer looked at her a while longer. Then she tied the sling.

"Yes," she said, "I expect you will."




When the healer turned round to Shade, he was standing by the door with his bandaged hand held out in front of him, the way a child holds out a hand for a sweet.

"Can I see?" said the healer.

He looked at her as though nobody had ever asked him that before. Maybe nobody had.

"Yes," he said.

She unwound his handkerchief and cleaned the cut and bound it properly with clean cloth. He watched every turn of the cloth with total attention.

"Thank you," he said, when she'd finished.

"You're welcome."

"Is that what people say? You're welcome?"

"Mostly."

He seemed to put it away somewhere. "Thank you," he said again, to try it, and she said, "You're welcome," again, and Tamsin, in the bed, laughed, and the baby woke up and was furious about it.




Outside, Camp was eating breakfast. Mama Baga put a bowl of porridge into Elizabeth's good hand and a bowl into Shade's bandaged one, and pointed at a log by the fire.

They sat on it.

Shade ate his porridge left-handed and very slowly, the way you'd eat something you'd been told about and didn't quite believe in yet.

Then Mama Baga came and stood in front of him, and dropped something in his lap. A sack of potatoes. A knife.

"Peel," she said.

He looked at the potatoes. He looked at the knife. He looked up at her.

"I know how," he said slowly. "It's in here. How to do it. The thumb, the angle." He picked up the knife. "But he never did. Not once. I'd know. He knows how and he never did it."

"Then you'll be the first," said Mama Baga, and went away.

He peeled a potato. It took him a long time. The peel came off in one long curl, all the way round, without breaking, and he held it up and looked at it in the light.

"Look," he said to Elizabeth.

She looked.

"That's mine," he said.

She found her eyes had filled up, and didn't know why, and didn't say sorry for it.




The archive was where it had been, the long wagon painted a blue that had been bright once. The tall folded man was at his desk inside under his lamp.

Nana's cookbook was on the low shelf by the door, wrapped in its clean paper. There was a card propped in front of it now, in his small square letters.

HER GRANDMOTHER.
GIVEN BY ELIZABETH.
TWO PAGES USED. ONE FOR A YOUNG WOMAN AND HER BABY.
ASK BEFORE USING.

She stood and read it.

"I put the baby in," said the archivist, without looking up. "It seemed relevant."

"It is," said Elizabeth.

She took the diary out of her coat.

It was battered. It had been in her pocket through a monster and a pond and a launderette and down some library steps. The corners were soft. There was a smear of duck-pond mud across the back that she was fairly sure wasn't going to come off. Inside it was four years of her. The plumber. The Tuesdays. The girl in the shoe shop. The night Nana died, written at four in the morning to nobody.

The archivist looked at the diary. Then at her. He didn't reach for it.

"I want to give you this," said Elizabeth.

He waited.

"He read it," she said. "Pathwell. At my party. Sitting on my floor. He read it like a magazine, without asking, and he didn't put it down when I came in. He just kept reading." She turned it over in her good hand. "I want it to be somewhere people ask."

The archivist put his pen down.

"It won't be yours," he said. "Once you give it. You understand that. You can't have it back because you want it."

"I know."

"And if it's ever needed—"

"I know. I gave you my grandmother's book. I know." She held it out. "Ask me before anyone reads it. That's all. Just ask."

He took it in both hands.

He took a card out of his drawer and wrote on it, and showed her. ASK DONOR BEFORE READING. And then, after a moment, under that, smaller, in a different kind of capitals, as though he were writing something down that he'd been told and agreed with:

ESPECIALLY PATHWELL.

"Is that allowed?" said Elizabeth.

"I'm the archivist," he said. "It's allowed."

He didn't shelve the diary beside the cookbook. He laid it in a shallow wooden tray just inside the door, among other new arrivals waiting their turn. A bundle of letters. A child's school report. Somebody's reading glasses, which had very obviously been handed in by accident.

It was close enough that she could have reached out and taken it back.

She went out without touching it. That was harder than the shoulder.




When she came out, the sun was up over the trees.

Shade was on his log by the fire, with the sack of potatoes and a big pot, and the pot was nearly full of peeled ones, and round his feet in the dirt there were a great many long curls of potato peel, every one of them unbroken.

He looked up at her. He looked at her empty hand, where the diary had been. He didn't ask.

She sat down on the log next to him. Not too close.

He gave her a potato.

She looked at it. She looked at her arm, in its sling.

"I can't," she said. "One hand."

"I know," said Shade. "You can hold it. And I'll peel it. That's two hands."

She held the potato. He peeled it, carefully, round and round, in one long curl, while she held it still.
