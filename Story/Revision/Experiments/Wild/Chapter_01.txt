One: The Good Ones

NANA'S SUGAR COOKIES (THE GOOD ONES)

2 cups flour
1 cup sugar
1 cup butter, soft, NOT melted
1 tsp vanilla
A pinch of salt. A PINCH. Not a teaspoon, whatever anybody says.

Butter and sugar till pale. Then the rest. Chill if you can wait. You can't.
Roll thin. Cut. Hot oven, till the edges go.

Lizzy likes the edges.




It was the crash that woke her.

Elizabeth said "Sorry," into her pillow, before she was awake enough to know what for. Then she lay there and listened. Nothing. Then a small, careful scrape from the living room, like somebody putting a lamp back exactly where they'd found it.

She got up.

The party had been the building's idea. Mrs. Okafor from 4B had knocked on her door the second week and said that a new person on the floor was a reason, and that she had a cake pan and nothing to do with it, and Elizabeth had said "Oh, you don't have to," and then "Sorry, yes, of course, that's so kind," and that had been that. Fourteen people she didn't know had come and stood in her living room among her boxes and eaten Mrs. Okafor's lemon cake and been very nice about the boxes. She'd said sorry about the boxes eleven times. She'd counted, afterward, lying in bed.

There was a man sitting on her living room floor.

He was sitting cross-legged in the middle of the rug with one of her boxes open in front of him and a paper plate of leftover lemon cake balanced on his knee, and he was reading her diary.

She knew him. Sort of. He'd been at the party. He'd been the one who stood by the boxes all evening, reading the labels on them like they were wine lists, and she'd thought he was somebody's husband. Everybody's husband, a little. The kind of man who comes with someone.

Everybody else had gone home at eleven.

"Sorry," said Elizabeth. "Can I help you?"

He held up one finger, the way you would to a waiter, and finished his page. Then he turned it.

"That's my diary."

"It's very good," he said. "You're very hard on yourself on Tuesdays."

"Sorry—" She stopped. "No. Why am I sorry? Put that down."

He looked at her for the first time. He had a pleasant, tired, ordinary face, the kind you'd forget on a bus, and a long coat he hadn't taken off all evening, and lemon cake on his chin.

"I will," he said. "In a minute. I'm nearly at the bit with the plumber."

Something hit the front door.

It wasn't a knock. It was a sound like a sack of wet laundry thrown hard at the wood from the other side of the hall, and the door jumped in its frame, and the chain swung.

The man looked up at the door. Then at her.

"Are you expecting anyone?"

"It's two in the morning."

"That isn't a no."

"No. No. I'm not expecting anyone."

He put the diary down on the rug, open, face down, to keep his place, which made her want to scream, and stood up.

She went to the door. She didn't know why. It was her door. Somebody was on the other side of it. You go to the door.

She put her eye to the peephole and saw nothing. Black. Not the hall. Not the light. Just black, pressed right up against the little lens, wet, and moving.

The smell came under the door. Low tide, on a hot day. Rotting seaweed and something underneath it that was worse.

"Sorry," said Elizabeth, through the door, to whoever it was. "We're closed."

The door bent inward. Not much. An inch, in the middle, as if somebody very large were leaning on it. The wood made a sound wood isn't supposed to make.

The man was suddenly beside her. She hadn't seen him cross the room. He put one hand flat on the door, the way you'd put a hand on a horse to calm it, and stood listening with his head on one side.

"Hm," he said.

"What is it?"

"Something that shouldn't be here." He took his hand off the door and looked at her, and there was something in his face now that hadn't been there over the diary. Interest. Sharp, careful interest, the way a man looks at a lock he's been asked to pick. "Something that's here for you, by the look of it."

"For me?"

"It's your door."

The door bent again. Further. Plaster came down off the frame in a fine white rain onto her bare feet.

"You've got a cookbook," he said. "An old one. Handwritten. It was in the box by the window, I saw it earlier. Where is it?"

"How do you—"

"Where."

"The kitchen," said Elizabeth. "On top of the bread bin. Sorry, it's—"

He was already gone into the kitchen.

He came back with it. Nana's cookbook. It lived in a bread bag, a plastic bread bag from a bakery that had closed twenty years ago, with a red twist-tie on the end, because Nana had said that kept the damp out of it and Elizabeth had never had the nerve to find out whether that was true. He tore the bread bag off it like wrapping paper.

"Hey—"

"What's your favorite thing in here?"

"What?"

"Don't think about it. Your favorite. The first one."

The door groaned.

"The sugar cookies," she said. "Nana's sugar cookies. The ones with the—"

He'd found it already. Turned straight to it, as if he'd known where it would be. A page gone soft and gray with years of hands, and stuck to it in one corner a little crust of something that had been dough once. NANA'S SUGAR COOKIES (THE GOOD ONES). And under that, in a different pen, newer, shakier: Lizzy likes the edges.

He put his hand flat on the page.

"You might want to stand behind the couch," he said.

Under his hand the writing began to shine.

It didn't glow all at once. It went the way the pen had gone, a stroke at a time, gold following Nana's hand along every letter, slowing where she'd slowed, stopping where she'd lifted the pen to think, starting again. It went down the list of ingredients. Two cups. One cup. A pinch, which Nana had put in capitals, because a pinch was a pinch, not a teaspoon, whatever anybody said. It went all the way down to Lizzy likes the edges, and lit that last of all, and brightest.

The door split down the middle.

Something black came through the crack, slow and thick, like cold honey, and began to feel its way down the wood toward the floor.

The light under his hand was gathering. It was pooling up between his fingers.

And Elizabeth, who had not once in thirty-one years taken anything out of anybody's hands, reached out and took the cookbook out of his.

She didn't decide to. She just couldn't have him doing that to Nana's sugar cookies, whatever it was, in her living room, at two in the morning, without asking. She took the book by its spine and pulled.

He didn't let go in time. The page tore. It came away clean along the old fold and stayed flat under his palm, shining, and the rest of the book came away in her hands.

And the light went into her.

It went in through her hands, through the book, and for a second she wasn't in her living room at all. She was nine, and standing on the step stool at Nana's counter with flour to her elbows, and the kitchen was hot from the oven and the radio was on to a ball game neither of them was listening to, and Nana was taking the tray out, and saying, "Edges for you, middles for me," the way she always said, every single time, and putting the hot cookie with the most edge on it into Elizabeth's hand, and saying, "Blow on it first, Lizzy, you're not a dragon."

And then she was on the floor of her living room, on her back, with the cookbook on her chest and the ceiling going round.

The light in his hand had gone wrong.

It was spitting, the way a cheap sparkler spits, and bits of gold were dripping off the edge of the torn page and running down his wrist and dropping on her rug, where they lay there flickering like spilled fat in a pan.

The door gave up.

It came off its hinges and fell into the room flat, with a bang, and the thing came in over it.

It was big. Taller than him. Black and wet and shapeless, with no front and no back, and the eye kept sliding off it, the way the eye slides off a word you've stared at too long. It came across the fallen door and it went straight over the little puddles of gold on the rug, and they went out underneath it, one, two, three, like somebody pinching out candles.

Then it went for the light in his hand.

He brought the page up to meet it.

When they hit, there was a sound like a bell rung under water. The thing shuddered all over and pulled back, and the smell came off it in a wave that hit Elizabeth in the stomach.

She did what he'd told her. She got behind the couch. She was sick there, on the floor, behind her own couch, and then she lay with her cheek on the carpet and her arms round Nana's cookbook and listened.

Banging. A crash that was probably the lamp. The man saying, quite calmly, "No, that's mine, that's not for you," as if he were taking something back from a dog. A long, wet, sliding sound, and then a thump that shook the floor under her cheek.

Then nothing.

Then a hum, so low she felt it in her teeth before she heard it, growing, until the windows rattled in their frames.

A flash of white light over the top of the couch, and a soft pop, like somebody opening a bottle in another room.

"You can come out," he called. "It's gone. Most of it."

She came up over the back of the couch one inch at a time.

The living room was destroyed. That was the only word for it. There was black stuff on every wall, and on the ceiling, and in the boxes, slowly dripping, and it smelled like a fish market in August. The lamp was in two pieces. The door was lying flat in the middle of the room like a raft.

He was standing in the middle of it all, with her diary in one hand and the paper plate of lemon cake in the other.

The cake was perfectly clean.

"I kept it off the floor," he said, when he saw her looking. "Seemed a shame."

"What," said Elizabeth. "What was. What."

"That? Nothing very interesting. A cleaner. It cleans things up." He ate a bit of the cake. Then he stopped chewing and looked at her properly, at her face, at the cookbook held against her chest. "Hang on. You really don't know?"

"Know what?"

"Huh," he said. And he looked at the broken door, and at her, and back at the door, and she watched him add something up and not like the total.

He went to the doorway, stepping round the worst of it, and leaned out into the hall, and looked up and down. When he came back, he'd decided something. She could see it in the way he stood.

"Right," he said. "Well. Your flat's been marked."

"Marked?"

"Marked. Magically. Speaking." He waved at the walls with the cake. "So I really think we should be somewhere else."

He said it the way people say the tickets are sold out when they haven't actually checked.

"I'm not going anywhere," said Elizabeth. "Sorry. I'm not. I don't even know your name."

"Very sensible. Where are your shoes?"

"I'm not—"

"Shoes are always a good idea," he said, kindly. "Wherever you're not going."

She looked down at the cookbook in her arms. The torn edge, where the sugar cookies had been. Her thumb was on it, running along it, the way you'd run your tongue over a chipped tooth.

"I heard her," said Elizabeth. "Just now. I heard my Nana. She said—"

She couldn't say what she'd said.

He was quiet for a moment. It was the first time he'd been quiet since she'd come into the room.

"She wrote it," he said. "That's all. When people write things down, they leave a bit of themselves in it. That was her bit." He looked at the torn page in his own hand, gone dull and gray now, nothing on it at all. "I'm sorry. That was her, and I used it."

It was the first time all night she'd heard anybody else say sorry. It was so strange that she nearly didn't hear what came after it.

"Shoes," he said.

Down the hall, outside, something hit a door. Wet. Heavy.

They both looked at the empty doorway.

"I'm only saying," he said, "shoes."

She got her shoes on. She didn't untie them first; she just jammed her feet in and crushed the backs down. She got her coat off the hook, over her pajamas. When he held his hand out for the cookbook, she very nearly gave it to him.

She didn't. She put it inside her coat, and buttoned the coat up over it.

He looked at the coat, and at her. Something went across his face, very quick. It might have been surprise.

"And the diary," she said, and held out her hand.

He looked down at the diary in his own hand as if he'd forgotten he had it.

"I'm not finished with it," he said.

"I know you're not."

There was another wet thud down the hall, closer.

He put the diary in his own pocket, and smiled at her, as if this were a perfectly reasonable compromise, and held out his hand.

"Ready?"

"No."

"Perfect."
