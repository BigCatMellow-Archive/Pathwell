Chapter Ten

They didn't need magic to find her. Stansbury said so afterward, with some satisfaction. It was two in the morning in a village of four hundred people, and there was exactly one light on in the whole of it, and it said SUDS.

The bell over the door went.

Elizabeth was sitting on the long bench in front of the dryer in her own clothes again, warm, with the Christmas jumper folded on her knee because she hadn't been able to decide whether to put it back in the box. She didn't get up.

Pathwell came in first. He'd found a dry coat from somewhere, or Stansbury had; it was too short in the arms and had a library badge on the lapel. Stansbury came in behind him with a carrier bag of wet books, and set it down by the door, carefully, like a bag of puppies.

Pathwell looked round the launderette. At the dryers, going round with nothing in them. At the bench. At the empty end of the bench.

"He was here," he said.

"He went."

"Which way?"

"The other way," said Elizabeth. "As long as he can."

Pathwell stood there a moment in his short coat.

Then he looked at her, and she could see him at it: reading her face for whatever was about to come out of it, so he could be there first, with something ready.

She didn't give him a shape.

"Tell me what you did," she said.

He went quiet.

"To Wren," said Elizabeth. "He says you know. He says it isn't his to tell. So I'm not asking whether now's convenient. I'm not asking whether I'm up to it. I'm asking what you did."

"Lizzy—"

"Elizabeth."

He stopped. He looked at Stansbury, by the door.

Stansbury looked back at him, and didn't move, and didn't help.

"Elizabeth," said Pathwell.

He sat down. Not next to her. On the floor, with his back against the dryer opposite, with the heat of it on his back, so they were facing each other across the tiles, the way two people sit in a hospital corridor when there aren't enough chairs.

"She lived in a lighthouse," he said. "Wren. Her husband was the keeper, for forty years. Then he died. And they put a machine in the lighthouse instead, the way they do now, so it didn't need a keeper. And the board wrote to say she'd have to leave the cottage. It wasn't hers, you see. It was the keeper's. And there wasn't a keeper anymore."

He looked at his hands.

"She wrote to me. Somebody'd told her about me. She asked me to help her write a letter to the board. That was all. She wanted help with the letter. She'd never written a letter like that. She wanted to get the words right." He was quiet a moment. "She had a blue oilcloth on the kitchen table. And a cup of tea she never drank. She just held it. And the sea out of the window, gray."

"Shade remembers that," said Elizabeth. "The kitchen. Her hands."

His hands went still.

"Does he," he said.

"Did you help with the letter?"

"I read the letter. The one from the board." He was looking at the floor now. "And I knew it wouldn't work. I knew the man who'd signed it. A dry, careful little man, who'd never in his life changed his mind about anything. A letter wouldn't have moved him an inch. And she was old, and the winter was coming. And I sat at that table and I did the sum."

Behind him, the dryer went round with nothing in it.

"So I made a working," said Pathwell. "Instead. A pull. On him. On the man on the board. So he'd change his mind. So he'd wake up one morning and decide the old woman could stay, and not know why he'd decided it."

"You made him," said Elizabeth.

"I made him want to."

"That's the same."

"Yes," he said. "I know. It is."

"Did she ask you to?"

"No."

"Did you ask her?"

"No." His thumb was pressing into the palm of his other hand, hard, over and over. "I didn't ask her. I didn't ask him. I knew the quickest way. I'd pay for it myself. It was mine to pay. So it seemed like mine to choose."

Elizabeth sat on the bench with the reindeer jumper on her knee.

"What happened?"

"He changed his mind. The letter came. She could stay." Pathwell looked up at her at last. "And she knew. She knew it hadn't been her. She'd wanted to stand up in front of them and fight for it. She'd been writing that letter for a month. She'd have lost, probably. But it would have been hers to lose." His mouth went thin. "She moved out a fortnight later. To her sister's. She said she couldn't sleep in a house somebody had bought for her with something she hadn't been told about. She wrote to me and said that. In very good words. She'd always been going to write a very good letter."

"Where is she now?"

He didn't answer straight away.

"In her sister's churchyard," he said. "Three winters ago. I heard in the spring. I didn't go."

The launderette was very quiet. Even Stansbury, by the door, had gone still.

"And the working didn't settle," said Pathwell. "It wasn't clean. I'd paid for a thing she didn't want and he never chose, and it had nowhere proper to go. Some of it was left. More than should have been. A pull, with nothing on the end of it. I went looking, and I couldn't gather it all up again."

"And then Shade."

His face changed. Less guarded. Something under it she didn't want to name.

"And then Shade," he said.

"You made him."

"I made the mess he came out of," said Pathwell, slowly. "That part's mine. How you get from a mess to a man, I've no idea. I'm not sure anybody has."

"Would you do it again?"

"I wouldn't know how."

"That's not what I asked."

He looked at her across the tiles.

"No," he said. "I wouldn't."

She thought about the kitchen. The blue oilcloth. The sea. An old woman's hands round a cup, and a man across the table, certain.

"And my door," she said.

"For me. Or for him, following me." He held her eyes. "Never you. I said your door was marked because I'd made my mind up about what was going on before I'd even looked. Like the letter. Decide first. Then go and fetch the reasons."

She was quiet a long time.

"You thought paying for it yourself made it all right," she said. "That if it cost you, it was yours to decide. That's how it works with you."

He didn't answer.

"Isn't it?"

"At the time," said Pathwell, "I'd have said I was being grown-up about it."

"And now?"

"Now I haven't got a word for it."

"Yes, you have," said Elizabeth. "You've got enough. What came after, the part that turned into him, maybe you didn't choose that. Everything before it, you chose."

He looked down at his hands. Then back up at her.

"Yes," he said.

She let out a breath. It went out of her like something she'd been carrying up a lot of stairs.

By the door, Stansbury picked up his carrier bag of wet library books, and held it against his chest.

"Where now?" he said.

Pathwell turned his head toward him, the way he always did, to answer.

"Camp," said Elizabeth.

They both looked at her.

"Because Mama Baga isn't either of yours," she said. "Because nobody there belongs to anybody. And because Mama rubbed my feet." She heard how that sounded. She didn't take it back. "Nobody's rubbed my feet since I was six."

Stansbury looked at her a moment. Then he nodded.

"Dot," he said. "She'll come past the library in the morning. With the flour. She always stops at the library on the way back, because I give her the books the library's throwing out, and she pretends she doesn't want them." He hitched up the carrier bag. "We can walk there by morning. It's not far."

"Shade's gone that way," said Pathwell, quietly. "The library. That's where he'll be. He thinks of it as—" He stopped.

"His address," said Elizabeth.

Pathwell looked at her sharply. "How do you know that?"

"He told me," said Elizabeth. "We talked. He talks, if you sit and watch the dryer with him."

She stood up. She looked at the reindeer jumper on her knee. Then she folded it again, neatly, and put it back in the lost property box, on top, where whoever had lost it would see it first.

"Come on," she said.
