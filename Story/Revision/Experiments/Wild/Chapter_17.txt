Seventeen: A Wooden Spoon

She went home on the bus.

That was her idea. Pathwell had said, on the library steps, under the bad bear in the saucepan hat, that he'd see her home. He'd started to say how. Something about a door. She'd said she'd like to go on the bus, on her own, if that was all right.

He'd looked at her for a moment.

"All right," he'd said.

That was all. She'd waited for the rest of it, and there wasn't any, and she'd realized on the way to the bus stop that she'd been waiting for the rest of it all week, every time he spoke, and that it had stopped coming somewhere around Thursday.

It was two buses and a train. She sat by the window the whole way with her arm in its sling and Mama Baga's girlhood boots on her feet and a paper bag of rolls on her knee from the woman in the bakery, who had said she was not to go all that way on an empty stomach, as if she'd known Elizabeth for years. Nobody on any of the buses looked at her twice. A woman with a sling and a bag of bread. She could have been anybody. She found she liked it more than she could say.




The door of the flat had been mended.

Not mended. Replaced. It was a different door, a newer one, painted the same sort of cream as the others on the landing, with a new lock that shone. There was a note taped to it in very large careful writing.

ELIZABETH. I HAVE YOUR NEW KEY. DON'T WORRY. — B. OKAFOR, 4B.

Mrs. Okafor opened her own door before Elizabeth had even finished reading it. She must have been listening for the stairs.

"Oh, there you are! Oh, your arm! Oh, I'm so sorry, I'm so sorry, come in, no, come here, no, sorry, you want your own place, of course you do." She had the key in her hand already. "The door was all broken, the whole frame, in the night, I heard it, I thought it was a burglar, I'm sorry, I rang the landlord, I hope that was all right. I'm so sorry. There was a terrible smell. Like a pond. I'm sorry. I had the man put a new one on. I'm sorry, I should have asked, but you weren't—you were—"

"Mrs. Okafor."

"Sorry."

"You don't have to be sorry," said Elizabeth. "You rang the landlord. You got me a door. Thank you."

Mrs. Okafor stood on the landing with the key in her hand and looked at her.

"I know," she said, after a moment, a bit helplessly. "It's just a thing you say. Isn't it?"

"Yes," said Elizabeth. "I know it is."

She took the key. She opened her new door.

The flat was the same. Exactly the same. The boxes, half-unpacked. The rug with the little scorch marks on it, like spilled fat, where gold had dripped off a torn page. The welcome card on the windowsill, in Mrs. Okafor's writing, with balloons on it.

And next to the welcome card, in the jar on the kitchen windowsill, the wooden spoon. Standing up straight. Exactly where she'd left it.

Spoons are very reliable.

She stood in the kitchen door, in her coat, and looked at it for a long time.

Then she put the kettle on. It took ages, one-handed. She made a cup of tea, and drank it standing up, looking out of the window at the street, and then she went into the living room and started on the boxes.




It took her nine days to unpack. She could only use one arm, and she kept stopping to sit on the floor and look at things.

She went to see the doctor, with the machine. The doctor looked at the pictures of her shoulder and said whoever had put it back in had known exactly what they were doing, and asked where she'd been when it happened. Elizabeth said she'd been visiting family. She said it before she'd thought about it, and then, on the way home on the bus, she thought about it a great deal.

She went back to work on the second Monday. Gary had moved her desk nearer the window, without being asked, which was the kindest thing anybody at work had done for her in three years. He said, "Good to have you back," and then, after a pause, "Is there anything—you know. That you need," and she thought about it properly before she answered, and said, "A chair with arms, please," and he went and found one.

She said sorry eleven times the first week. She knew, because she counted.

She said it to the photocopier. She said it to a man in the lift. She said it to Gary when she couldn't hold a file and a coffee at the same time.

The second week, she said it four times.

The third week, she said it twice, and both times she meant it.




On the second Saturday she made the sugar cookies.

She hadn't decided to. She was looking for something in the kitchen drawer and found the cookie cutters, Nana's, in a little bag, the ones shaped like stars and hearts and one like a horse that never came out properly. And she stood in the kitchen with the bag in her good hand and realized she didn't have the recipe.

The recipe was in a wagon in a field, on a low shelf, wrapped in clean paper. Except that it wasn't. That page was gone. It had gone gray under a stranger's hand on her own living room floor, and then gone nowhere.

She stood in the kitchen for a while.

Then she got the spoon out of the jar.

She did it from memory. Two cups of something. One cup of something else. A pinch, and a pinch was a pinch, whatever anybody said. She did it one-handed, with the bowl wedged against her hip on the counter, with the spoon. It came out too wet, and she added flour, and then it came out too dry, and she added an egg, which she was fairly sure Nana had never done, and then she stopped worrying about what Nana would have done and just did it.

She rolled them with a bottle because she couldn't find the rolling pin. She cut stars and hearts. She cut a horse, and it didn't come out properly.

The edges burned. Not badly. Just at the very edge, a thin dark line round every star, the way they used to.

She stood in the kitchen with the tray in front of her and the smell of them filling up the flat, filling up all the corners where the boxes had been, and found that she was crying, and didn't say sorry to anybody for it, and ate the edge off a star.

There was a knock at the door.

It was Mrs. Okafor. She'd smelled them on the landing.

"Sorry," said Mrs. Okafor. "I don't want to—only it smells like—sorry."

"Come in," said Elizabeth. She wiped her face with the back of her good hand. "I made too many. I always make too many. Do you like edges or middles?"

Mrs. Okafor looked at the tray. At the stars with their burned edges.

"Middles," she said. "If that's all right. I'm sorry. I know the edges are supposed to be the best bit."

"No," said Elizabeth. "That's perfect. That's exactly right."

They sat on the floor, because the chairs were still under a box, and Elizabeth broke the edges off and ate them, and gave Mrs. Okafor the middles, and Mrs. Okafor told her about her husband, who had died eleven years ago, and who had also liked edges. Elizabeth told her about Nana. Not all of it. Some of it. The crossword. The camp bed.

Mrs. Okafor said sorry nine times in an hour. Elizabeth counted. She didn't mind at all.




On the third Saturday there was a letter.

It was the first letter that had come to the flat that wasn't a bill or for the person who'd lived there before. The envelope was addressed in capitals, small and careful, each letter put down exactly where it was wanted. It had a stamp on it, very straight, in the exact corner.

She knew who it was from before she opened it.

DEAR ELIZABETH,

THIS IS MY FIRST LETTER. MRS. PATEL SAYS YOU START WITH DEAR EVEN IF THEY ARE NOT. I THINK YOU ARE THOUGH.

PATHWELL HAD YOUR ADDRESS. I ASKED HIM FOR IT. HE SAID HE DID NOT KNOW IF YOU WOULD MIND AND THAT I SHOULD ASK YOU. SO I AM ASKING. DO YOU MIND.

I HAVE A JOB. IT IS AT SUDS. THE S IS STILL OFF. I FOLD. THE LADY WHO OWNS IT SAYS I FOLD BETTER THAN ANYONE SHE HAS EVER HAD AND ALSO I DO NOT TALK TOO MUCH. SHE PAYS ME ON FRIDAYS. I WALK THERE FROM CAMP. IT TAKES AN HOUR. I GO THE RIGHT WAY THE WHOLE WAY NOW. I DO NOT HAVE TO GO THE OTHER WAY.

MAMA SAYS YOUR SHOULDER IS TO BE LOOKED AFTER. I AM TO SAY THAT.

THE PEEL GOES ALL THE WAY ROUND NOW. NEARLY ALWAYS. BISCUIT IS WELL. TAMSIN HAS CALLED THE BABY ROSE. DOT STILL CHEATS.

PATHWELL COMES ON THURSDAYS. WE PLAY. HE HAS LOST THIRTY-ONE GAMES. HE HAS WON TWO. HE DID NOT KNOW WHAT TO DO WHEN HE WON. HE SAID SORRY. MAMA SAID THAT IS NOT WHAT YOU SAY WHEN YOU WIN AND HE SAID HE KNOWS BUT IT IS THE ONLY ONE HE HAS PRACTICED.

HE TOLD ME ABOUT WREN AGAIN. I ASKED HIM TO. I HAVE A BOOK WITH LIGHTHOUSES IN IT. HERS IS NOT IN IT. I LOOKED.

I AM DOING THE CROSSWORDS FOR BEGINNERS. I AM BAD AT THEM. MRS. PATEL SAYS EVERYBODY IS AT THE START.

I DO NOT HAVE ANYTHING ELSE. I THINK THAT IS WHERE YOU STOP.

FROM SHADE.
(STARLING COUNTY LIBRARY, MAIN STREET, AND CAMP.)

She read it four times standing in the hall. Then she sat down on the floor with her back against the new door and read it again.

Then she got up and found a pen, and a piece of paper, and sat at the kitchen table with the spoon in its jar at her elbow, and wrote back.

First, she told him she didn't mind. She underlined it.

She told him about the cookies. She told him about Mrs. Okafor and the middles, and Gary and the chair with arms, and the doctor with the machine. She told him that her shoulder told her about the weather now, the way the healer had said it would, and that it had said rain on Tuesday, and been right.

Then, at the bottom, she wrote:

I have one for your crossword. My Nana used to say it in her sleep. I could never get it.

Seven letters. Something you can't take back.

She looked at it for a while. Then she signed it, and put it in an envelope, and wrote his address on the front, both of them, the library and Camp, and walked down to the postbox on the corner in Mama Baga's boots, in the rain her shoulder had told her about.
