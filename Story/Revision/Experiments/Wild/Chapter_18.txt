Chapter Eighteen

On the fourth Saturday, at ten o'clock in the morning, somebody knocked on her door.

They knocked, and then they waited.

She knew it was him by the waiting. Nobody else she knew waited like that, as though they'd been told about it and were trying it out. She stood in the hall in her socks with her arm out of the sling for the first time, held carefully against her side, and looked at the new door for a moment before she opened it.

Pathwell was on the landing.

He was wearing his own coat. She hadn't seen it since the car park. Somebody at Camp had washed it and mended the sleeve, and it was too clean, and he looked uncomfortable in it, like a boy on the first day of term. He had a paper bag in one hand and an envelope in the other.

"Hello," he said.

"How did you get here?"

"Two buses and a train." He looked faintly amazed about it. "A woman on the train asked me to hold her baby while she found her ticket. I've never been asked that. I didn't know what to do. I held it." He thought about it. "It was sick on me."

"They do that."

"So I've been told. By everybody on the train."

She looked at him on her landing, in his too-clean coat.

"Come in," she said.

He looked at the doorframe. At the new door, painted cream. He looked at it the way you'd look at a place on a road where you'd once had an accident.

Then he came in, and wiped his feet, which he had not done the first time.




She made tea. He stood in the kitchen doorway and watched her do it, two-handed now, slowly, and didn't offer to help, and she could see what it cost him.

The bag was from the bakery in Starling. Rolls. The woman behind the counter had sent them. She'd asked after Elizabeth's arm, he said, and then told him to take these, and then told him he looked as if he'd been sleeping in a cupboard, which he had.

The envelope was from Shade.

"He gave it to me on Thursday," said Pathwell. "He said it'd be quicker. He said he didn't trust the post with this one." He put it on the table, carefully, squared to the edge. "I didn't read it."

"I know you didn't."

"It's sealed. He licked it twice. He watched me put it in my pocket."

She laughed. She opened it at the table, with her tea, while Pathwell sat on the other side and looked out of the window, and didn't read it upside down, which she could see him not doing.

DEAR ELIZABETH,

I ASKED EVERYONE.

MAMA SAID PROMISE. THAT IS SEVEN.

MRS. PATEL SAID APOLOGY. THAT IS ALSO SEVEN. SHE SAID IT VERY QUICKLY AS IF SHE HAD KNOWN FOR YEARS.

DOT SAID GOODBYE. THAT IS SEVEN TOO. BUT DOT CHEATS.

I ASKED PATHWELL. HE DID NOT SAY ANYTHING FOR A LONG TIME. THEN HE SAID HE THOUGHT IT WAS WHICHEVER ONE YOU HAD JUST DONE.

I THINK THAT IS CHEATING AS WELL. BUT I HAVE WRITTEN IT DOWN.

FROM SHADE.

P.S. THE PEEL WENT ALL THE WAY ROUND NINE TIMES ON WEDNESDAY.

She read it twice. Then she put it down on the table, and put her hand flat on it.

She didn't say anything for quite a long time.

Pathwell went on looking out of the window.

"Did he ask you?" she said.

"He asked me."

"Which one did you think it was?"

He didn't answer straight away.

"I don't know," he said at last. "I told him the truth. I've been giving people the quick answer for so long, I'm not sure I've ever stopped to work one out."




He had something else in his coat. She'd known he had. He'd had his hand on it since he sat down.

He took it out and put it on the table between them, beside Shade's letter.

It was a page. Old paper, soft, torn clean along one edge where it had come away from a binding. It was gray. Not dirty. Gray all through, the way ash is gray. There was nothing on it at all. No writing. No ingredients. No pinch, underlined twice. No Lizzy likes the edges.

"I kept it," said Pathwell. "I don't know why. It's nothing now. There's nothing on it. I used it all." He didn't look up. "It was in my coat the whole time. In the shop. At Camp. In the pond. It's been in my pocket for a month."

She looked at it.

She didn't pick it up.

"There's something else," said Pathwell.

"I thought there might be."

"I told you it came for me. At the launderette. That I'd decided what was happening before I'd looked, the same as I decided about Wren's letter." He was looking at the page now. Only at the page. "That's true. But it's not all of it."

She waited.

"I'd read your diary," he said. "Before the door went. I'd read the stairs. And the camp bed. And the soup, carried up every week of the winter and brought back down again. I'd read four years of you carrying things." His thumb pressed into his palm. "And when the door went, and I had to run, and I didn't know how far I'd get, I thought, she'll carry it. If I'm in trouble, she'll carry it. She'll get me there. So I told you it came for you. I didn't only get it wrong. I picked you. For that."

The kettle ticked as it cooled.

"You picked me," said Elizabeth, "because I'd carry you."

"Yes."

"And I did."

"Yes," said Pathwell. "You pushed me halfway to Camp in a handcart. With my bad arm hanging over the side. And I let you. I lay in the cart and let you."

She sat with her hands round her tea.

She waited to be angry. She could feel it, somewhere. It was there, the way the weather was there in her shoulder. But it wasn't the main thing. She was surprised by what the main thing was.

"I'd have done it anyway," she said. "If you'd asked."

"I know," he said. "That's the worst part. I never even had to ask."

"Next time," said Elizabeth, "ask."

He looked up at her.

"Next time," he said, slowly, as though he were writing it down. "All right."

"And I'll say no, sometimes."

"Yes." Something happened at the corner of his mouth. "I've noticed you've started doing that."

"It's going very well," said Elizabeth.




There were cookies left from the morning. She'd made another batch. She'd been making them every Saturday. She'd got the egg out, this time, and it was better without it, Nana had been right, which Nana always was.

She put the tin on the table and took the lid off.

He looked at them. Stars and hearts with a thin burned line round every edge. One horse, which hadn't come out properly.

"Edges or middles?" she said.

He looked at the tin for a long time.

"I don't know," he said. "I've never had one."

"Never?"

"Not a whole one. I've had a page of one." He looked at the gray page on the table. "I don't know which I am."

"Then you have to try both," said Elizabeth. "That's the rule. Nana's rule. Nobody gets to say until they've tried both."

So he did. He took a heart and broke it, carefully, the way Kevin had held his crayons, and tried the edge, and then the middle, and sat there with half of each in his hands, chewing, with an expression of total concentration.

"Well?" she said.

He swallowed.

"I think I'm the horse," he said.

She laughed so hard she had to hold her shoulder. He sat there and watched her, with a bit of burned edge in one hand and a bit of middle in the other, and he didn't look as though he'd made a joke. He looked like somebody who'd told the truth and found out, to his amazement, that it was funny.




Afterward, she picked up the gray page.

She held it in both hands. It was very light. It didn't feel like anything. It didn't smell of Nana's kitchen on a Sunday. It just felt like paper.

She got up and found a pen.

She sat down again with the page on the table and wrote on it. She wrote slowly, in her own writing, which was not Nana's writing and never would be, and she didn't try to make it be. SUGAR COOKIES. Two cups. One cup. A pinch, which she underlined twice, because a pinch was a pinch, whatever anybody said. No egg. She crossed the egg out, so whoever came after would know somebody had tried it.

At the bottom, she wrote: Lizzy likes the edges.

And under that: Mrs. Okafor likes the middles.

And under that, after a moment: P. is the horse.

Pathwell watched her do it. He didn't say anything at all. When she'd finished, he looked at the page, and then at her, and his eyes had gone bright, and he looked away out of the window quickly, as if there were something very interesting going on in the street.

"It goes back in the book," said Elizabeth. "At Camp. It's Camp's book. It should have all its pages."

"Yes," said Pathwell.

"I'm going to take it. Today." She folded the page once, carefully, along the old fold, and put it inside her coat. "Will you come?"

He turned from the window.

"Are you asking?"

"I'm asking."

He looked at her for a long moment, across the kitchen table, across the tin of cookies, across Shade's letter with its four answers.

"Yes," said Pathwell. "I'd like that very much."




She put her boots on in the hall. Mama Baga's boots, the girlhood ones. She did up the laces two-handed, slowly, and he stood by the door and didn't help.

She got her keys. She looked round the flat. The boxes were gone. The rug still had its little scorch marks, like spilled fat. Shade's two letters were on the kitchen table, side by side, squared to the edge.

On the windowsill, in its jar, the wooden spoon stood up straight, the way it always did. Spoons are very reliable. It would be there when she got back.

She opened the door.

On the landing, Mrs. Okafor's door opened an inch, and then shut again, very quietly, which was the most tactful thing Elizabeth had ever seen anybody do.

She turned round in the doorway and looked at him. At Pathwell, in his too-clean coat, in her hall, waiting to be told.

"Ready?" said Elizabeth.

He thought about it. Properly. She watched him do it.

"No," he said.

"Perfect," said Elizabeth, and locked the door behind them.
