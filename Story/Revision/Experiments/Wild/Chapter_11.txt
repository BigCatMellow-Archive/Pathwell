Eleven: A Bear in a Hat

They walked through what was left of the night, along a lane between hedges, the three of them, not saying much. Stansbury carried his bag of wet books. Pathwell walked with his arm round his ribs. Elizabeth walked between them in her own dry clothes and Mama Baga's boots, and the boots were the only part of her that wasn't tired.

The sky went gray, and then pale, and the birds started, all at once, as if somebody had given them a signal.

They came into the town from the other end, by the bakery, which was open, with its lights on and the smell of bread coming out of its door into the street like something you could hold. Stansbury went in without a word and came out with three rolls, still too hot to hold properly, and gave them one each.

They ate them walking. It was the best thing Elizabeth had ever eaten.

The library was at the end of the main street, low and brick, with its PUBLIC LIBRARY sign and its paper banner about story hour. It wasn't open. It wouldn't be open for hours.

There was someone sitting on the steps.

Shade was sitting on the top step, with his back against the library door and his long coat buttoned to the neck and his hands in his pockets, watching the street. He looked as if he'd been there all night. He looked as though he might have been waiting for a bus.

He saw them. He didn't get up.

Pathwell stopped at the bottom of the steps. Stansbury stopped beside him. Elizabeth went on, up two steps, and stopped.

"Morning," said Shade.

"You're on my steps," said Stansbury.

"They're the library's steps," said Shade. "I'm a member." He took the white card out of his coat and held it up, so Stansbury could see it from the bottom. "It's my address."

Stansbury looked at the card. At the owl. At SHADE in biro, in careful capitals.

Something happened to his face.

"Who issued that?"

"A woman at the desk. Gray hair, very short. Glasses on a chain. She was kind."

"Mrs. Patel," said Stansbury, faintly.

"Was it the wrong thing to do?"

Stansbury didn't answer for a moment. Then he said, "No. No, it's—you're entitled. Anyone in the county is entitled. You're entitled to six books at a time and two DVDs."

"I haven't taken any out," said Shade. "I didn't know which ones were mine."

Pathwell, at the bottom of the steps, hadn't said anything at all. His right hand was in his coat pocket. Elizabeth could see the pocket moving.

"I'd like to go to Camp," said Shade, to her. "If you're going. I'd like to see it. It's in here." He touched his temple. "The fire in the middle. The wagons. A woman in a red shawl. I'd like to see if it's the same."

"Dot's coming," said Elizabeth. "With the truck. She'll take us."

"He's going to say no," said Shade, looking at Pathwell.

"He doesn't get to," said Elizabeth.

"That's not ideal," said Pathwell, from the bottom of the steps.

She turned round and looked at him.

He shut his eyes. "I know. I heard it as it came out."

Shade stood up.

He meant to come down the steps. Elizabeth could see that. He stood up, and took his hand out of his pocket to hold the rail, and the hand went.

It went the way it had never gone before. It went all at once, out and down, toward Pathwell at the bottom of the steps, so hard it pulled his arm out straight and his shoulder after it, and his whole body went after his shoulder, off balance, off the top step, toward her.

She caught him.

She didn't decide to. You don't decide that. Somebody was falling down the steps toward her and she put her arms out and took his weight, the way she'd taken Nana's a hundred times on the stairs at home, at the knees, with her back straight. She got him. He grabbed at her to stop himself, and what he got hold of was her arm, her left arm, hard, above the elbow.

And from the bottom of the steps, for half a second, it looked like what it looked like.

Shade, throwing himself down the steps at her. His fingers dug into her arm. Her, dragged half off her feet.

"I've got him," said Elizabeth. "It's all right. He fell. I've got him."

Pathwell's hand was already out of his pocket. It had nothing in it. He looked round, fast, for something, anything, and there was nothing, he'd given it all away, and then he looked past her, past Shade, at the library door. At the glass panel in the door. At the inside of the glass panel, where someone had taped up a child's drawing so you could see it from the street.

A bear, in a hat. In crayon. With KEVIN at the bottom.

"No," said Stansbury.

Pathwell went up the steps past her, two at a time, round his ribs, and put his hand flat on the glass, over the bear.

"No," said Elizabeth. "Don't. I don't need—"

He wasn't listening. He was looking at Shade. Only at Shade.

"I don't need to be rescued," said Elizabeth, as loud as she'd ever said anything. "Pathwell. I'm standing right here. I've got him. He fell. I don't need you to rescue me."

"Let go of her," said Pathwell to Shade.

"He's not holding me. I'm holding him."

"Let go of her."

"Pathwell!" That was Stansbury, coming up the steps. "That's not yours. That's not even the right kind—that's a child's, it's pride, it won't do what you want, it's not made for—"

The bear lit up behind the glass.

It lit gold, from the crayon, all of it, every wobbly line, and it was pride. Elizabeth felt it go through her like warm water, a small boy's pride, enormous, total. This is my bear. He has a hat. I drew him. Look.

It wasn't made for pushing anybody away from anybody. There wasn't any of that in it at all.

Pathwell pushed anyway.

And it went wrong.

It didn't push Shade. It didn't push anything the way he meant. It went out of the glass in every direction at once, a great flat wave of a little boy's pride with nowhere to go, and the glass panel in the door cracked across from corner to corner, and then every window along the front of the library went at once, outward, into the street, in a glittering sheet. The book-return slot by the door burst open and shot books out across the pavement like a fruit machine paying out. The paper banner for story hour tore off the wall and went up into the air and came down slowly over the street like a flag.

And the steps moved.

Elizabeth was holding Shade. She felt the step go out from under her heel, she felt the whole of the weight go wrong, and she couldn't let go of him and she couldn't keep her feet, and they went down together, the two of them, sideways, down the library steps, and her left shoulder hit the edge of the bottom step with the whole of both of them on top of it.

She heard it before she felt it. A deep wet click, inside her.

Then nothing.

Then pain, so white the street went away.

Somebody was making a noise, and it was her.

Stansbury was there, kneeling in the glass on the pavement. He had a hand cupped behind her head and the other resting on her arm above the elbow, not moving it, just keeping it where it was.

"Tell me your name," he said.

"You know my name."

"Tell me."

"Elizabeth."

"Can you move your fingers? That side. Just the fingers."

She moved them. Pain went up the arm like a lit fuse.

"Good," said Stansbury. "That's good. Don't do it again."

Beside her, Shade got up onto his knees. There was a long cut along the palm of his left hand, from the glass. He looked at it with polite interest, as though somebody had handed it to him on a plate. Then he looked at her.

"You didn't let go," he said.

"No."

He looked at her a while longer.

At the top of the steps, Pathwell was standing in front of the library door. In front of the glass panel, which was cracked from corner to corner, and empty. There was nothing behind it now. No bear. No hat. Just a bit of Sellotape on the inside of the glass, where a child's drawing had been, and a faint gray mark on the tape where a crayon had touched.

He stood looking at the place where it had been.

Stansbury got up off his knees, slowly. He went up the steps, past his brother, and looked at the empty place behind the glass. He was quiet so long that a pigeon landed on the railing, thought better of it, and left.

"Kevin," he said, at last. Just the name.

Pathwell said nothing.

"He drew that on Saturday. He showed it to her." Stansbury nodded at Elizabeth, down on the pavement. "He's going to come in next Saturday and ask where it is."

"I'll—"

"Don't." Stansbury didn't raise his voice. "Don't say you'll pay for it. Don't say you'll replace it. Don't say you'll find him a better one. You can't. It was his bear."

Elizabeth sat up on the pavement, with her arm held against her side.

Something was wrong in the front of her coat. Something warm, much too warm. She got it out with her good hand.

The foam dagger. It had cracked, a long split from the tip right down into the grip, and it was hot, hot in a way foam has no right to be, and it smelled faintly of a fuse.

If one cracks, Stansbury had said. If it gets warm. Stop using it.

She held it for a moment. Then she put it back in her coat, because there wasn't anywhere else to put it, and she only had one hand.

Down the end of the street, an engine was coming. A very old one, and a very loud one, driven like a drum kit.

The green truck came round the corner and stopped outside the library with a bang, among the glass and the books and the torn paper banner. Dot looked out of the window at the windows. At the books all over the pavement. At Elizabeth on the ground, holding her arm.

"What," said Dot, "did he do?"

Nobody answered her.

Shade got up. He went round the back of the truck and let down the tailgate and climbed up into the back among the sacks of flour, and then he turned round, and knelt, and held down his good hand to Elizabeth.

She looked at it.

She looked up the steps at Pathwell, standing in front of the empty glass. He had no instructions for her. He had nothing at all.

"I chose Camp," she said, up the steps, to him. "I'm still choosing it."

He looked down at her.

"I know," he said.

She took Shade's hand. He pulled her up into the back of the truck, carefully, by her good arm, among the flour. She sat down on a sack.

Stansbury came down the steps. He put his bag of wet library books into the back of the truck, beside her, without a word. Then he went back up the steps to his brother, and stood next to him, in front of the empty glass, and took out his keys, and unlocked the library door.

"Dot," said Stansbury, not turning round. "Take them to Mama. Then come back for us."

"What about you?"

"We're going to sweep up," said Stansbury. "And then we're going to telephone Mrs. Patel. And then he is going to tell her what he did. All of it. In order. Without the clever bits."

Dot looked at Pathwell, at the top of the steps, in his too-short coat.

"Rather you than me," she said, and put the truck in gear.
