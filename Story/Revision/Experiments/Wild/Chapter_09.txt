Chapter Nine

The launderette behind the church was called SUDS, in green letters, and one of the S's had fallen off and been propped up against the window from the inside, so that it was also, in a way, still there.

It was warm. It was very warm, and it smelled of hot cotton and soap powder, and four of the big dryers along the back wall were going round on their own with nobody's washing in them, which Shade said was how they kept the place warm at night. A sign on the wall said PLEASE DO NOT LEAVE CHILDREN OR PETS UNATTENDED, and under it, in biro, somebody had written OR HUSBANDS.

There was a lost property box by the door. A big cardboard one, overflowing. Socks. A scarf. Three single gloves that didn't match. A Christmas jumper, very large, red, with a reindeer on it whose nose had once lit up.

Elizabeth stood in the middle of the floor and dripped.

"You can't sit in that," said Shade. "You'll get ill."

"I haven't got anything else."

He looked at the lost property box. Then at her.

"No," said Elizabeth.

"It's clean," said Shade. "It's a launderette. Everything in here is clean. That's what it's for."

So she went into the little toilet at the back and took off her wet coat and her wet pajamas and put on the Christmas jumper, which came down to her knees, and a pair of somebody's tracksuit bottoms that were too long and had to be rolled four times, and two odd socks, one striped and one with cartoon cats on it. She put her diary and the foam dagger into the front of the jumper, where there was a pocket, a big one, the kind for keeping your hands warm in, and came out.

Shade looked her over, from the wet hair down.

"Good," he said.

"I look ridiculous."

"You look dry."

He'd put money in one of the dryers for her wet things. He'd counted it out from a little purse, exactly, without having to think about it, coin by coin. Then he sat down on the bench in front of the dryer, a long wooden bench bolted to the floor, and watched her clothes go round.

She sat down at the other end of the bench.

They watched her clothes go round.

"I like this," said Shade, after a while.

"The launderette?"

"Watching it go round." He didn't look away from the window of the dryer. "Everything else in my head was his before it was mine. Everything I know how to do. Every place I've been, I'd been before I got there. But he never did this." He nodded at the dryer. "Not once. There's nothing in here about this. Nothing about sitting and watching your things go round and get dry." He was quiet a moment. "So this is mine."

Her coat went past the window, and then her pajamas, and then her coat again.

"How long?" said Elizabeth. "Since you—started?"

"Five weeks. And two days."

"Where have you been?"

"Walking. Mostly. The other way, as long as I can, until I can't. Then the right way for a while, until I can walk the other way again." He looked down at his right hand, lying on his knee. It was very still. It was being held very still. "I joined a library," he said. "In a town. The second week."

"A library?"

He reached into his coat and brought something out and held it out to her. A library card. Plastic, white, a little bent at one corner. STARLING COUNTY LIBRARIES, it said, with an owl reading a book. And under that, in biro, in the space for the name, in small careful capitals:

SHADE.

"They asked for an address," he said. "I didn't have one. The woman behind the desk was very kind. She put the library's address. She said, well, you're here now, aren't you." He took the card back, and looked at it, and put it away in his coat, on the left side. "It's the only thing anywhere with my name on it. That I wrote. Not him."

She thought about the owl. The library van, nose down in the duck pond. Stansbury, reading about a bear to Kevin.

She didn't say anything about it.

"Why do you keep saying sorry?" said Shade.

She looked at him.

"You said it to the ducks," he said. "When you got out of the water. Under your breath. And to the dryer, when you bumped it. And to the woman in the jumper on the wall."

"There's no woman in a jumper on the wall."

"There's a picture of one." He pointed. A faded poster on the wall, for a brand of washing powder, with a woman smiling in a very white jumper. "You said sorry when you walked in front of her."

Elizabeth looked at the poster.

"I don't know," she said. "It's just—you say it. In case. Before anybody can be cross. My Nana used to say I'd apologize to a door for walking through it."

"Did you?"

"Probably."

Shade thought about this.

"He doesn't," he said. "Pathwell. Never. Not in anything in here." He touched his temple, once. "Not one time, in all of it, that I can find. I looked. When I was walking. I went through it all looking for it."

"He's said it to me," said Elizabeth. "Tonight. Lots of times."

Shade turned his head and looked at her, for the first time properly, the way you'd look at someone who had told you the sea was green.

"Then that's new," he said. "That's since."




Her coat went round. Her pajamas went round.

"One of those things came through my door," she said. "At two in the morning. When he was in my living room, reading my diary. And another one, today, in a car park. It went straight for him. It ate him. I had to cut him out."

Shade's hand moved on his knee. "You cut him out."

"With this." She took the foam dagger out of the front of the jumper.

He looked at the dagger. Then at her, in the reindeer jumper, holding it.

"Good," he said.

"That's all?"

He thought about it. "He's out. He's breathing." He went back over it, checking. "Then that's all right."

"He says the first one came for me."

"Why would it?"

"It was my door."

"With him behind it," said Shade.

She didn't answer.

"I don't know what brings them," said Shade. "I only know what brings me. And it isn't you. It never was you. I didn't know you were in the world until you were standing in a pond." He looked at his hand. "Whatever went to your door went where he was. Where I was coming. You were just the one who lived there."

Elizabeth didn't move on the bench.

"So I wasn't—" She stopped. Started again. "He took me out of my home. He made me run, at two in the morning, in my pajamas, and I lost my shoe, and I went through a wall, and I gave away my grandmother's book, because I thought—" She stopped again. "Because he looked at me and said it came for you. Like I was something. Like I mattered enough for something to come for me."

Shade watched her coat go round.

"You were never the point," he said.

He didn't say it unkindly. He didn't say it kindly, either. He said it the way he'd said Wren, like a thing that was just true.

It went into her a long way.

She sat in a stranger's Christmas jumper in a launderette at the edge of a village she didn't know the name of, and let it.

Her coat went round. Then her pajamas.

After a while she found she was all right. Not good. But all right. She found something underneath the hurt that was almost like being put down after being carried a long way. If she wasn't the point, nobody had to come for her. She could just be a woman in a launderette.

"Then what was?" she said. "The point."

"Him," said Shade. "Me. Both of us. We're too close together. Like two people with the same name in the phone book. The things that come can't tell which of us they want."

"What did he do to you?"

Shade was quiet for so long that she thought he wasn't going to answer.

"There's a kitchen," he said at last. "That's all I've got. The beginning of it. A kitchen with a blue oilcloth on the table, and a window, and the sea outside the window, gray. An old woman sitting at the table with her hands round a cup of tea she isn't drinking. Wren. And him, sitting across from her, being certain."

"Certain about what?"

"I don't know. I only have the feeling. Not that he was right. That he'd stopped caring whether he was. That he was going to sort it out, and that was the end of the question." Shade looked at his hand. "Then nothing. Then me, on a road, five weeks ago, walking."

The dryer stopped. Her clothes lay in a heap at the bottom of the drum, warm.

Neither of them got up.

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because he looks at you," said Shade, "when you talk. I watched him, on the green. He looks at you as if he's listening." He paused. "He looks at me like I'm a stain on a good shirt."

"And what am I supposed to do with that?"

"Don't ask me what he did. It isn't mine to give you. I've only got the edge of it." Shade looked at her straight. "He's got the rest. He knows what he did. Ask him."

His right hand came off his knee.

It came up, on its own, and turned, palm out, toward the window, toward the street. It shook in the air. He let it, for a moment, and watched it, as though it were a weathervane.

"They're coming," he said. "Closer. He's looking for you."

"How?"

"I don't know. He'll have a way. He always has a way." He took hold of the hand and brought it down. "I'll go. I'm not ready to be in a room with him."

He stood up.

"Wait," said Elizabeth. "Where will you go?"

"The other way. For as long as I can." He looked round the launderette. At the dryers going round with nothing in them. At the poster of the woman in the white jumper. "Maybe I'll find another one of these."

He went to the door.

"Shade."

He stopped.

She didn't know what she'd been going to say. Then she did.

"Thank you," said Elizabeth. "For the dryer."

He looked at her for a moment.

"You didn't say sorry," he said.

"No."

He nodded, once, as though he'd been told something very good, and went out into the dark. The bell on the door rang behind him. Through the window she watched him go up the street, quick, in his long buttoned coat, with his left hand holding his right hand down, like a man walking a dog that wanted to go the other way.

She got her clothes out of the dryer. They were warm. She held them against her chest a moment, in a heap, before she put them on.
