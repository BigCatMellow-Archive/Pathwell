Chapter Thirteen

She slept most of the day in the healer's wagon, on a camp bed under the window, and when she woke up it was afternoon and Tamsin's baby was looking at her.

Tamsin was asleep. The baby wasn't. The baby was lying across her mother's chest with its chin on its fists, looking at Elizabeth across the wagon with the flat, total attention of someone who has only been alive for three days and has decided to take it seriously.

"Hello," whispered Elizabeth.

The baby went on looking.

"I know," said Elizabeth. "Me neither."

Her shoulder had gone from a scream to a sulk. She lay and listened to Camp outside. Somebody chopping wood. Somebody singing the same two lines of a song over and over, as if they couldn't remember the rest and weren't going to let that stop them. A dog. Dot's voice, very loud, telling somebody they'd put the flour in the wrong wagon, and somebody else saying it was the right wagon, and Dot saying it was the right wagon yesterday.

Then the green truck's engine, going away, toward the town.

She lay there and thought about what it would be like to go back. Not now. Sometime. The flat, with the boxes. The welcome card from Mrs. Okafor on the windowsill, next to the jar with the spoon in it. Work on Monday. Somebody at work saying, "Good weekend?"

She found that she was smiling at the roof of the wagon.

She also found she didn't know what day it was. She'd lost count somewhere around the pond.

"What day is it?" she said, out loud.

"Sunday," said Tamsin, without opening her eyes. "I think. Ask Dot. Dot keeps the days. Nobody else can be bothered."

Sunday. Work tomorrow. Elizabeth lay and waited to feel worried about it, and the worry didn't come. It was as if she'd left it in the pond with the van.




Shade was where she'd left him, more or less. He'd finished the potatoes. Somebody had given him onions, and he'd done the onions, and somebody had given him a basket of beans to top and tail, and he'd done those, and now he was sitting by the fire doing nothing at all, with his hands in his lap, watching the pot.

His right hand was tied to his belt.

He'd done it with a bootlace, through a belt loop, round his wrist, in a neat knot she was fairly sure was one of the ones nobody had shown him.

He saw her looking.

"It pulls more here," he said. "He was here, a lot. When he was young. It's all through the place." He looked at the knot. "And he's coming. With Dot. I can feel him coming. It's worse when he's coming."

"Does it hurt?"

He considered this. "It's like being on the wrong end of a dog. All day."

She sat down on the log. Not too close.

"I don't have to be here," said Shade. "When he comes. I can go into the wood."

"Do you want to go into the wood?"

He thought about it.

"No," he said. "I want to stay by the pot. Mama said I could watch it. Nobody's ever said I could watch something."

"Then stay by the pot."

"He'll think I'm here for him."

"Let him think it," said Elizabeth.

Shade looked at her sideways.

"You sound different," he said. "From the pond."

"I've had a sleep."

"No," said Shade. "That isn't it."




The truck came back at five, driven like a drum kit, and stopped in the middle of the ring.

Stansbury got out of the passenger side, slowly, and stood for a moment in the late sun with his eyes shut. He looked like a man who'd swept up a great deal of glass.

Pathwell got out of the back.

He'd been sitting on the flour sacks. There was flour all down one side of his borrowed coat and in his hair. He stood beside the truck and he didn't look at the fire, where Shade was sitting tied to his own belt. He looked at the wagons, one after another, as if he were counting them. As if they might have moved.

Then he looked at Elizabeth, in her sling.

He came across the ring.

He stopped in front of her.

"How's the arm?" he said.

"Ask the healer," said Elizabeth. "She's the one who knows."

He opened his mouth. Shut it again.

"That's fair," he said.

"It wasn't meant to be fair. I just don't know. It's out of the socket and then it's in again, and it hurts, and I'm not supposed to use it for anything, and I don't know what that means for how it'll be." She looked at him. "So ask her."

He looked round. The healer was sitting on the step of her wagon with a cup of tea, watching all this with no expression at all.

Pathwell went over to her. Elizabeth watched him go. She watched him stand in front of the healer's wagon in his floury coat, and say something, and the healer say something back, quite a lot of something, and him stand and listen to all of it, nodding, without once interrupting.

When he came back he said, "Six weeks. And you're to have it looked at by a proper doctor when you get home, with a machine. And she says you'll have a shoulder that tells you about the weather."

"Did you say sorry to her?"

"Why would I say sorry to her?"

"Did you?"

He looked at her. "Yes," he said. "I did. She said it wasn't her shoulder."




Mrs. Patel, said Stansbury, had been very calm on the telephone.

He told it at the fire, after supper, with his hands round a mug of Mama Baga's coffee, which was black and thick and tasted like a tarred road, and which everybody at Camp drank as if it were a test of character.

"She came down," said Stansbury. "In her coat, over her nightie. She stood on the pavement and looked at the windows. And then she looked at my brother. And then she said, 'Well. You'd better tell me.'"

"And did he?"

"He did. In order. All of it. Without the clever bits." Stansbury drank his coffee. "It took forty minutes. She didn't interrupt once. When he'd finished, she went into the office and came out with a broom and a dustpan and gave them to him. She said, 'The insurance will want a story. I'll give them one. It will be a shorter story than that.'"

"What about Kevin?"

Stansbury didn't answer straight away.

Across the fire, Pathwell was sitting on an upturned crate, as far from Shade as the ring went. He was holding his coffee and not drinking it. He was looking at the fire.

"She said Kevin comes in at ten on Saturdays," said Stansbury. "With his nan. She said she wasn't going to tell him. She said she was going to leave that to whoever had done it."

The fire cracked.

"That's six days," said Elizabeth.

"That's six days," said Stansbury.

Pathwell said nothing at all.

On the other side of the fire, Shade's right hand pulled against its bootlace, toward Pathwell, again and again, like a dog that has seen a cat.




After supper there were dominoes.

Elizabeth hadn't known Camp played dominoes. It turned out Camp played dominoes the way some families pray. There was an old wooden box of them, worn round at the corners, and a board balanced on two crates by the fire, and four people played at a time while everybody else stood round and told them what they were doing wrong.

Dot cheated. Everybody knew Dot cheated. It was part of the game.

"Can I?" said Shade.

They looked at him. The man tied to his own belt.

"Do you know how?" said Mama Baga.

Shade started to say yes. Elizabeth saw him start to say it. Then he stopped, and went inside himself a moment, looking.

"No," he said, slowly. "There isn't any. He watched people play. A lot. Here. But he never sat down." He looked at the board. "He never played anything he might lose."

Across the fire, Pathwell looked up.

"Then sit down," said Mama Baga. "Dot'll teach you. Dot'll teach you wrong, but she'll teach you."

He sat down. He played with his left hand, because his right was tied. He lost the first game, and then the second, and the third very badly, and every time he lost, he looked down at the dominoes left in front of him with an expression Elizabeth had never seen on that face before. Not on either version of it.

He looked delighted.

"I lost," he told her, after the third game.

"You did."

"I lost," he said again, to Mama Baga, and Mama Baga said, "You did, love," and Dot said, "You lost because you're rubbish," and Shade said, "Yes," and looked so pleased about it that Dot started laughing and couldn't stop.

Elizabeth looked across the fire.

Pathwell was watching Shade play dominoes, and his face was doing something it didn't know how to do. He looked like someone standing outside a lit window in the cold.

She got up. She went round the fire, with her coffee in her good hand, and sat on the ground by his crate. Not too close.

"You could play," she said.

"I don't play."

"I know. He told us."

Pathwell looked at the fire.

"I'd lose," he said.

"Yes," said Elizabeth. "That's the game. Somebody loses."

He didn't answer for a long time. Behind them Dot was shouting that it was her double six, she'd had it all along, she'd been saving it.

"It's six days," said Pathwell at last, very quietly.

"I know."

"I don't know what to say to him. To a boy. About a bear." His thumb pressed into his palm. "I've talked my way into and out of every kind of room there is. Courts. Kitchens. Once a coronation. I don't know what to say to a six-year-old about a bear."

"Seven," said Elizabeth. "He told me. He's seven and a quarter."

"Seven and a quarter," said Pathwell, as though that made it a great deal worse, which it did.

She drank her coffee. It tasted like a road.

"Maybe you don't say anything first," she said. "Maybe you let him."

He turned his head and looked at her.

And that was when the dog started barking.




It was Camp's dog, an old black-and-white thing with a gray muzzle that had been asleep under the healer's wagon all evening. It came out from under the wagon backward, barking, with every hair on its back standing up, and it barked at the dark between the trees.

Dot stopped shouting about her double six.

Shade had gone very still at the board, with a domino in his left hand. His right hand wasn't pulling toward Pathwell anymore. For the first time all day, it had stopped. It was pointing at the trees. The same way as the dog.

"Oh," said Shade.

Pathwell was on his feet.

Out in the dark, between the trees, something was coming. Elizabeth could hear it. Not footsteps. A sound like a wet sheet being dragged across a floor, a long way off, and then not so far off.

"Mama," said Stansbury, very calmly, putting down his coffee.

"I hear it," said Mama Baga.

"It's both of us," said Shade. He was standing too now, by the dominoes. "Here. In one place. All day." He looked across the fire at Pathwell, and for once there was no shut door in his face at all. "I'm sorry. I should have gone into the wood."

"No," said Elizabeth.

The dog went on barking. The sound in the trees came on, wet and slow and getting bigger.

Behind her, in the healer's wagon, the baby woke up and began to cry.
