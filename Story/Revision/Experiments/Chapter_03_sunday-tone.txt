Chapter Three

She found him staring up at a streetlight, Nana's cookbook tucked under one arm and her diary under the other.

"So then," he said, without looking down from the light, "where to?"

"I'm not going anywhere without getting some questions answered."

"Sounds good, Lizzy."

"Don't call me that."

"Isn't that what your grandmother called you?"

"That's… that's not an answer. And don't think for a second I forgot you still have her cookbook."

"You'll get them back at the counter."

"What counter?"

"Lizzy, I can answer all your questions, even the ones you'll have next. We just need to swing by a bookstore first."

"What kind of bookstore is going to be open this late?"

Pathwell turned. "The right kind."

He shifted the diary and cookbook under one arm, pulled a folded piece of paper from his pocket with the other, and pressed it flat against the concrete wall behind her.

Light bled through the creases, spiderwebbing along the wall. A soft square, no bigger than the paper itself, glowing against the grey. He peeled back a layer and the square doubled. Another layer, and it doubled again. The light stretched past the edges of the paper until a doorway stood where the wall had been, warm and humming faintly.

"What is that?"

"Like I said, a bookstore. We really do need to work on those listening ears, Lizzy. Now, don't be shy. They have the most amazing coffee."

"How do I know this isn't some kind of trap?"

"You don't."

Pathwell stepped through and turned left.

She stood on the sidewalk, staring at a door that shouldn't exist. At rows of books stacked floor to ceiling. From somewhere inside, a loose page drifted out, spinning once in the air before settling at her feet. The handwriting was faded, but the ink smelled like lavender.

Then the smell of the place reached her. Old paper and dried leather, binding glue gone brittle, and beneath it all, dust that had been settling longer than she'd been alive.

He still had her books.

She stepped through.

The street noise cut out like someone had closed a window. No cars, no sirens, no wind. Just the soft creak of shelves bearing more weight than they were built for, and a silence that felt like it had been here practicing for years.

Books. Floor to ceiling, wall to wall, stacked and leaning and wedged into every surface the room could offer. Not books — lives. Journals with cracked spines, letters bundled in ribbon, recipes on index cards spotted with oil. Thousands of them, maybe more, pressed together so tightly the shelves bowed under the effort.

"Lizzy, would you mind closing the door?"

She turned. Pathwell was already halfway down an aisle, moving like he knew the place by feel. She pulled the door shut and the frame dissolved into the wall behind her. Just shelves now. Shelves in every direction.

"Pathwell?"

He was gone.

"Pathwell!"

A shush from somewhere deep between the rows.

She moved forward because standing still felt worse. The first aisle was fine — dusty, cramped, unremarkable. But as she turned a corner, her chest tightened. Not fear exactly. Something heavier, pressing against the inside of her ribs like a fist she couldn't unclench. Her eyes burned. She blinked hard and kept walking.

It passed. Two steps later her skin flushed warm and her pulse dropped and she felt, for no reason at all, like she was about to cry over something she couldn't name. She stopped and reached toward the nearest shelf. The spine of a journal was warm to the touch.

She pulled her hand back.

The next turn hit her like a door slamming. Her heart punched against the base of her chest, her mind catching on a single thought and looping it — where am I, where am I, where am I — her fingers going numb at the tips, her lungs reaching for a breath that wouldn't come. She braced herself against a shelf and the books there seemed to lean toward her, pressing closer, the aisle narrowing with each exhale she couldn't quite finish.

Grey crept into the edges of her vision. The last thing she registered was the carpet against her knees.

When her eyes opened, they were heavy. She was in a chair, her feet up on an ottoman. A thin orange cat sat in front of her, green-eyed and waiting.

A cup of coffee swung into view. "Just in time." A plain white mug on a plain white saucer.

She took it by instinct and sipped. It was awful. Reused filters, dirty pots, dollar-store beans. It tasted like late nights spent studying. Like the past.

"That's terrible."

"I know."

"Why does it help?"

"Familiar things are useful."

The room came into focus. A large oaken counter, a round man in round-rimmed glasses behind it, and dim lights that weren't trying hard enough. Nana's cookbook and her diary sat on the counter beside a ledger.

The cat rubbed its head against her shins. She reached down and the skin on the back of her hand split open. Fast, surgical, a single claw dragged through the soft part between her thumb and wrist. She yanked back. Blood welled up in a thin line.

The cat's eyes gleamed as it licked its chops.

"He wanted his tribute," the man behind the counter said. "I would let him have it, personally."

Her eyes darted back and forth, looking for the punchline.

"Hey, it's okay," Pathwell said. "You're fine."

He snapped a strip of paper from his pocket and laid it across the cut. Gripping it firmly, he whispered a few words. The paper glowed warm and faded into her hand. The wound and the pain went with it, leaving behind a lingering feeling that reminded her of a boy she knew in middle school.

"Love note," he said, and smiled. "Cures most wounds."

He rubbed his thumb over the thin line it had left behind. "Unfortunately, scars are outside my wheelhouse. How about this. You finish that coffee there, and I'll answer any questions. Deal?"

She gulped the coffee down.

"I'll take that as a yes." The chair beside hers huffed as Pathwell flopped into it. "Right, so you're wondering where we are. The truth is, we're in a bookstore, commonly referred to as the Space Between. As for Boots there, he did indeed sample the blood from your cut. He's the full-time guardian and part-time blood god."

Boots had already jumped onto the counter. The shopkeeper rubbed him behind the ears. "We pay him in mice."

"A blood god?"

"Neither here nor there. What's important is where we're going."

"Where are we going?"

Pathwell clapped his hands. "Glad you asked."

He got up and went to the counter. Elizabeth put the mug down and followed him. She reached for Nana's cookbook.

The shopkeeper put one finger on it.

"His transaction first."

She looked at Pathwell. "You said I'd get them back at the counter."

"And look," Pathwell said. "A counter."

"That's not the same thing."

"It's alarmingly close."

The shopkeeper looked at Pathwell over his glasses. "Yours?"

"No."

"Good." He didn't move his finger. "After settlement. He brought outside property into an open transaction, and I dislike paperwork."

He went back to his ledger, making ticks and carrying ones.

"So what do we owe?" Pathwell said.

Without looking up, the shopkeeper sighed. "You already owe quite a bit."

"I'm good for it."

"All bills come due eventually, Mr. Pathwell."

"What do I owe for today?"

The shopkeeper set the order on the counter and looked it over. Journals, bundles of letters, sheets of poetry, a few pages of music, blank archival paper, and a wooden crate tied with twine. Enough of it that the counter complained.

"Some of these are very old, Mr. Pathwell. They won't come cheap. Did Camp Cunnan ask you for all of this?"

Pathwell became interested in the crate. "They need it."

"That wasn't what I asked."

Pathwell didn't answer.

The register dinged and clacked, numbers jumping. "We could settle today, past bills and all, for… a Rai Stone."

"Oh, come on. Be serious. A Rai Stone?"

"I'm afraid I am." The shopkeeper removed his glasses. "And quite frankly, Mr. Pathwell, you already owe more than I allow most."

"Surely we can barter here."

"Outside of a significant Rai Stone, I could do it for, say, thirty Lydian coins."

"Okay, now you're just messing with me."

The shopkeeper was unmoved.

"Aren't you?"

"Unless you have something else to barter with…"

"I've got potential to sell. Lots."

"You did."

Pathwell chuckled nervously.

Elizabeth looked between them. "What does that mean?"

Neither of them answered her.

"Are you sure?" the shopkeeper said.

"Can we do this before I change my mind?"

The shopkeeper turned to the wall behind him and slid open a panel that had blended into the soft brown paneling. Behind it sat a cast-iron cube. He pulled a short sheet of paper from his sleeve and laid it over the door. His hands moved over it, turning it light blue. His fingers danced. A thunk, a clank, and shifting gears.

A door popped open from nothing.

Inside sat a leather-bound tome, its edges gilded in silver. A dot was branded into the center of the cover, surrounded by concentric rings and lattice arcs. Thick, heavy and dense, with thin pages.

He lifted it with both hands and placed it on the counter with reverence.

"Mr. Pathwell, are you sure this is worth what it will cost?"

"I am."

"Then say the words."

Pathwell stepped to the book and placed his right hand on the cover, his left across his chest. "I understand the cost of my needs."

The silver pages glowed a beautiful blue, like a distant sky.

"I give what may be, to protect what will."

The pages burned bright. Roots of light shot from the book and grasped Pathwell's forearm, wrapping tight, intertwined and wild.

"Perdat quod esse poterat."

They pulsed, turning a violent blue. New roots split off, snaking up his arm and digging their way in. Splintering thinner, then burrowing, twisting, wriggling. Taking hold.

From the center of the book, fibrous tendrils wove upward, gnarled and knotted. The tree grew to Pathwell's chest before splitting and branching out. Some branches grew mangled, withered, dead. The tree bloomed in patches, sick and breathless, and took its full shape, sitting sickly and worn.

The roots pushed deeper into his arm. Whatever the pain was, it had all of him now.

"You don't have much left to give, Mr. Pathwell."

"Take what you will, and spare me the lesson."

Orange flames took hold of the branches, spreading out from around the tree and burning bright against the light blue leaves. The roots burned a frightful orange, searing the arm they wrapped.

Pathwell screamed in a million voices, voices lost to time, burning out to become memories never made. One by one they fell quiet, until all that was left was a harmony of extinguished cries.

The fire burned down, taking all but a few living branches at the core and leaving only scorched fibers reaching out. Pathwell dropped to his knees, his arm still held up by the burrowed roots.

Then the roots let go, and he caught the counter with one hand. Elizabeth was halfway around it before she knew she'd moved.

"Thank you, Mr. Pathwell." The shopkeeper was already writing. "Your bill has been paid in full. Will that be paper or plastic?"

Pathwell looked up. His right arm was shaking, but there wasn't a mark on it.

"Surprise me."

He tucked the arm against his side and pulled himself upright, as though posture had become the problem.

The shopkeeper slid Nana's cookbook and the diary across the counter. "These were never his to spend."

Elizabeth took them. The diary was cool under her hand. The cookbook felt heavier, which was stupid. Missing a page should have made it lighter. Her thumb found the ragged edge where the page had been.

Pathwell saw, and looked away.

"You just sold your potential," she said.

He flexed the shaking hand once. "Some of it."

"Which part?"

"If I knew that, I'd make worse decisions with more confidence."

The shopkeeper pulled a handcart out from behind the counter. It unfolded twice, extended once, and stopped at a size that shouldn't have fit back there in the first place.

Pathwell stared at it. "You had that the whole time?"

"Yes."

"You've watched me carry things out of here by hand."

"Yes."

"For decades."

The shopkeeper set the first bundle on the cart. "Your account is settled now."

Pathwell looked genuinely wounded.

Elizabeth laughed. One short, surprised sound. Both men looked at her.

"Don't make anything of that."

They loaded the cart. Elizabeth held her books against her chest while Pathwell moved the Camp order. He tried the crate one-handed, and his arm shook hard enough that he put it back down. He waited, then used both hands.

When everything was loaded, the shopkeeper walked to a door beside a shelf of ledgers. Elizabeth was sure it hadn't been there before. He opened it, and cold pine air came into the shop. Beyond the threshold were trees and a dirt path, silvered by a moon the city had been hiding.

Pathwell took the cart handle.

Elizabeth looked behind her. There was no city door.

The shopkeeper followed her glance. "If you want the sidewalk, I can return you to the sidewalk."

Pathwell stopped with his back to her.

Elizabeth looked down at the diary and cookbook in her arms. Hers again.

Her apartment still existed. Hotels existed. Police existed, although explaining the front door would be an ambitious project. Her meeting was still out there, getting closer.

Nobody was stopping her.

Pathwell's fingers tapped the cart handle once.

A forest. A place called Camp Cunnan. Stacks of books somebody needed badly enough for Pathwell to leave part of his future on a ledger without being asked.

She could go home.

Instead she stepped up to the cart and put Nana's cookbook on top of the Camp books. Then the diary beside it.

"Don't lose those."

Pathwell looked at the books, then at her. "Wouldn't dream of it."

She put both hands on the side of the cart. "Where are we taking these?"

His smile came back slowly. "Camp Cunnan."

"If you keep saying that like it explains anything, I'm going home."

"Understood."

He pulled the cart through the doorway. Elizabeth went with him.

"Mind the cart on the hill," the shopkeeper called after them. "It pulls left."

Then he closed the door behind them.
