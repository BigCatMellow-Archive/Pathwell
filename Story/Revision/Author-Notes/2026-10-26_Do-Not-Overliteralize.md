## CRITICAL EDITORIAL RULE — Do Not Overliteralize the Author's Writing

One of the central failures identified during earlier revisions was **overliteral editing**: treating expressive, figurative, conversational, or physically unconventional language as defective because its meaning was not literally or mechanically precise.

Your review must actively guard against repeating this mistake.

### 1. Figurative truth is not literal truth

A smell can splatter across a room. A door can close with desperation. A box can season the floor with books.

None of these constructions is automatically wrong because the described action cannot occur literally.

Evaluate what the image communicates, whether readers can understand it, and whether it creates the intended sensation.

**Do not correct a metaphor simply because it is physically impossible.**

An image should be reconsidered when it creates genuine confusion, communicates an unintended meaning, clashes with the scene's emotional purpose, or fails aesthetically—not merely because its literal mechanics are impossible.

### 2. Do not confuse physical blocking with unnecessary choreography

Characters are physical beings. They gesture, hesitate, move, fidget, handle objects, occupy space, and communicate without speaking.

These actions can provide characterization, subtext, humor, rhythm, emotional expression, physical presence, or dramatic timing.

Not every gesture requires a direction or an object.

For example, the author's original sideways half-nod accompanying "Magically speaking" was intended as an almost shrug-like qualification—an unspoken "so to speak."

An earlier revision transformed this into a nod toward residue on the wall. That interpretation was spatially clearer but altered the intended character behavior.

**Greater physical precision is not necessarily greater artistic accuracy.**

Distinguish purposeful expressive movement from repetitive, confusing, or redundant choreography.

Do not remove blocking merely because the dialogue remains understandable without it.

### 3. Pleasure is a legitimate narrative function

A sentence can earn its place by being enjoyable.

It can make a character more endearing, a setting more memorable, a conversation funnier, or an ordinary object more meaningful.

It does not have to reveal information, establish a clue, advance a plot event, or directly develop a theme.

The author's original storytelling often found personality in things other editors might dismiss as unnecessary.

**Do not confuse a sentence being unnecessary for plot comprehension with its being unnecessary for the story.**

Equally, do not preserve genuinely repetitive or unsuccessful prose merely because it is decorative.

### 4. Preserve meaning before improving clarity

Before changing an unusual sentence or gesture, determine:

- What is the author trying to communicate?
- What does the reader experience through this particular wording?
- Is the effect literal, figurative, emotional, humorous, rhythmic, or social?
- Is there actual confusion, or is the wording simply unconventional?
- Would the proposed revision preserve the intended effect?
- Is the replacement merely more conventional, shorter, or more literally precise?

If the intended effect is uncertain, flag the passage for the author instead of confidently rewriting it.

**An editor's plausible interpretation is not automatically the author's intention.**

### 5. Do not turn these safeguards into new rules of excess

This is not a mandate to add metaphors, gestures, jokes, or narrator comments everywhere.

Plain language is valuable. Stillness is valuable. A short sentence can be perfect. Sometimes dialogue needs no accompanying action.

The goal is to protect the author's freedom to use whatever technique serves a particular moment.

Do not impose a minimalist style, but do not impose a whimsical style either.

### 6. Required diagnosis when recommending changes

Whenever you suggest changing a figurative expression, physical gesture, conversational aside, or unusual sentence, explicitly distinguish:

**A. Actual problem:** The line causes demonstrable confusion, inappropriate pacing, repetition, contradictory information, or an unintended effect.

**B. Artistic preference:** The line works, but you personally favor a different style.

**C. Meaning-altering edit:** Your proposed revision changes the character's intention, narrator's attitude, emotional emphasis, humor, or figurative effect.

**D. Genuine improvement:** The revision demonstrably strengthens the passage while preserving its intended purpose.

Do not present category B or C as though it were automatically category D.

### The overarching principle

**Edit the author's expression to make it work better. Do not replace the author's expression with something easier for an editor to justify.**

The goal is not to make every sentence literally correct, maximally efficient, or conventionally polished.

The goal is to understand what the author is accomplishing and improve the experience without sacrificing it.
