## The reviser's response (R26)

### The two big ideas: do both

- **She opens the door and sees the blob (¶23).** Do it. It gives her an act of her own in the first pages. The panel's prose reader stopped believing at "Do you have shoes?" because she only complies (Panel-2026-10-08, minority stopper). It also answers the readers who couldn't tell what the blob was.
  - Keep it a glimpse: she cracks the door, sees something wet, smells low tide, and he slams it shut over her arm.
  - Then rework the break-in reveal (¶79, "It was some sort of blob").
- **Her panic when he uses the cookbook (¶69).** Grabbing the book is her first real choice, and readers called her want unclear (panel, convergent). If the reason is plainly "that's Nana's book and he's destroying it", it reads as a choice.

### Agreed, with a suggestion

- **¶63, more fantastical magic.** Yes, but through her senses rather than adjectives: two or three short beats (the gold rising off the letters, the room's sound dropping away, the door going quiet for a second). That gives slow motion without explanation.
- **¶65, a better low tide.** Add to it, don't replace it. "Low tide, a long way from any sea" is the line readers quoted. The second mention makes it worse, with one specific thing.
- **¶73, more poetic.** Rather than poetic, name the feeling with one real thing from that kitchen.
- **¶33, the box that falls on its own.** "He shoved a box aside" or "He dropped a box".
- **¶39, more detail.** One physical detail: the chain jumping, paint cracking along the frame, or the smell under the door.
- **¶81, worded poorly.** Try "It rolled over the fallen gold and snuffed it out."
- **¶87, cut the clear bright note.** Yes; "The impact sang." alone is stronger.
- **¶125, blocking after "Huh."** Yes. He steps over the ooze or picks up the banner while she watches from the bathroom.
- **¶167, "His attention".** The paragraph before is hers, so "His" reads as hers. Start with "The man looked down the hall." Note that ¶127 and ¶165 both open the bathroom door.
- **¶185 (Ch2), "Will you slow down!?" has no speaker.** Put it in the same paragraph as "Elizabeth struggled to keep up…".
- **¶59, "Good… that's good."** Yes. It saves "Perfect." for the last line, and two panel readers flagged "Perfect." as a repeated button.
- **¶131 and ¶139, the cuts.** Fine. "I'm not going anywhere until you tell me…" still carries her refusal.

### Pushback

- **¶7, the moved paragraph.** "The party was still out here" now comes before she steps out into the room. Use "out there", or let the party paragraph be what she sees as she steps out.
- **¶9, the rewrite.** It loses the contractions the book's voice uses. Suggest: "But she'd never caught his name, and she'd thought he'd left with the rest of them."
- **Mechanics:**
  - "No, not at all" (¶15) needs the comma.
  - ¶77 is now a comma splice; keep "and".
  - In ¶117, "waived" should be "waved", as its own sentence: `…a mess." He waved it off.`
- **¶5, the cut.** The banner and "since she'd started" already say she's new, but the cut loses the light-switch joke. It's the author's call.
- **¶11, finding him on the floor with the boxes already knocked over.** Yes. It fits R27: the crash is the stack going over, and he keeps reading anyway.

### Not covered by the notes

The panel's top gap is why this man is in her flat at all, and "You really don't know?" never gets answered. Chapter 1 is where one hint could go, such as him glancing at the box he was drawn to.

### Strong craft directions, but don't apply mechanically

**¶63 — first magic.** Most valuable expansion candidate. Slow Elizabeth's subjective experience for two or three distinct beats rather than merely increasing scale or adjectives: light and handwritten letters behaving impossibly, ordinary sound falling out of focus, her inability to understand what she's witnessing. Make this moment *impressive to Elizabeth* rather than generically spectacular. Protect the threat advancing at the door; this is not permission for an extended explanation of the magic system.

**¶69 — the cookbook.** Strong direction. Pathwell sees usable material; Elizabeth sees an irreplaceable book with her grandmother's handwriting being damaged by a stranger. Make her immediate panic legible before or through the snatch, without pausing for a retrospective statement of grief. The magic and the physical destruction arrive together.

**¶73 — Nana's memory.** Seek emotionally *specific* rather than simply “more poetic”: a distinct sensation, movement or remembered detail only that kitchen supplies. Avoid abstractly informing readers how deeply she felt; allow the detail to carry it. This is a cautious voice/emotional-category choice for the author under R7.

**¶65 / ¶39 — low tide and extra thud detail.** A specific additional unpleasant sense impression on the *second* low-tide mention could distinguish escalation. One new physical cue at the door may sharpen danger. Do not elaborate each blow: “Wet. Heavy.” and the short thuds are effective because they accelerate pressure.

**¶23 — Elizabeth opening the door and glimpsing the blob.** An interesting *option*, not an automatic upgrade. It gives her a voluntary action early, but may sacrifice suspense and create new blocking/timing problems: how far does she open it, how does Pathwell shut it, why isn't she struck, and what is still left for the later break-in reveal? If tried, compare the original's tension with a very brief glimpse and adjust the later description accordingly. This note is more cautious than R26's unconditional “do both.”

### Push back or reconsider

- **¶9 recognition sentence:** “he'd had left” is ungrammatical; the added clauses make a natural line cumbersome. Prefer the compact contracted form: “But she'd never caught his name, and she'd thought he'd left with the rest.” Preserve what she knows from the welcome party without adding unsupported prior backstory.
- **¶15 “No, not at all”:** prefer the original **“Not at all.”** Its economy makes Pathwell's rude, oblivious answer to “Do you mind?” funnier. If the longer version is retained, it needs “No, not at all.”
- **¶89 “The blob ripped and recoiled”:** “ripped” sounds forceful but has no clear physical referent. Did its surface tear, its shape split or its body snap backward? Retain the violent reaction while making the image perceivable; the original “rippled” is clearer in this respect.
- **¶117 “Oh, that was nothing” + waved it off:** the original understated denial is already funny after the wrecked living room. “Oh” and the gesture risk explaining the joke. The original is preferable unless the gesture also performs specific blocking.

### Mechanical repairs required if the author's wording is used

1. ¶15 add the comma in “No, not at all.”
2. ¶33 give the dropped box a mover and repair the malformed quoted “The old one.”
3. ¶77 the proposed deletion joins two complete clauses with a comma; retain a conjunction or split the sentences. “Dripped” is fine.
4. ¶83 rewrite the movement and strike as a clean active sequence rather than the layered “and … was met sharply with” construction.
5. ¶117 `waived` → `waved` and attach the action as a correctly punctuated new sentence.
6. ¶167 restore unambiguous Pathwell/Elizabeth referents; ¶185 (first line of Chapter 2) give the speaker.

### Overall disposition

**Preserve the cuts and focus the expansion on the first magic and the cookbook.** The recurring strength of these marked-up notes is insisting on Elizabeth's *experienced reality*—what she sees, how a room is arranged, what a stranger does to Nana's book—not a demand for new plot architecture. Resist changes that lengthen already effective deadpan lines, trade precise action for grander but vague verbs, or over-ornament the memory.
