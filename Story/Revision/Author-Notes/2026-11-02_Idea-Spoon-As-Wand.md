## The reviser's thoughts at the time

**Why not in this book as a general rule:**

- *The rule is the book's best idea.* Handwriting holds what the writer felt, and using the page uses it up. Readers in both council rounds named this as the most original thing in the book: the grocery lists, the unsent letters, MORE IF THEY'RE SICK, "The copy keeps the words." / "Not the thing." It works because it's narrow. If every well-used object can be charged, the system loses its edges.
- *The spoon's power is that it's ordinary.* It's the one thing of Nana's that survives. In Ch17 she stirs Sunday dumplings with it and writes the recipe in her own hand. A spoon that becomes a wand would undercut the book's argument that ordinary things matter without being magic. Ch15 makes the same point: "Ordinary flame… because that was what fire did."

**It's closer to the existing canon than it sounds.** Objects can already carry power when someone works them: Stansbury imbues foam weapons with the energy from children's art, and Pathwell's brass hardware does things. What isn't in canon is an object charging itself through use.

**Ways to use it:**

1. **As a joke in this book, with the magic denied.** For example, Milo is sure the spoon is a wand and waves it at something, and nothing happens. Or Stansbury examines it with full collector's seriousness and says "It's a spoon. Keep it." You get the visual, and the spoon keeps what it means.
2. **As a rule for a later book.** Objects are charged only by craft, Stansbury-style imbuing, never by inheritance or use alone. Then the spoon could become a wand someday, but only because someone *made* it one, and that would cost them something, as every working in this world does.
3. **A third option to weigh then:** objects hold *use* rather than *feeling*. A spoon stirred ten thousand times might hold "stirring", which is a direction or a motion (like the grocery lists' "movement in sequence"), not a memory. That keeps the system consistent and could make a funny, very literal wand.
