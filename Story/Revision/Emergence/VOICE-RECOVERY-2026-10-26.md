## What is being audited

1. **Nonliteral language:** personification, transferred actions or emotions, impossible but effective verbs, sensory crossings and comic comparisons.
2. **Oral narration:** loose connective clauses, intentional repetition, unusually long sentences broken by strategic fragments, a narrator with a slight opinion.
3. **Wrong-detail humor:** what Pathwell notices under pressure, absurd bargains, casual underreaction and narrator digressions.
4. **Emotional experience:** body before comprehension, tactile magic, imperfect memory, unspoken grief, humane ordinary details.
5. **Structural gains:** clear staging, cause and effect, viewpoint limits, proportion, character choice and actual plot/payoff decisions. These gains count; recovery must not break them.
6. **Lineage:** separate first draft → intermediate working document → author's August baseline → current manuscript. Identify **when** a choice vanished; never attribute it to an AI revision merely because the current text differs.

## Decision labels (provisional, never automatic changes)

- **RECOVER:** an expressive effect was removed without a compensating gain and still fits present scene and rulings.
- **REWORK:** valuable voice/idea, but exact early words, plot or placement no longer fit; suggest the *effect*, not a compulsory paste.
- **KEEP REVISED:** the later text actually does the job better, or the original's effect already survives.
- **LEAVE ALONE:** plausible alternatives; no sufficiently evidenced net gain from another edit.
- **OPEN:** causal/context/author-taste question; do not resolve by invented prose.

**For each entry**, include historical verbatim quotation, source location, **actual present text**, change in function, gains/losses, provisional disposition, confidence, dependency/lock, and smallest potential intervention. Do not call `RECOVER` a selected patch. A possible replacement that changes narrator voice, metaphor, joke or dialogue remains author-owned under [AGENTS](../../../AGENTS.md).

## Guardrail: figurative language is not a factual mistake

A smell can "splatter" across a room as a way of making its arrival violent and sudden. A door can open "with panic" without the narrator believing doors have feelings. Diagnose **what the transfer does to the reader**, not whether the subject can physically do the verb.

Before flagging an apparent impossibility, distinguish:

- **Causal/staging error:** who moved, what contacted what, where, when, whose knowledge; literally false in a way that misdirects the action.
- **Failed figure:** the intended transferred sensation/attitude is unclear, unintentionally comic, inconsistent with the register, or competing with a stronger nearby image.
- **Density problem:** several individually good figures pile up and blur staging, punchline or emotional focus.
- **Successful strangeness:** a distinctive image compresses otherwise unexpressed meaning; keep even if it is not physically possible.

**Editorial burden of proof:** name the adverse reader effect **in context** before replacing. "An odor doesn't splatter" does not meet that burden. Nor does "more concise" by itself prove better prose. This principle applies to author's own new lines as well as AI-authored passages.
