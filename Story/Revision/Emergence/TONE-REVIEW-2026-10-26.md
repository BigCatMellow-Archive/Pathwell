## 1. What you're trying to do

These are the tone targets, in my words, each traced to its source. They are mostly consistent with each other. Where two notes pull apart, I've said which one wins under your order of authority.

1. **The gap.** A real situation, a slightly wrong response, and both played straight. The narrator never signals that the response is wrong. *"The situation is always real. The response is always slightly wrong."* (tone register; North Star; prose voice §6)
2. **Tonal range, not tonal purity.** The reader moves between *this is fun / this is weird / these people feel real / that was unexpectedly sad / wait, that's an interesting question*. A laugh can sit right beside something serious without making either one fake. (Voice Guide)
3. **The mundane keeps mattering inside the extraordinary.** Dry cleaning, bad coffee, shoes, a damaged car. *"Extraordinary events do not stop ordinary life from mattering."* (Voice Guide; Framework §15)
4. **Irreverence punctures grandeur, never sincerity.** *"The story should never become embarrassed by caring."* (Voice Guide)
5. **Emotion arrives indirectly, through objects; one sad moment, said once.** People may collapse over something trivial. (Voice Guide; Rules; Line-Notes: "Naming the grief makes it smaller")
6. **Theme is discovered, never delivered.** *"If a character says the theme out loud, it's failed"*, and the README extends that to the narrator. (Thesis; README after-every-pass step 1; prose voice "Thesis statement as prose fault")
7. **A narrator with a dry opinion, used sparingly.** Pratchett-adjacent. The narrator is present at arrivals, transitions, aftermath and one-line character observations. It is quiet in action, magic, sensation, Shade's scenes and after a punchline. *"That restraint IS the irreverence."* (narrator register; prose voice)
8. **Mostly clear, invisible prose.** Occasional sentences that stand out because the prose around them gives them room. *"Do not turn every observation into a clever observation."* (Voice Guide)
9. **Each character's humor is their own.** Pathwell: logistics and evasion. Elizabeth: sincere, snapping, small. Stansbury: precision. Mama Baga: care given as orders. Shade: an exact mind meeting an ordinary thing. Camp's minor characters: plain. (character bible; Voice Guide: avoid "everyone sharing the same sense of humor")
10. **Pathwell keeps his madness.** Velocity, delight, physical eccentricity, sudden stillness. His growth is ethical, not temperamental. *"Do not cure Pathwell of being Pathwell… Keep the madman with a box."* His irreverence comes back during restitution; he can joke without using humor to end someone's anger. (North Star)
11. **Elizabeth grows her own irreverence.** Early she endures the comic rhythm, in the middle she interrupts it, and by the end they play. The last exchange is funny as well as meaningful. (North Star; tone register)
12. **A Sunday light touch, with the weight kept.** Warmth, people you enjoy, meals, a soft landing after the hard moment; the reader needn't brace, but the danger and loss are real. (R24; R1 "longer, darker"; README scene diagnostic Q5, which replaces the brace-test)
13. **Don't polish the humanity out.** Some clumsy lines and stupid jokes are fine if they're true. (Voice Guide "Imperfection")
14. **Figurative truth and expressive blocking are yours to use.** A smell can splatter; a sock can surrender; a gesture can mean "so to speak" without pointing at anything. Pleasure is a legitimate function. But no minimalist style and no whimsical style is imposed either. (Do-Not-Overliteralize rule, 2026-10-26; Line-Editing Protocol "purpose, not austerity")

**Two clarifications the notes force on my earlier reviews:**

- **On "British register."** In the Phase 2 review I called V2's dry diction an import foreign to your voice. Your own `pathwell_prose_voice.md` names Pratchett as the closest single-author reference, so a dry, British-tinged irreverence is a legitimate target. The real limits are density and placement: "self-consciously clever narration," "generic whimsical fantasy prose," and attitude used "sparingly." **I'm narrowing that finding:** the problem is *how often and where* the narrator is clever, not that it is.
- **On Shade.** The archived tone register calls Shade "sinister rather than comic." The current character bible supersedes it with "Not a joker… When he's funny, it's because an exact mind meets an ordinary thing." I've used the current version.

### 3.2 One sad moment, said once (target 5)

- **Ch16, the crying litany** ("Elizabeth cried for Nana. / For the cookbook. / For the diary… / For the fact that choosing Milo had been right and had still cost her things she wanted back."). It names every loss, and the last item is the thesis. The Voice Guide wants something small to carry it. *Proposal (structure, not wording):* keep the laugh breaking into crying, keep the litany as short as two or three objects (the grocery lists, the pockets, "the sound of him asking for help"), and cut the last item. The new emotional wording is yours.
  - **Diagnosis:** cutting the last item is **A**, since it's the thesis stated at the emotional peak. Shortening the list is **B, bordering on C**. The length may be the point (crying that won't stop), so that's your call.
- **Ch4, the cookbook given.** The Oct 5 report counted it told four times: the act, the raised hand, "didn't go back for it," and a later mention. Cut the last two echoes; the act and the hand are the scene.
  - **Diagnosis:** **A**, repetition.

### 3.3 One shared deadpan in the minor cast (target 9)

The character bible says Camp and the minor characters should be *plain*, and that "a minor character with a polished one-liner is often borrowing the narrator's voice." The book currently gives almost everyone a polished, compact verdict:

| Character | Polished line | Allowed? |
|---|---|---|
| Archivist | "Specificity is expensive." "That is generally the opposite of an archive." | **Yes.** R33: "the archivist stays the one dry wit." |
| Shopkeeper | "Three weeks is not a character trait." "Accounting." | **Yes.** Your own character ("paper or plastic," "We pay him in mice"). |
| Healer | "Shelves are famous for respecting medical instructions." "Briefly? Very." | **No.** She should be plain and clinical. "Briefly? Very." is borderline; the shelves line is the narrator's voice. |
| Curator | "Paid is not completed." "Useful," … "Not returned." | **Borderline.** Precise is right for a curator; the epigram rhythm is the shared deadpan. |
| Woman with the road notebook | "He'd still argue." "I always bring it back." | **Borderline.** The Oct 5 report asked whether she should be plainer. |
| Bartender | "You complained about the batter then too." | **Fine.** It's a plain fact that happens to be funny. That's the model. |

**Diagnosis:** taken one at a time, each of these lines is **B**. The shelves line, for one, is a pleasure, and pleasure is a legitimate function under your rule. The **A** is only cumulative: together they produce what your Voice Guide lists as a failure, "everyone sharing the same sense of humor." So the fix is not to flatten the minor cast. It's to break the pattern in one or two places so the archivist and shopkeeper stay distinctive.

**How to get there:** leave the archivist and shopkeeper dry. If you agree the pattern is real, pick *one or two* of the healer, the curator and the notebook woman to make plainer, the bartender's kind of funny. Changing one of their lines is **C**, since it changes who she is. Any alternative wording would be a candidate for you, not a fix.

### 3.4 Narrator in the wrong place (targets 7 and 8)

**a) Commentary inside action.** The register says the narrator is quiet in action, magic and Shade's scenes.

- **Ch15:** "There were advantages to children who had spent the morning taking instructions from people carrying turnips." **Withdrawn as a cut.** In context it follows the boy's instant "Okay." It explains *why* he obeys, as a one-line callback to Camp's turnip-carrying children, and the action resumes in the next sentence. It doesn't stall the rescue.
  - **Diagnosis:** at most **B**. It's technically narrator presence in action, but it works. Read it aloud in the R58 pass; leave it unless it trips the pace there. The same goes for "His sock had finally stayed up. Elizabeth hated that detail immediately." That's the gap in her own perception, and it should stay.
- **Ch9:** narrator glosses beside Shade. The Oct 5 report flagged four. Its own opinion was that Shade's lines "are the best writing in the stretch and need no gloss."
  - **Diagnosis:** **A** only where a gloss re-explains what Shade's line already showed (Line-Notes §12: once the gesture reads, don't explain it again). Check each of the four. Any that adds a new perception is not a gloss; keep it.
- **Ch14:** Mama Baga's and Shade's mechanics speeches ("Your intent is split…", "You are going to use the existing connection as the return line…"). Magic should be clinical. Shade explaining precisely is in character; Mama Baga explaining mechanics is the docent pattern (`forbidden_patterns` #2). **Shorten hers to the orders** ("Stop." "Lift your hand." "Let the frame fall.").
  - **Diagnosis:** **A**, pacing at the climax, by your own forbidden-patterns list.
  - **C risk:** if the explanation is also her care (telling them how so they survive it), cutting it all changes her. Keep one clause of why if it reads as care.

**b) Glance traffic as stage direction.** Craft says the narrator "needn't be disembodied stage direction", and the archived oral-vs-clinical guide names "Pure stage direction where a small interpretive note would land" as a fault. In canon, "looked" runs at **13–17 per 1,000 words** in Ch6, 8, 13, 14, 16 and 18. The Oct 5 report measured your own pages at about 2 per 1,000; your pre-August Ch2 is under 2. The Oct 5 report asked you (Q10) whether cutting toward your rate is the right target; I don't see an answer recorded.

**Correction.** The first version of this review used "Stansbury looked at Pathwell. Pathwell looked away." (Ch7) as an example of the narrator going blank. In context the next sentence is "A day ago Elizabeth might have missed it: a conversation happening before anyone spoke." That is purposeful blocking: subtext, and Elizabeth learning to read them. It's exactly what your rule protects. **Keep it.**

The rate is a symptom, not a target. Your rule says not to remove blocking merely because the dialogue is understandable without it. Sampling Ch13 and Ch16, the looks split into two kinds:

- **Purposeful (keep):**
  - Shade looking at the coat, the grinder, the clothesline, the knot. That's his exact mind meeting ordinary things.
  - "The archivist looked back, and the game began."
  - "Milo looked toward the ash, then down at his bare foot."
  - "Pathwell noticed her noticing, and looked away first."
- **Repeated bare beats (the problem):** "Shade looked at Elizabeth." / "Elizabeth looked at him." / "Mama Baga looked at Stansbury." / "Mama Baga looked at Elizabeth." These come one after another as the only beat before a line of dialogue, doing the job of "said." Ch16 has about a dozen.

**Diagnosis:** **A** (repetition) only for the second kind, and only where several come in a row. A single bare look can be timing. Rather than cutting toward a number, take each run of bare beats and keep the one that carries timing. For the rest, either drop it or, where a transition goes flat, use the room for one small narrator note (the register's arrival / aftermath / one character observation). That last option is yours to write.

**c) Attitude personification.** **Withdrawn.** The Ch4 cluster the Oct 5 report flagged is mostly gone from canon; the onion and blanket lines it quoted are no longer there. What remains, "One sock had surrendered halfway down his calf," is clear, funny and characterizing. Under your rule, a physically impossible image is not a defect. No change recommended.

### 3.5 Keep Pathwell's madness visible late (target 10)

Canon gets this right; protect it. The North Star's specific warning is against the "subdued responsible adult." The current Ch17–18 balance is right: he objects aloud, complies, and stays funny. The risk is new prose that *certifies* his growth instead of showing his impulse being checked:

- V2: "Elizabeth appreciated the question he had learned not to make."
- V2: "He no longer looked around to see whether anyone had noticed."

Don't adopt those. The model is canon's "though he pretended otherwise with less commitment than before": the old behavior is still there, just weaker.

Ch18's physical eccentricity is thinner than early Pathwell's: hands in pockets, careful ribs. That's justified by the ribs. If you want one more trace of velocity, the North Star suggests *showing* the first impulse ("He should still visibly have the first impulse"). That would be your line to write.

### 3.6 The ending's tone (targets 2 and 11)

P55 left one open question: does the move from confession to geese banter read as forgiveness? On my cold read in Phase 1, it didn't. It read as unrewarded, with Elizabeth walking away from his confession to the shelves. The tone holds. Leave it for the uninformed reader panel as planned; don't pre-fix it.

## 5. V2 versus canon on tone

On tone specifically, canon is closer to the notes.

V2's overlay mostly adds narrator presence. To be precise under your rule, its *images* are not the problem. Your own first draft puts figurative language straight into action: hinges fighting a bulging door, blob pieces landing "with the weight of a soaked blanket." The problems are two other things:

- **Glosses after gestures.** The half-nod is followed by a sentence explaining what it means. That's **A** (Line-Notes §12) and **C**, since it turns your unspoken "so to speak" into a stated one.
- **Wry tags after observations, at a density** that runs against target 7 ("sparingly"), target 8 ("Do not turn every observation into a clever observation") and the register's "The punchline is the last word." It also adds narrator commentary inside action (the Ch1 blob fight; the Ch15 shelf line).

Individually many of these tags are **B**. The **A** is cumulative: V2 imposes a whimsical style, which your rule says not to do any more than a minimalist one.

V2's genuinely tone-positive additions are the **gap** kind: physical and situational comedy played straight. Pathwell glaring at each stone; "rescued or captured"; "invent a child… going badly"; Stansbury "just looking at his car"; the ribs "conducting the actual vote." Those fit target 1 and are worth taking (see the Phase 2 harvest list).
