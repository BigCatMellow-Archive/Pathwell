## Specific gap being repaired

The current [Chapter 1](../../../Chapters/Chapter_01.txt) has three acts in quick succession:
1. A welcome party happened in **Elizabeth's own apartment**. Pathwell appeared among the guests; she never caught his name.
2. After the guests leave, she finds him rummaging through boxes, quietly reading her diary.
3. An unrelated cleanup creature arrives before any cookbook working, and Pathwell assumes Elizabeth's apartment is marked.

The reader can see trespass and magical danger but is given **no earlier visible sign that the trespasser entered for handwritten records rather than for Elizabeth**. Chapter 3 shows Pathwell's appetite for charge and that the papers are not his to spend; Chapter 9 says Elizabeth was never the target. The *reasons for his break-in and for the blob pursuit are different*, but prior cold-reader panel and the author's Oct 16 assessment both say the opening motive isn't recoverable enough.

### Correcting a previous overstatement

The older [Aug 22 interview](../../../Interview/BIBLE_DECISIONS_2026-08-22.md#3-chapter-1-entry-and-false-importance-setup) and the [character bible](../../../Story_Files/character_bible.md) say Pathwell encountered her **slightly open apartment** while scavenging; the later manuscript explicitly puts the **party inside that apartment**. Those statements are **not inherently irreconcilable**: a guest-filled party may have an open entrance, and he can follow a strong charge *through that party*. However neither the present manuscript nor the ruling tells us exactly who left the door open or how Pathwell first learned of the party. **Do not add those logistics as already-known facts or rewrite the Bible yet.** The local issue is absent motive evidence, not a provable geography impossibility.

## Controls and candidates

### A — Existing Chapter 1 (no change)

> She knew him, a little. He'd been at the party, talking to everybody. He'd laughed at her joke about the landlord, which was more than anyone from work had managed. She'd never caught his name. She'd thought he'd left with the rest.

### B — Minimal party-level physical cue (preferred controlled trial)

> She knew him, a little. He'd been at the party, talking to everybody. **She'd assumed someone from work had brought him. She'd seen him peering into an open box of old notebooks once, before somebody asked for more cake.** He'd laughed at her joke about the landlord, which was more than anyone from work had managed. She'd never caught his name. She'd thought he'd left with the rest.

**Complete noncanonical Chapter 1: [EXP-0008B](EXP-0008B-Ch01-Party-Notebook-Cue-Noncanonical-2026-10-16.txt).** The entire file is a copy of the current chapter with **only** the two bolded sentences inserted. This is the strongest candidate for author evaluation, **not a commit to Story/Chapters**.

**Mechanism by experience:** Elizabeth recalls his interest in an open box of older handwritten matter; she was hosting and moved on. At the reader's first pass he seems improperly curious. After the Space Between, a reader has enough material to infer he was scouting charge. It does **not** say he came for Elizabeth, knew her before the party, or caused the blob by accessing the notebooks. The notebooks are an ordinary moving-box prop, **not a new enchanted object, stored inventory, or promised payoff**.

**Potential fault:** The first sentence about “someone from work” is a plausible **assumption by Elizabeth** (not a certified fact), and the second adds a tiny bit of extra inventory/staging. Her reason for not confronting him is simply that she is busy hosting; the 'more cake' interruption may be slightly convenient. A skeptic could still ask why Pathwell knew to attend *this* party; the author's intended **scavenging by chance/sensing** gives a world-side answer, but that side of the story is deliberately not in Elizabeth's head.

### C — Explicit motive dialogue (comparison only; rejected for test)

Possible stronger but poorly voiced alternative: she asks why he touched the diary and he explains that old handwritten pages hold something useful he can spend. This might provide near-perfect literal comprehension by Chapter 1, **at the cost of** the original “Do you mind?” / “Not at all.” joke, a characterically implausible honest explanation under a door attack, and an early magic lesson. **REJECT as larger, worse first trial.** No dialogue drafted into production.

### Strongest case against B

A can legitimately argue that **the audience should discover his interest through the fact of the diary in his hand**. We might be solving a reader comprehension issue with an extra sentence that repeats what he is already doing. The proper comparison question is not “does B show a notebook?” but **“after reading Ch3, can readers tell why the party guest stayed and searched?”** If a fresh reader gets that from A, B is redundant. If both fail, a pre-party explanation or magical sensing scene may not be the right fix: perhaps one **later** question from Elizabeth about his unwanted theft would satisfy the contract, but such a change belongs to a different experiment and must not spoil the later reveal.

## Proposed blind, no-priming questions

1. Immediately after Ch1: “What is the stranger doing here, to the best of your knowledge? What is he interested in?” (Do **not** ask about notebooks.)
2. Immediately after Ch3: “What explains why he stayed in her apartment and took her things? What explains why the creature appeared there?” Listen for *two causes*, not one.
3. Immediately after Ch9: “Was Elizabeth ever the creature's target? Was she important to Pathwell before the creature arrived?”
4. Ask for one line that prompted each inference; note guesses and uncertainty. Keep A/B groups separate.

Success requires that a reader can articulate **a scavenger who seized an opportunity to get charged writing** after Ch3, and **a pursuer following Pathwell/Shade, not Elizabeth** after Ch9. A mere increase in suspicion in Ch1 is not sufficient evidence of success.
