## What must happen in sequence

The current scene first shows the cleanup creature as a wet substance at Elizabeth's chained front door **before** any magical work. Pathwell closes the door, gets Nana's cookbook, locates the sugar-cookie recipe and puts his hand over it. Letters glow; the already damaged door starts to break; light consumes the page. **Elizabeth grabs the cookbook, leaving the page under his palm**. Only then does the original author's irreplaceable sensory sequence begin: white light, butter/sugar, the sound of Nana's voice, baking memory; Elizabeth stumbles and the broken working drops strands of gold. The creature breaks through, devours fallen waste, and attacks the remaining light. This is not Elizabeth conjuring a monster and not a lesson about magical rules.

The decisive reader problem is **not whether gold light is visibly supernatural** — it is. The author asks whether *Elizabeth gets an instant to be struck by it* instead of experiencing the page only as property at risk. There is a risk of replacing a startling sensory memory with a redundant spectacle.

## Exact controls — one replacement each, entire chapters available

### A — current manuscript (the control)

> He placed his hand flat on the page, the letters glowed a soft gold.

**Strength:** Fastest, closest to author's own early draft, lets the later sensory cascade be the scene's main marvel.  
**Fault:** Comma splice and almost instantaneous visual information; no time for Elizabeth's initial fascination. That lack is the author's identified problem.

### B — short, visible motion [full draft](EXP-0011B-Ch01-First-Magic-Restrained-2026-10-16.txt)

> He placed his hand flat on the page. Gold traveled along Nana's handwriting, lighting the uneven letters one after another. Elizabeth leaned closer.

**Difference:** +80 characters over A, including space and punctuation. One paragraph, no new objects or dialogue. A first motion of gold is now visible, and Elizabeth involuntarily leans toward it before the following threatening door paragraph.

**Pros:** Little friction; fixes the comma splice; stays in one visual register without suggesting new charge/ownership mechanics.  
**Cons:** “Gold traveled … lighting … letters” is visually generic; it may still feel like the basic glow in A with more words. “Elizabeth leaned closer” makes physical movement without a new observed payoff. Most vulnerable to being needless padding.

### C — animate ink under illumination [full draft](EXP-0011C-Ch01-First-Magic-Ink-and-Perception-2026-10-16.txt)

> He placed his hand flat on the page.
>
> Gold ran along the ink in little starts and stops, even through the crooked places where Nana's pen had hesitated.
>
> Elizabeth leaned close enough to see the ink still there beneath it.

**Difference:** +154 characters over A, two added paragraph breaks, no other change. It is the **existing visible handwriting** that behaves impossibly; Elizabeth notices the singular features of Nana's pen strokes. The continued presence of ink under light means this is a *visual experience*, not a newly defined rule by which words literally detach from a page.

**Pros:** An ordinary remembered thing acts extraordinarily; the book remains specifically Nana's, not interchangeable spell fodder. The closer look gives the added perception a physical reason. “In little starts and stops” plays against the smoothness of stock glowing-runes prose. The following door crack returns the threat at once.

**Cons / author's decision:** “Where Nana's pen had hesitated” is an *inference Elizabeth may make from crooked ink*, not an established personal memory from elsewhere. Some readers may think we are inventing microscopic spell mechanics; if so, trim to “Gold ran along the crooked handwriting” without qualifying the cadence. Three paragraphs may feel precious between door thuds. It is meant as **substantial test input**, not an automatic upgrade.

## Critical comparison and recommendation

**Choose C for the author's in-context read**, with A as a serious alternative. It best responds to the *specific author's complaint*: time seems briefly to dilate, but by close physical detail and perception, not a literal slow-time spell or an unearned omniscient aside. The reader may feel that the crooked pen strokes are more important to Elizabeth than luminous gold; that is the right kind of competing pressure before her book grab.

**Strongest counterargument:** The original first-magic encounter is already the sensory transport to Nana's kitchen. C risks artificially announcing “wonder” before the actual moment arrives. If that happens when read aloud, prefer A rather than B merely as a compromise. A sentence that repeats the same light in new words is not a win.

The prior EXP-0009 fuller paragraph (“Elizabeth leaned closer. She could still read Nana's writing through the light. The ink shone even in the places his fingers didn't touch. For a second the thudding at the door sounded far away”) has a **distant-door cliché** and overlaps both A and the following “glow … consumed the page”; it is not the default simply because already written. EXP-0011 resets the trial to *the current manuscript* after the approved R50 door change.

### What to ask without priming a reader

After reading Chapter 1 once: “Where did you first feel that something impossible was happening? What stood out in the scene?” Then: “Did the episode feel rushed or slow at any moment?” Do not ask whether the handwriting is *wonderful*, or suggest that the magic should slow time. Compare whether A or C first points to Nana's handwriting versus generic gold light, and whether the cookbook grab still feels spontaneous.
