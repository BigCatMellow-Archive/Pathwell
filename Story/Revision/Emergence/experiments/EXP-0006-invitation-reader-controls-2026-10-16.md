## Thesis to test
Elizabeth may **freely invite Pathwell** without deciding that the wrong he confessed has been forgiven. Current Version C preserves a real browsing interval but later resumes familiar joking. A reader might understandably read that reset as reconciliation.

## Actual story constraints
- Ch16: Elizabeth tells Pathwell she cannot make herself responsible for his becoming better and does not yet know whether she forgives him.
- Ch17: he works under other people's direction, but she **does not yet know** he withheld help when Shade begged.
- Ch18: silent cookbook put-back → “I could have called someone. I didn't.” → Elizabeth's attempted analogy → his “That's not what I did” → **another hour browsing** → options/geese comedy → explicitly asks “Do you want to come?” → final three lines.
- Author explicitly selected this Version C **and** the no-absolution invitation. The proposed test may not replace that choice without author approval.

## Tabletop observations
- The **bookend works**: Ch1 has Pathwell ask and impose momentum, Ch18 has Elizabeth ask and Pathwell volunteer. That is a nonverbal change in agency.
- Her time among shelves and her explicit invitation are on the page. Yet **elapsed time alone** is not the same as visible processing of a new moral fact. The reading can go either way. Neither possibility is verified by this editorial pass.
- The right fix, if any, is **smallest**: observe an unsolicited reader interpretation before manufacturing a dramatic absolution conversation. New emotional beats are author-review material under [Writing Against Sameness](../../Writing-Against-Sameness.md).

## Cold questions (not administered)
Read the book in order, without saying which ending is under test; after the last line ask:
1. What changed in Elizabeth's view of Pathwell in the shop, if anything?
2. Does going with him mean she forgave him? What scene supports your inference?
3. Who could have refused the proposed geese adventure?
4. Where did you become distracted, disbelieve her reaction, or feel rushed?

## Decision
**A remains selected; B and C remain conditional tests, D excluded.** No prose or ending change. Readers should not be told to infer a lack of forgiveness before answering.

## Author feedback after control design — 2026-10-16

**Author (tentative):** “Maybe understanding or like, she knows now isnt the time to press him and he needs a distraction?”

**Updated reader hypothesis:** Elizabeth hears a shame-driven admission and recognizes that further questioning in this moment may not be helpful. She can offer **companionship and a practical distraction** through an outing she independently wants. This is **not stated forgiveness**, not a solemn decision to rehabilitate Pathwell, and not proof the issue is resolved. His offered company is not the price of her invitation.

**Controls remain unchanged** until tested, but the reading question should be broader than binary “forgiven / unforgiven.” Test whether the current ending communicates:
1. She understands why he confessed and isn't trying to make him confess again.
2. She knows the emotional conversation needn't be finished tonight.
3. She still wants the geese trip herself, and is **not now responsible for treating his shame**.
4. She chooses to invite a flawed companion rather than declare moral equivalence or pardon.

This is a **possible motivation**, not author approval for an extra interior monologue, therapy-like dialogue, a one-hour grief-processing scene or a change to the “Perfect.” lines. The author-origin wording is in the [feedback record](../AUTHOR-FEEDBACK-2026-10-16-Opening-Choice-Invitation.md).
