## The headline

**The first half passes the author's test.** The blind reader answered "keep reading?" with yes after all nine chapters, and would read the second half "without hesitation". Its best chapter was Ch4 ("the chapter that made me want the book"). The blind reader found every hard beat in Ch1–4 lands somewhere soft: the cake, the dumpling, the coffee, the handcart, the soup. The lens reader confirmed the same thing.

Both readers name the same places to protect: **Camp (Ch4) and the bar (Ch6)**. The lens reader's "It was a decent time. She let it be one." names the mode. Its section 7 is the list of what not to touch.

**The convergent finding is the same as in the back half: the voice.** Both readers, independently, heard the book's dry straight men blur into one corrective deadpan:
- the blind reader: "the world sometimes feels like one witty voice in several costumes";
- the lens reader: the "No. [correction]." shape from five speakers, and Elizabeth picking it up in Ch7–9.

Pathwell, the shopkeeper, the children, Ken and the waitress stay distinct. The speaker who blurs most is Stansbury after Ch5. In the author's own Ch5 he's showy and vain; from Ch6 on he only corrects.

## Questions for the author (Q)

**Q1. The corrective deadpan beyond the healer** (extends EXP-0019 Q1, which R59 answered for the healer). The "No. [correction]." lines are:
- the archivist (Ch4): "No." The archivist dipped his pen. "That is generally the opposite of an archive.";
- Stansbury (Ch5): "No. You said something came through a door and tried to kill you.";
- Stansbury (Ch6): "No. I ordered it because I enjoy disappointment.";
- Shade (Ch8): "No. I'm getting coffee.";
- Elizabeth (Ch8): "No. It's one of them."

*Lean (the lens reader's, and mine):* keep the archivist's (he's the sanctioned dry wit, R33) and Shade's (it's his literalness). Reconsider Stansbury's Ch6 line and Elizabeth's Ch8 line. Both are character-defining lines, so nothing is written here.

**Q2. Stansbury's vanity after Ch5.** In the author's Ch5, Stansbury is theatrical and a little pompous: "Oh, heavens no." / "Always so crass, Pathwell." / shaking the tree and staying dry. From Ch6 on he flattens into the same correct, precise register as the archivist and the Mama Baga who faces Pathwell. One trace of the Ch5 vanity somewhere in Ch6–7 would set him apart. *Lean:* yes, one trace. It needs a new line, which is his to write.
