## Author-selected direction and review method

1. **Live in the questions.** The author does not know the answers in advance to how someone moves from a passenger's life to a more self-directed one. Elizabeth is an avenue of exploration, not a lesson plan. Do not force her to "learn" a predetermined moral or turn every scene into evidence for it.
2. **Treat existing thesis statements as possibilities, not conclusions that must be proved.** The author explicitly selected this on 2026-11-19 ([R71](../Decisions.md#the-authors-rulings-for-this-revision)). The *current thesis document* remains an intact reference for the current manuscript; its authority over a **future exploratory version** is under review.
3. **Interview only where needed; at most one focused question at a time, following what the author actually says.** Do not reflexively turn each answer into a new question, lead with constrained false choices, prewrite a character arc, or ask the author to confirm the assistant's theory. *The author explicitly halted escalating Shade psychology probes on 2026-11-24 because they were trying to answer questions the novel should live in.* [Interview §39](../../Interview/AUTHOR_GOALS_INTERVIEW_2026-11-19-20.md#39-interview-method-correction--stop-answering-the-questions-the-novel-should-inhabit-2026-11-24).
4. **Explore the author's lived inspiration only insofar as it serves the story.** He asked that personal-origin questions address him, rather than treating Elizabeth as an alter ego. When the discussion wanders into personal history without clear relevance, return to the creative question.
5. **Keep author statements, manuscript facts, and assistant hypotheses visibly distinct.** The author's recollection of why pruning was invented is testimony; the archived draft supplies corroborating text but not a comprehensive composition chronology. An assistant's explanatory reading is **not automatically approved lore**.
6. **Do not overliteralize — including during brainstorming.** An analogy about drink choice, habit, impulse, or relief illustrates the *felt pull toward fate*. It is not by itself a law of magic, literal addiction, diagnosis, mandatory degree of compulsion, or metaphysical proof of determinism. The existing [Do Not Overliteralize rule](../Author-Notes/2026-10-26_Do-Not-Overliteralize.md) already also protects figurative prose, expressive gestures, rhythm, pleasure, and unusual language from automatic "clarification."
7. **Allow productive ambiguity and surprise.** Do not build new rules to answer every "how" or "why" automatically. Mechanics and structure are tools when a story needs them, not proof of craft by themselves. Conversely, genuine contradictions, confusion, and consequences are still valid problems to address.
8. **Protect the creative experience as well as the book's craft.** The author prefers discovery/gardening, warmth, oddity, irreverence with heart, and room for an interesting idea that exists because "it just seemed different." This confirms prior [R68](../Decisions.md#the-authors-rulings-for-this-revision) on Sunday Morning tone; it does not erase the original manuscript's real gains, dangers, or grief.
9. **Do not mistake AI-assisted revision results for the author's original goals.** Many revisions involved AI. Compare original draft text, proposals and author decisions where documented, actual scene effects, and unresolved attribution separately. An author-approved change is a real past choice but not necessarily his present preference, and AI assistance does not make a scene automatically wrong. Elizabeth may surprise herself by acting; that preference is not an arc formula.
10. **Don't silently reopen established decisions or alter the manuscript.** The author has authorized a review and documentation here, not a new outline, revised endings, restored narrator, or rewriting. Ask for an author selection before replacing a locked target. Keep the historical choices distinguishable from choices for any new version.
