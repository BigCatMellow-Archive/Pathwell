Chapter One

It was the crash that woke her.

Elizabeth lay still for a moment, listening. Something had fallen over in the living room, something heavy enough to make an impressive noise of it.

She pulled herself out of bed and made her way toward the sound.

Stepping into the living room, she found a man going through her boxes. One stack had been knocked over, scattering winter sweaters across the rug. The man sat among them, paying no more attention to the mess than he might have to the weather.

The party was still scattered around the apartment. Plastic cups on every available surface, a tray of cheese cubes going dry, and half a sheet cake that now only read ELIZ because her coworkers had started eating from the wrong end. A WELCOME ELIZABETH banner hung by one corner over the kitchen doorway, having lost its enthusiasm sometime during the night.

It had been a good party, better than she'd expected. Everyone had left hours ago, leaving her with the cleanup and enough cheese to see her through the week.

Everyone, apparently, except him.

She recognized the man, though she couldn't remember his name. He'd been at the party, and she'd assumed someone from work had brought him. She remembered seeing him peering into an open box of old notebooks, before somebody asked for more cake. He'd laughed at her joke about the landlord, which was more than anyone from work had managed.

She was fairly certain she'd seen him leave.

But there he was, sitting on the floor with one of her boxes open beside him, reading her diary like a magazine in a waiting room.

He turned a page.

Elizabeth stood there, trying to decide which part of this deserved her attention first. The stranger, the boxes, the diary, or the possibility that he'd been here the entire night.

She settled on the stranger.

"What are you still doing here?"

"Reading," he said, turning another page.

She opened her mouth to explain the difference between what she'd asked and what he'd answered.

Something hit the front door.

A thud. Wet. Heavy.

The man looked up from the diary.

Then at her.

"Friend of yours?"

"What?"

Another sound came from the hallway, something shifting against the wood.

Elizabeth went to the door, turned the lock, and opened it against the chain.

The smell reached her first. Low tide, but worse, as though the sea had left something behind and the sun had been particularly diligent about it.

Something wet and dark sagged into the narrow opening. It pressed against the chain, finding what little room the gap offered, and began to squeeze through. A thick smear dragged down the doorjamb.

Then the door slammed shut.

Elizabeth stumbled back.

The man was beside her now, his shoulder against the wood and his hand on the bolt. She hadn't even seen him cross the room.

The chain rattled. The door bowed inward, straining under the weight of whatever was on the other side.

Then it settled.

The man's expression changed.

He turned away from the door and went straight back to the boxes.

"Where's your cookbook?"

"Excuse me?"

"Your cookbook." He shoved a box aside. "The old one."

"My what?"

"The old one. Handwritten. Notes in the margins. I know you've got one around here somewhere."

Another thud struck the door, harder this time.

"Now."

"They're… they're in a box in the kitchen?"

He rushed past her.

There was a tearing sound from the kitchen, followed by the unmistakable noise of someone going through boxes with absolutely no intention of putting anything back.

For one absurd second she wondered if he meant to cook something.

The thought didn't survive the next blow to the door.

He returned carrying a book, looking rather pleased with himself considering the circumstances.

Nana's cookbook.

Three rubber bands held its spine together, doing the work the original binding had abandoned years ago. Jones Family Recipes, written in her grandmother's blue marker, still showed through the worn plastic cover.

"Don't think. What's your favorite recipe?"

"I—"

"Don't. Think."

The door creaked. The wood stretched and complained, putting up a respectable fight against something that appeared to have a great deal more weight than patience.

"My Nana always made the best sugar cookies?"

"Good… that's good."

He flipped through the cookbook, searching among pages softened by years of use. Some were stained, others folded, and a few were covered in little corrections written in the margins. Nana had been particular about her recipes, though apparently not particular enough to write them down correctly the first time.

He stopped at a yellowed tab.

Nana Jones Sugar Cookies.

"There we are."

He placed his hand flat against the page.

"You should probably get behind something."

"What are you doing?"

Gold began to move through the ink.

It followed the letters, tracing the loops and crooked lines of Nana's handwriting. It even found its way through the places where her pen had hesitated, filling each one with light until the whole recipe seemed to be waking up beneath his hand.

The door cracked.

Something dark pushed through the splintering wood, feeling along the gaps, reaching farther into the room.

Under his palm, the gold spread across the page. A pool of light formed beneath his hand, warm and bright, spilling over the edges of the paper.

"What are you doing!?"

Elizabeth grabbed the cookbook by its spine and pulled.

The page tore free.

It stayed stuck to his palm while the rest of the book came away in her hands, leaving a ragged edge where her grandmother's recipe had been.

Then everything went white.

A scent hit her, buttery and sweet, followed by a taste.

Sugar cookies.

But there was something else, something that had come along with the smell and the taste, arriving before she could make any sense of either.

Someone else.

A voice that couldn't be there.

She was twelve again, standing in her grandmother's kitchen. Sunlight poured through the open window above the sink, the fresh smell of rain mixing with warm sugar and butter. Her grandmother stood beside her, smiling, saying something Elizabeth couldn't quite make out.

No, she could. She knew that voice.

And with it came a feeling she hadn't felt in years, something warm and safe and terribly familiar, something she had spent a long time trying to remember and an equally long time trying not to.

For a moment, the apartment was gone.

There was only the kitchen, the smell of cookies, and her Nana standing beside her.

Her body moved before she did.

Elizabeth stumbled backward into the wall, clutching the cookbook. It slipped from her hands and landed open on the rug.

The light in the man's palm had gone wrong.

It spat and flickered, loose threads of gold dripping from the torn page onto the carpet. A few clung to his fingers, stretching and breaking as he moved his hand.

Another wet thud struck the door.

This time the wood gave up.

The frame split and the door crumbled beneath the weight of the thing pushing through it.

Now she could see the whole creature, though whole was giving it more credit than it deserved.

It was a blob. An enormous mass of dark, rotting, gelatinous something, rolling itself into her apartment and taking pieces of the doorway along for the ride.

It seemed to have no particular shape in mind. The thing gathered itself, sagged, then gathered again, pushing across the floor in wet folds.

It rolled over the fallen threads of gold.

The light disappeared into it.

Then the creature turned toward the gold still burning in the man's hand.

It lunged.

He raised the glowing page to meet it.

The impact sang.

A clear, bright note rang through the room, so entirely out of place that for an instant Elizabeth could only stare.

Then the blob rippled and recoiled, the stench of wet-rot splattered across the room.

Her stomach objected immediately.

Elizabeth ran for the bathroom, one hand over her mouth, the other reaching for the door.

Behind her, the creature lunged again.

It struck the man hard enough to knock him from his feet and send him sprawling across the living room. She caught a glimpse of the thing looming over him before she slammed the bathroom door shut.

There were sounds of struggling, pounding, kicking.

A loud, wet smack.

Something hit the wall.

Then nothing.

Elizabeth stood with her back against the door.

She waited.

There was no sound except her heart, which seemed determined to make up for the silence on its own.

She pressed an ear to the wood.

Stillness.

A stillness interrupted by a dull rumbling, rumbling that grew into shaking, a shaking that worked its way through the walls and into the floor beneath her feet.

Light streaked in from under the door.

Then a pop.

And everything went quiet again.

Elizabeth didn't move.

"You can come out now. It's gone… mostly."

Mostly.

She considered the word for a moment.

It didn't improve with consideration.

Slowly, she cracked the bathroom door.

Her living room had exploded.

At least, that was the best explanation she could come up with for the state of it.

There were pieces of rotting gelatinous ooze across the walls, the floor, the furniture, and what remained of her moving boxes. A particularly large piece was still making its way down the kitchen doorway, leaving a greenish trail behind it.

The welcome banner had fallen into the mess.

The cake, somehow, was fine.

And there in the middle of it all stood her last guest, holding Nana's cookbook and her diary.

"What. What just happened?" she asked from behind the door.

He looked around.

"That? That was nothing. Just cleaning up a mess."

He sounded almost satisfied with himself.

Elizabeth looked past him at the wall, where another lump of ooze had finally surrendered to gravity and dropped onto the carpet.

"Cleaning up?"

"Well, there was a mess."

Then he looked at her more carefully.

"You really don't know?"

"Know what?"

"Huh."

He looked from her to the ruined doorway and back again. His mouth opened a little, then closed, as though he'd been about to ask something and thought better of it.

Elizabeth came a little farther out of the bathroom.

"Right, so…" He shifted the books under one arm. "We should probably get going."

He made his way toward what was left of the front door, stepping over the debris and carefully avoiding a puddle of ooze.

The door itself was gone, or at least it wasn't where a door had any business being.

He stuck his head into the hallway, looked one way, then the other, and went still.

Elizabeth watched him.

Whatever he'd seen, or heard, he didn't seem interested in sharing.

When he turned back, he had the comfortable expression of someone who'd decided what ought to happen next.

"Your house has now been marked. Magically speaking."

He gave a little sideways half-nod with the last two words, a small, almost shrugging movement that seemed to qualify the statement without taking any of it back.

"So we should probably be anywhere but here."

"Marked?"

"Yes."

"By what?"

"Well, that's rather the trouble, isn't it?"

"No, the trouble is that there's a giant hole where my front door used to be, and you're standing in my apartment holding my things!"

"Also true."

"I'm not going anywhere until you tell me what is going on."

"You really have no idea?" He studied her face for a moment, apparently still looking for something he expected to find there.

She gave him nothing.

"Well, that confusion is quite normal. Do you have shoes?"

"Yes, but—"

"Good. Now here's the thing. We really do need to get going. So please. Grab your things."

The last part carried rather more demand than request, despite his best efforts with the word please.

Elizabeth looked at the cookbook under his arm, bent open at the ragged edge where Nana's sugar-cookie recipe had been.

Something tightened in her throat.

"Why could I hear her voice?"

"What?"

"My Nana. Why could I hear my Nana?"

"Because it was hers."

He shifted the book, tucking it more securely against his side.

"Now, really, we should be going."

"No, look at what you did! My apartment is wrecked. How am I going to even clean… whatever this is?"

She gestured toward the living room.

"Understandable reaction. Reasonable, even. Unfortunately, we really need to be going."

"You're not listening to me."

"I am. Quite carefully."

"Then answer my questions!"

"I've answered several."

"You haven't answered anything!"

"Well, now we're having a disagreement about what constitutes an answer, and I'd rather not have that conversation here."

Elizabeth stared at him.

He glanced toward the hall.

"Preferably somewhere with a front door."

She stepped fully into the living room, and the smell came back to greet her. Her stomach dropped as another wave of rot hit her.

There were things she needed to collect. Her phone, her wallet, clothes for tomorrow. The apartment was ruined. Her grandmother's cookbook was missing a page. A man whose name she didn't know had apparently saved her life, possibly destroyed her home, and was now insisting they leave before she had time to understand either event.

She started toward the bedroom.

"Shoes," he said.

"I know!"

"Now."

Something in his voice made her look back.

He was no longer watching her. His attention had shifted down the hallway.

"I did say please."

A wet thud came from somewhere beyond the ruined doorway.

Elizabeth froze.

Another sound followed, something heavy dragging itself across the floor.

She looked at the empty space where her front door had been.

Then at the man.

Then at her shoes.

Whatever questions she had could apparently survive a change of location.

She jumped into her shoes, threw on a coat, and turned to find him waiting by the doorway.

He smiled, took her by the hand, and started toward the hall.

"Ready?"

"No."

"Perfect."


Chapter Two

"Will you slow down!?"

Elizabeth struggled to keep up as the man charged down the stairs, taking them two at a time with the confidence of someone who had never considered falling down them.

"What about all my stuff?"

"Replaceable."

"My stuff?"

"Yes."

They burst through the lobby and out onto the street, where the cold night air struck Elizabeth hard enough to steal what little breath she'd managed to keep.

She stopped on the sidewalk, doubled over, and tried to get it back.

The man continued several steps before noticing he'd lost her.

Looking up, Elizabeth could see her apartment window. The light was still on.

She'd been there for three weeks. Three weeks. Twenty-one days of unpacked boxes, takeout dinners, and trying to work out what came next. She'd barely started putting things away, never mind making the place feel like somewhere she lived.

Her feet almost turned back on their own.

The only thing she'd properly unpacked was Nana's mixing spoon. She'd left it on the kitchen counter, intending to find a drawer for it eventually. It was an ordinary wooden spoon, worn smooth in the middle and darkened along the handle, and she'd carried it across the city wrapped in a dish towel because she hadn't trusted the movers with it.

Now it was sitting in an apartment without a front door.

And there was that thing. Whatever that thing was.

The man came back toward her.

"You okay?"

"No," she snapped.

"Fair."

She straightened, pulling her coat more tightly around herself. Her hands were shaking, although whether from the cold or from everything that had just happened was anybody's guess.

"Where are you taking me?"

"There's a lovely Chinese spot just around the corner here."

She stared at him.

"I have a meeting at nine tomorrow."

"I swear it's really just around the corner."

"No, I have a meeting. At nine. In the morning."

"Right."

"My landlord is going to kill me. How am I going to explain the door? I have to get my dry cleaning before the meeting. Where am I going to get a new door? I don't have my wallet. Do you think someone's going to steal my stuff? Should I call the cops?"

The questions came faster than she could consider the answers. Each one seemed to bring two more along with it.

He nodded politely through the whole thing, his gaze drifting up toward the streetlight above them. He had the patient expression of someone listening to a particularly long explanation of a problem he had no intention of solving.

Elizabeth ran out of breath before she ran out of questions.

"This is all a dream," she said. "Tell me it's all a dream."

He pinched her arm.

She slapped his hand away.

"Why would you do that?"

"It's not a dream. Just a really weird Tuesday for you."

"It's Thursday."

"Which makes it all the weirder."

He turned and started walking again.

Elizabeth remained on the sidewalk, rubbing the spot on her arm and considering, not for the first time that evening, whether the man was an idiot.

She looked back toward her apartment.

A shadow filled the window.

It was there for only a moment, dark against the light she'd left burning.

She didn't stay to see if it moved.

By the time she caught up with him, he was turning the corner.

She followed, expecting to see the coffee shop she passed every morning on her way to work. It was a little place with expensive coffee and a woman behind the counter who had begun remembering her order, which Elizabeth had taken as one of the first encouraging signs that she might eventually belong in the neighborhood.

Except there was no coffee shop.

There was a laundromat.

An old one, with metal shutters pulled across the windows and a sign whose better days had clearly been behind it for some time.

Beyond that was a park.

A whole park, with trees and benches and a path disappearing between them.

Elizabeth stopped.

There had not been a park here yesterday. She was quite certain of it. Parks were difficult things to overlook, particularly when they occupied space previously devoted to several perfectly respectable buildings.

She looked back around the corner.

The street behind her was still there.

She looked forward again.

Three weeks she'd lived in this neighborhood, and she no longer recognized a single thing.

The man seemed entirely satisfied with the arrangement.

"So then, what'll it be? You more of a lo mein girl? I'm all about the dumplings myself. The dumplings are perfect here."

Elizabeth looked at him.

"You just destroyed my home, and now you're hungry?"

"I wouldn't say hungry. More peckish."

He considered the distinction for a moment, apparently finding it an important one.

"And for the record, I saved you. Most people are thankful when someone does that."

"Thankful!?"

"Well, eventually."

They arrived at a little Chinese shop open to the street, its windows crowded with photographs of food whose colors had faded into several variations of orange. A red neon dragon flickered above the counter, occasionally losing its head before finding it again.

The place smelled of frying oil, soy sauce, and the particular warmth of a kitchen that had been working long after most sensible people had gone home.

"Two orders of fried dumplings, please."

"I'm not hungry," Elizabeth said.

"Oh, these aren't for you."

He turned toward her, as though remembering some important point of etiquette.

"And I don't share."

"Then why am I here?"

"Company?"

He gave her a small, hopeful look, then returned his attention to the counter before she could object.

Elizabeth considered leaving.

She could turn around, walk back to her apartment, call somebody, or find a police officer and explain that a man had broken into her home, fought a monstrous collection of rotting jelly in her living room, and then brought her to a Chinese restaurant in a neighborhood that no longer appeared to be her neighborhood.

She couldn't imagine that conversation ending well.

Instead, she took a seat at one of the plastic tables along the curb.

The chair protested as she dropped into it, its thin legs bending slightly beneath her before deciding to cooperate. She folded her arms on the table and rested her head against them.

The cold worked its way through her coat.

She put her hands into her pockets, rubbing her fingers together for warmth. There were a few coins in there, a receipt, and something folded tightly enough to have survived several trips through the laundry.

A piece of paper brushed against her hand.

The ticket.

Elizabeth sat up.

Her dry-cleaning ticket.

She pulled it out, smoothing the yellow paper against the table.

She could still get her clothes.

The cleaners would have everything ready in the morning. She could pick them up on the way to the meeting. It would be tight, but she'd managed worse mornings than that.

She could still make it.

For the first time since she'd woken up, there was an actual problem with an actual solution.

She emptied the rest of her pockets onto the table, digging through the coins for enough money to cover the pickup. The bright yellow ticket sat on top of the little pile.

Then a truck roared past.

It came close enough to rattle the table and lift the edge of the ticket. The paper skittered across the plastic surface, slipped over the side, and caught a gust from the truck as it passed.

Elizabeth reached for it.

Her fingers missed by inches.

The ticket danced once along the curb, caught the gutter, and disappeared.

She stood so quickly the chair nearly went over behind her.

"No, no, no—"

She looked down the street.

Nothing.

She checked beneath the table, along the curb, even in the narrow gap between the chair legs, as though the ticket might have changed its mind and returned.

It hadn't.

"Okay," she said.

She stood with her hands open in front of her.

"Okay, I can just… I can…"

She could explain it to the cleaners. There would be a record. They would have her name. She could call in the morning, or—

Her breath caught.

The smell came back.

Wet. Rot.

It had followed her, clinging to the inside of her coat, waiting there beneath the cold air and the smell of frying oil.

She pressed her hands flat against the table.

She opened her mouth, but nothing came out.

It was a dry-cleaning ticket.

A small square of yellow paper. She knew that. She knew perfectly well how ridiculous it was to be standing on a sidewalk, surrounded by traffic and strangers, feeling as though she'd just lost something irreplaceable.

But there were the boxes. Three weeks of boxes she couldn't bring herself to unpack. Bare walls. Her grandmother's spoon on a kitchen counter she might never see again.

A life she'd packed into garbage bags and moved across the city because staying had felt worse than leaving, only to discover that leaving didn't feel like much of anything at all.

And now she couldn't even manage to keep hold of a piece of paper.

She wasn't sure when the tears started.

She only knew she couldn't stop them.

Elizabeth sat back down, buried her face in her hands, and let herself cry.

The city carried on around her.

Cars passed. Somewhere down the block a siren complained its way through traffic. A group of people walked by laughing about something one of them had said, their voices drifting away before she could make out the joke.

A taxi splashed through a puddle.

The neon dragon continued its struggle to remain entirely illuminated.

Nobody seemed particularly concerned that her life had come apart at a plastic table outside a Chinese restaurant.

Then the chair opposite her scraped against the pavement.

The man sat down with a tray of dumplings and the satisfied air of someone whose evening was finally beginning to improve.

He set Nana's cookbook and her diary on the table beside him.

He was just reaching for the food when he noticed Elizabeth.

He looked at her.

Looked at the dumplings.

Then back at her.

He sighed.

"Oh, fine, you can have a dumpling."

Elizabeth raised her head.

He had already selected one, though from the care with which he made his choice, she suspected it wasn't an easy sacrifice.

He placed it on her side of the tray.

"There."

She stared at it.

He sat back, apparently satisfied that the matter had been handled, and began eating.

Elizabeth wiped her face with the heel of her hand.

The dumpling sat between them, steaming faintly in the cold night air. Its edges were uneven, one side a little darker than the other, and a small pool of grease was collecting beneath it.

She watched the steam rise.

She had no idea what she was supposed to do with it.

The man inhaled his second dumpling, then a third, chewing with the enthusiasm of someone who'd been thinking about these things all evening.

He gave her elbow a little nudge.

"You gonna eat that?"

"No."

"Suit yourself."

He reached over with his fork and claimed the abandoned dumpling.

"I'm tellin' ya, Lizzy, you don't know what you're missing here."

"I've had dumplings before."

"Ah, but not these dumplings."

He said it with such complete sincerity that Elizabeth almost found herself wondering what could possibly distinguish them from any other dumpling.

Almost.

She wiped the last of the tears from her cheeks and looked across the table.

"Who the hell are you?"

He paused with another dumpling halfway to his mouth.

"Pathwell."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"First name?"

"Pathwell."

"Last name?"

"Don't need one. Saves everybody time."

He resumed eating.

Elizabeth waited for him to offer something more.

He didn't.

"So, where to next?" he asked.

She blinked.

"Excuse me?"

"The night is young, Lizzy! Where do you want to go?"

"Home."

Pathwell chuckled softly.

It was a small sound, barely more than a breath, but it landed badly.

Elizabeth straightened in her chair.

"You wrecked my house."

He looked up.

"There's pieces of some… thing all over the place. You won't let me go home. I have a meeting I cannot miss in the morning, nothing to wear to it, and I don't even know where we are anymore!"

She gestured toward the street.

"There's a whole park around the corner that wasn't there yesterday!"

"Lovely park, isn't it?"

"No!"

He lowered his fork.

"All you can think to do is get some crappy food and ask me what's next?"

Pathwell glanced down at the tray.

For the first time that evening, he appeared genuinely offended.

"First off, I wouldn't go back there tonight. But that's just me."

He picked up another dumpling.

"Secondly, these things are perfect."

Elizabeth stared at him.

"Perfectly crappy," he continued. "They aren't trying to be anything they're not. You have no idea how hard it is to find that kind of quality."

He took a bite, closed his eyes for a moment, and nodded to himself.

There was no arguing with that sort of conviction.

Elizabeth looked away.

On the other side of the street, a man was trying to persuade a reluctant dog to continue walking. The dog had settled firmly on the pavement and appeared to be winning.

For a moment she envied it.

Pathwell finished chewing and brushed the crumbs from his hands.

"That being said…"

He stood, gathering Nana's cookbook and her diary beneath his arm.

"It's up to you what you want to do next. The choice is yours, Lizzy."

He looked at her as if he'd just offered something generous.

Then he turned and walked away.

Elizabeth watched him go.

He didn't look back.

She sat beneath the flickering neon dragon, its red light washing over the empty chair and the little table where he'd left her.

The tray was still there.

One last dumpling sat on her side.

He'd left it for her.

She looked at it for a moment, then toward the street where he'd disappeared.

He'd told her she could choose.

She could go home, or at least try to. Find a police officer. Find a hotel. Find someone who could explain why the streets had moved and what had happened inside her apartment.

She could make her own decisions.

It was just that none of them seemed especially good.

The dragon flickered overhead.

And then she heard her again.

Lizzy.

The name came with a warmth she recognized before she understood it.

Butter and sugar. The heat of an oven. The smell of fresh rain drifting through an open kitchen window.

Her Nana, standing beside her.

Elizabeth's eyes moved to the empty chair.

Then the tray.

Then the street beyond it.

She looked back at the table, slowly at first, then all at once.

The cookbook.

Her diary.

She was on her feet before she'd finished the thought.

He still has it.


Chapter Three

She found him at the corner, looking up at a streetlight.

He had Nana's cookbook tucked under one arm and her diary under the other, carrying both with the easy confidence of a man who'd never been told either book was his to carry.

The streetlight flickered. Pathwell watched it with a degree of interest she wished he'd shown in her questions. Elizabeth had to stop herself from looking up as well. She'd had quite enough of things not behaving the way they were supposed to.

"So then," he said, still looking at the light, "where to?"

"I'm not going anywhere without getting some questions answered."

"Sounds good, Lizzy."

"Don't call me that."

"Isn't that what your grandmother called you?"

He tried the name the way someone tries a familiar greeting across a counter. Elizabeth had known people who did that. None of them had pulled her grandmother's voice out of a recipe.

"That's… that's not an answer. And don't think for a second I forgot you still have her cookbook."

"You'll get them back at the counter."

"What counter?"

"Lizzy, I can answer all your questions. Even the ones you haven't thought of yet. We just need to swing by a bookstore first."

"What kind of bookstore is going to be open this late?"

Pathwell finally gave up on the streetlight and looked at her.

"The right kind."

He shifted the diary and cookbook under one arm, pulled a folded piece of paper from his pocket with the other, and pressed it flat against the concrete wall behind her.

Light bled through the creases, spiderwebbing along the wall. A soft square, no bigger than the paper itself, glowing against the grey. He peeled back a layer and the square doubled. Another layer, and it doubled again. The light stretched past the edges of the paper until a doorway stood where the wall had been, warm and humming faintly.

"What is that?"

"Like I said, a bookstore. We really do need to work on those listening ears, Lizzy. Now, don't be shy. They have the most amazing coffee."

"How do I know this isn't some kind of trap?"

"You don't."

Pathwell stepped through and turned left.

She stood on the sidewalk, staring at the doorway. It had begun as a folded piece of paper and was now doing the work of several hundred pounds of bricks and hinges, which seemed an unfair amount to ask of stationery.

Beyond it were shelves crowded with books.

A loose page drifted out, spinning once in the air before settling at her feet. Its handwriting was faded, but the ink smelled of lavender. She didn't know what to do with that information. She was still struggling with the door.

Then the rest of the place reached her: old paper and dried leather, binding glue gone brittle, dust that had settled undisturbed long enough to consider itself part of the building.

Pathwell had disappeared inside.

He still had her books.

She stepped through.

The street noise cut out as neatly as if someone had shut a window. Elizabeth glanced behind her for the traffic she'd been hearing a moment ago. Nothing but the door, the silence, and the quiet little complaints of shelves with more books than sense. She'd been in libraries quieter than this, but never one that seemed to be listening back.

Books.

Floor to ceiling, wall to wall, stacked and leaning and wedged into every surface the room could offer. Some had titles printed proudly along their spines; others had been bound by people who apparently possessed string, determination, and little else.

Not books — lives.

Journals with cracked spines. Letters tied in ribbon whose knots had been pulled tight by hands long gone. Recipes spotted with oil, with instructions crossed out and rewritten by somebody who had finally figured out why the bread kept falling. A packet of birthday cards that had never been sent. Music copied on the backs of invoices, the notes growing cramped as the paper ran out. The things people saved because they mattered, and the things they saved because they couldn't quite bring themselves to throw them away.

Thousands of them, maybe more, pressed together so tightly the shelves bowed under the effort. There was something wonderfully untidy about it, though Elizabeth couldn't have said why. For all she knew, she was looking at a very serious fire hazard.

"Lizzy, would you mind closing the door?"

She turned. Pathwell was already halfway down an aisle, moving as though he knew which shelves would lean and which floorboards would complain. She reached for the door, found a perfectly ordinary handle, and pulled it shut.

The frame dissolved into the wall.

She stood with her hand still raised, feeling rather foolish.

Just shelves now. Shelves in every direction.

"Pathwell?"

He was gone.

"Pathwell!"

A shush from somewhere deep between the rows.

She went looking for him. The idea of standing alone in this place waiting politely for Pathwell to return struck her as a particularly poor one.

The first aisle looked harmless. Dusty books, cramped shelves, a gap where someone had removed a volume and apparently forgotten to return it. She brushed past a stack of journals and came around the corner.

Her chest tightened. She stopped with one hand on the shelf, waiting for the feeling to pass. It wasn't fear, not at first. It was somebody's terrible, familiar disappointment, arriving fully formed and taking up room beneath her ribs.

She stopped beside a shelf of journals, waiting for the feeling to explain itself.

It didn't. Her eyes burned. She blinked hard and kept walking.

It passed. Two steps later her skin flushed warm and her pulse dropped and she felt, for no reason at all, like she was about to cry over something she couldn't name. She stopped and reached toward the nearest shelf. The spine of a journal was warm to the touch.

She pulled her hand back. None of that had been hers, and she held on to that.

Then something was. The next turn hit her like a door slamming. Her heart punched against the base of her chest, her mind catching on a single thought and looping it — where am I, where am I, where am I — her fingers going numb at the tips, her lungs reaching for a breath that wouldn't come. She braced herself against a shelf and the books there seemed to lean toward her, pressing closer, the aisle narrowing with each exhale she couldn't quite finish.

Grey crept into the edges of her vision. The last thing she registered was the carpet against her knees.

When her eyes opened, they were heavy. She was in a chair, her feet up on an ottoman. A thin orange cat sat in front of her, green-eyed and waiting.

A cup of coffee swung into view, with Pathwell behind it.

"Just in time."

A plain white mug on a plain white saucer. Pathwell held it with both hands, giving the dreadful thing an undeserved air of ceremony.

She took it by instinct and sipped.

It was awful.

Reused filters. A pot that needed washing. Beans chosen because they were cheap and coffee was coffee, wasn't it? She knew the taste. She'd made it for herself on late nights studying, when the kettle had been left on too long and she was too tired to remember whether she'd already poured a cup.

It tasted like the past.

"That's terrible."

"I know."

"Why does it help?"

"Familiar things are useful."

The room came into focus around her: a large oaken counter, a round man in round-rimmed glasses behind it, and dim lights that seemed content to illuminate just enough to keep the customers from walking into things.

Nana's cookbook and her diary sat on the counter beside a ledger. Elizabeth's eyes went to them at once.

The cat rubbed its head against her shins, with the practiced friendliness of a creature who had decided to be very pleased to see her.

Elizabeth reached down to pet it.

A claw flashed. The skin on the back of her hand split open, fast and surgical, a thin line dragged through the soft part between her thumb and wrist.

She snatched her hand back.

Blood welled up. The cat looked immensely satisfied with the arrangement and licked its chops.

"He wanted his tribute," the man behind the counter said. "I would let him have it, personally."

Her eyes darted back and forth, looking for the punchline.

"Hey, it's okay," Pathwell said. "You're fine."

He snapped a strip of paper from his pocket and laid it across the cut. Gripping it firmly, he whispered a few words. The paper glowed warm and faded into her hand. The wound and the pain went with it, leaving behind a lingering feeling that reminded her of a boy she knew in middle school.

"Love note," he said, and smiled. "Cures most wounds."

He rubbed his thumb over the thin line it had left behind.

"Unfortunately, scars are outside my wheelhouse. How about this. You finish that coffee there, and I'll answer any questions. Deal?"

Elizabeth looked at the cup. The coffee was still terrible, but at least it had the virtue of being a problem she understood.

She gulped it down.

"I'll take that as a yes." The chair beside hers huffed as Pathwell flopped into it. "Right, so you're wondering where we are. The truth is, we're in a bookstore, commonly referred to as the Space Between. As for Boots there, he did indeed sample the blood from your cut. He's the full-time guardian and part-time blood god."

Boots had already jumped onto the counter. The shopkeeper rubbed him behind the ears. "We pay him in mice."

"A blood god?"

"Neither here nor there. What's important is where we're going."

"Where are we going?"

Pathwell clapped his hands. "Glad you asked."

He got up and went to the counter. Elizabeth put the mug down and followed him. She reached for Nana's cookbook.

The shopkeeper rested one finger on the cookbook, politely but with such finality that Elizabeth stopped reaching.

"His transaction first."

She looked at Pathwell. "You said I'd get them back at the counter."

"And look," Pathwell said, spreading his free hand as though presenting an architectural triumph. "A counter."

"That's not the same thing."

"It's alarmingly close."

The shopkeeper looked at Pathwell over his glasses. "Yours?"

"No."

"Good." He didn't move his finger. "After settlement. He brought outside property into an open transaction, and I dislike paperwork."

He went back to his ledger, making ticks and carrying ones with the concentrated irritation of a man whose figures had developed habits of their own.

Elizabeth thought of Pathwell on her living-room floor, turning the pages of her diary like he was pricing it.

"So what do we owe?" Pathwell said.

Without looking up, the shopkeeper sighed. "You already owe quite a bit."

"I'm good for it."

"All bills come due eventually, Mr. Pathwell."

"What do I owe for today?"

The shopkeeper turned to the pile at the far end of the counter, the one Pathwell must have built while she was out, and looked it over. Journals, bundles of letters, sheets of poetry, a few pages of music, blank archival paper, and a wooden crate tied with twine. Enough of it that the counter complained.

"Some of these are very old, Mr. Pathwell. They won't come cheap. Did Camp Cunnan ask you for all of this?"

Pathwell studied a knot in the crate's twine, giving it the kind of attention he hadn't spared for the question. "They need it."

"That wasn't what I asked."

Pathwell didn't answer.

The register dinged and clacked. Numbers jumped across its face, apparently arriving at a conclusion the shopkeeper had expected and Pathwell was about to dislike.

"We could settle today, past bills and all, for… a Rai Stone."

"Oh, come on. Be serious. A Rai Stone?"

"I'm afraid I am." The shopkeeper removed his glasses. "And quite frankly, Mr. Pathwell, you already owe more than I allow most."

"Surely we can barter here."

"Outside of a significant Rai Stone, I could do it for, say, thirty Lydian coins."

"Okay, now you're just messing with me."

The shopkeeper was unmoved.

"Aren't you?"

"Unless you have something else to barter with…"

"I've got potential to sell. Lots."

"You did."

Pathwell chuckled nervously.

Elizabeth looked between them. "What does that mean?"

Neither of them answered her.

"Are you sure?" the shopkeeper said.

"Can we do this before I change my mind?"

The shopkeeper turned to the wall behind him and slid open a panel that had blended into the soft brown paneling. Behind it sat a cast-iron cube. He pulled a short sheet of paper from his sleeve and laid it over the door. His hands moved over it, turning it light blue. His fingers danced. A thunk, a clank, and shifting gears.

A door popped open from nothing.

Inside sat a leather-bound tome, its edges gilded in silver. A dot was branded into the center of the cover, surrounded by concentric rings and lattice arcs. Thick, heavy and dense, with thin pages.

He lifted it with both hands and placed it on the counter with reverence.

"Mr. Pathwell, are you sure this is worth what it will cost?"

"I am."

"Then say the words."

Pathwell stepped to the book and placed his right hand on the cover, his left across his chest. "I understand the cost of my needs."

The silver pages glowed a beautiful blue, like a distant sky.

"I give what may be, to protect what will."

The pages burned bright. Roots of light shot from the book and grasped Pathwell's forearm, wrapping tight, intertwined and wild.

"Perdat quod esse poterat."

They pulsed, turning a violent blue. New roots split off, snaking up his arm and digging their way in. Splintering thinner, then burrowing, twisting, wriggling. Taking hold.

From the center of the book, fibrous tendrils wove upward, gnarled and knotted. The tree grew to Pathwell's chest before splitting and branching out. Some branches grew mangled, withered, dead. The tree bloomed in patches, sick and breathless, and took its full shape, sitting sickly and worn.

The roots pushed deeper into his arm. Pathwell's free hand searched for the edge of the counter and missed. Elizabeth started toward him, but there was nothing on the book she could grab without touching the fire. She stopped one step short.

"You don't have much left to give, Mr. Pathwell."

"Take what you will, and spare me the lesson."

Orange flames took hold of the branches, spreading out from around the tree and burning bright against the light blue leaves. The roots burned a frightful orange, searing the arm they wrapped.

Pathwell screamed in a million voices, voices lost to time, burning out to become memories never made. One by one they fell quiet, until all that was left was a harmony of extinguished cries.

The fire burned down, taking all but a few living branches at the core and leaving only scorched fibers reaching out. Pathwell dropped to his knees, his arm still held up by the burrowed roots.

Then the roots let go, and he caught the counter with one hand. Elizabeth was halfway around it before she knew she'd moved.

"Thank you, Mr. Pathwell." The shopkeeper's pen had begun moving again. Elizabeth watched it scratch its way across the ledger, each neat little tick arriving while Pathwell struggled to get his knees under him.

"Your bill has been paid in full. Will that be paper or plastic?"

Pathwell looked up. His right arm was shaking, but there wasn't a mark on it.

"Surprise me."

He tucked the arm against his side and pulled himself upright, as though posture had become the problem. It was the kind of gesture Elizabeth might have expected from someone attempting to look presentable after falling off a chair, not from someone who had just screamed in voices that didn't belong to him.

The shopkeeper slid Nana's cookbook and the diary across the counter. "These were never his to spend."

Elizabeth took them. The diary was cool under her hand. The cookbook felt heavier, which was stupid. Missing a page should have made it lighter. Her thumb found the ragged edge where the page had been.

Pathwell saw, and looked away.

"You just sold your potential," she said.

He flexed the shaking hand once. "Some of it."

"Which part?"

"If I knew that, I'd make worse decisions with more confidence."

The shopkeeper pulled a handcart out from behind the counter. It unfolded twice, extended once, and stopped at a size that shouldn't have fit back there in the first place.

Pathwell stared at it. "You had that the whole time?"

"Yes."

"You've watched me carry things out of here by hand."

"Yes."

"For decades."

The shopkeeper set the first bundle on the cart. "Your account is settled now."

Pathwell looked genuinely wounded.

Elizabeth laughed. One short, surprised sound. Both men looked at her.

"Don't make anything of that."


Chapter Four

She had assumed, without quite thinking about it, that the word camp would mean something she could leave.

A camp was tents, fires, people who packed up when the weekend was over. She'd imagined something with edges: a place you could walk out of and point back to. After the bookshop, she felt she was entitled to at least that much geography.

Camp Cunnan announced itself in woodsmoke before it showed up.

Then lantern light through the trees.

Pathwell came down the last stretch of dirt road pulling the Space Between's handcart behind him with the offended posture of a man who had only recently learned the handcart existed.

Each time a wheel struck a stone, Pathwell turned a little to glare at it. On the fourth stone Elizabeth began looking ahead for the fifth, just to see whether he would do it again. He did.

Elizabeth walked beside it.

Nana's cookbook and her diary rode on top of the Camp books.

She had put them there herself, after the shopkeeper had offered to send her back to the sidewalk.

That fact still felt strange enough to keep checking.

Where the road gave out, a path went on into the trees, and Pathwell went with it without slowing down, the cart bucking over roots. Elizabeth kept one hand on her books until something sharp worked its way into her shoe. Her shoes had been bought for an office. She stopped to shake it out.

Pathwell didn't look back. By the time she'd got the shoe back on, the cart's rattle had gone on ahead of her, the lantern light had dropped out of sight behind the slope, and the path had split around a tree. She picked a side. Her books were on that cart.

"Pathwell!"

Nothing. She grumbled under her breath. "Darn it, where are you?" Then, louder: "Pathwell!"

The sounds of the forest were the only response.

She made her way around a large oak, tugged her shoe out of the undergrowth, and took one step with more confidence than it deserved. Her foot found a tree root. The rest of her followed.

The ground came up to greet her. Dirt got into her mouth.

She lay there for a moment, deeply annoyed with the woods and not particularly inclined to make friends with them.

A hand took her arm and pulled her up onto her knees.

"Now, you really do have to be careful, young lady. These trees are very spiteful."

A kind-faced old woman with a lantern was looking down at her, a red shawl over a white linen shirt and a scarf over her head. Her skin was sun-cured and a little wrinkled. "Where are you off to in such a hurry?"

"I was looking for my friend," Elizabeth said, as something between a confession and a complaint.

"Oh, you're Pathwell's friend?" The old woman sounded surprised. "He is waiting for you."

Elizabeth got the rest of the way to her feet, brushing off her clothes. "Where is he?"

"He said you would say that." The old woman smiled gently.

"Where are we?"

"He said you would say that too." She laughed lightly. "This way, if you please. Just follow me."

She led Elizabeth through the trees, around stumps, over fallen logs and between rocks, and never once seemed to consider slowing down for a woman who had spent the evening running for her life in office shoes.

Where a fallen trunk crossed the path at almost Elizabeth's height, the old woman hopped up and over it as if stepping across a curb.

Elizabeth climbed after her, somewhat less gracefully, and privately reconsidered everything she had assumed about the woman's age.

A short hike brought them into a clearing between two wagons.

A fiddle was playing somewhere. Smoke rose from the center of the ring, carrying the smell of something roasting and the faint sharpness of burning wood. Somebody was chopping more wood, which seemed unnecessary given the amount of it already burning, but Elizabeth was learning that this was not a night for questioning other people's arrangements.

Through the gaps between the wagons she caught the camp a piece at a time. A man in an old blazer and no shirt played a fiddle that was missing a string. A small boy leaned his whole weight into a crank that turned a boar over the fire. Children ran between the wagons with the solemn business of people who had agreed on rules and were already arguing about them. A woman read somebody's palm without looking as if she expected any surprises. People laughed around a table. Someone called for another mug.

The wagons made a loose ring around the fire, with smaller fires wherever people had decided to settle. A patch on one canvas roof had been patched again. Rope hung from pegs and ironwork gleamed where hands had polished it through years of use. Nothing matched. Almost everything had been repaired. For once the disorder didn't look like an emergency someone had left Elizabeth to clean up.

Near the fire, two children crouched over a beetle with the serious attention children reserve for creatures too small to protest.

"It's dead," one said.

"It's practicing," said the other.

"For what?"

The second child considered this. "Being dead."

Pathwell had beaten her there. The handcart stood in the middle of the ring, and three people were already unloading it without being asked. A boy carrying a basket of turnips stepped around it without slowing. One sock had surrendered halfway down his calf. He hooked it back up with his heel, failed, and carried on anyway. Then he put the turnips down and joined in.

The old woman set her lantern down, went to the cart and got her shoulder under the crate tied with twine, the one Pathwell had loaded with both hands and a great deal of complaining. She stood up with it. There was no effort in it, no sound of strain. She simply picked it up and was on her way.

One of the men lifted a bundle of blank archival paper and made a sound of genuine relief.

"We were down to six sheets."

Pathwell turned around, walking backward so he could enjoy having been right in front of as many witnesses as possible.

"You see?"

The man ignored him.

The old woman came back without the crate and looked Pathwell over properly, now that there was firelight. "You look terrible."

"I always look terrible."

"You look worse than that."

She glanced at the cart, then at the blank paper being carried away, then back at Pathwell.

"We did not ask for this."

"You needed it."

"We did."

Pathwell smiled. The old woman didn't.

"You could have written first."

Pathwell looked toward the man carrying the last bundle of blank paper. "On what?"

The old woman shut her eyes for one patient second. Pathwell took the opportunity to inspect the wheel of the cart. Elizabeth got the impression they'd done this together before.

Elizabeth had reached him by then. "You just left me in the woods," she said through her teeth.

"I did no such thing." He put an arm around her shoulders and turned her to face the old woman, as if presenting them to each other. "I sent someone to get you. She found you, didn't she? Thank you, Mama Baga. Elizabeth here would be figuratively, and quite literally, lost without you. She's just a little nervous after the bear."

"There was a bear!?"

"Not if you didn't see her." He swept his free arm out at the camp. "Welcome, Elizabeth, to Camp Cunnan, home of the Cunning Folk. Dwelling of charmers, blessers, wizards and witches. Abode of the spaewives and the gwyllion. Refuge for all manner of oracles, tarot readers, bone casters and druids. Haven to the mambos. Did I say runemasters?"

"You did not."

"Runemasters! Dwelling for all manner of fortune-tellers, prophets, mystics and diviners. Sanctuary, alcove, holdfast and haunt to many of the more spiritually attuned."

"And the occasional no-good thief," Mama Baga said.

Pathwell turned back to her, smiling. "Oh, please. I was only ever no good at being a thief."

Elizabeth stepped out from under his arm. "This is your home?"

He turned his palm up. "Home is such a permanent term. I prefer… layover."

"Humph," said Mama Baga. Then she looked at Elizabeth properly. "And you must be the one he has been dragging around. Are you being dragged?"

Pathwell answered for her. "No."

Mama Baga kept looking at Elizabeth.

Elizabeth looked at Pathwell, then at the handcart. "Less than I was."

Mama Baga's mouth moved at one corner. "I will take it." She nodded toward her wagon. "You look tired. Come with me."

Elizabeth followed. Pathwell didn't.

Mama Baga's wagon was close and warm, hung with bundles of dried plants and small jars labeled in a system Elizabeth had no category for: names, numbers, marks that might have been measurements or warnings. Rolled papers leaned in corners. Quilts took up more space than some of the furniture, and a curtain was half drawn across the back.

It smelled like tea, lanolin, smoke, and something that might once have been sage before spending thirty years inside a wagon.

"Sit before you fall over politely," Mama Baga said.

"I'm fine."

"That is not a posture."

Elizabeth sat on a wooden crate beside the wall. It had onions in it. She had begun to suspect the whole camp was run on the principle that any object might also have a second job.

Mama Baga poured something into a chipped cup and put it in Elizabeth's hands. It was tea, or something tea had become after a long argument with bark.

"Drink."

Elizabeth did. It was terrible.

Mama Baga didn't ask why Elizabeth smelled faintly of low tide or why she was wearing a coat over her pajamas. She shifted a cup so Elizabeth could set hers down and tucked a blanket away from her shoe. The questions, for the moment, were apparently going to wait.

She tucked the corner of a blanket away from Elizabeth's shoe.

Elizabeth nearly cried again.

"Well," Elizabeth said, finding a smile, "I've made quite an entrance."

"You're being awfully polite about it."

"I try."

"I can see that."

Elizabeth took another mouthful of tea, more to give herself something to do than because she wanted it.

"It's just been a long night," she said.

Mama Baga pulled up a stool. "I wasn't only thinking of tonight."

Elizabeth looked up. "You don't know me."

"No," Mama Baga said. "But I can get to know you."

Elizabeth drew the blanket higher over her knees. For once, she couldn't think of the polite thing to say.

The wagon door opened behind them. The boy with the turnips came in backward, carrying Nana's cookbook in both hands, her diary balanced on top.

"Pathwell said bring these to you," he told Mama Baga. "Where?"

"Those are mine," Elizabeth said, faster than she meant to.

The boy froze.

Elizabeth set down the tea. "I mean — yes. Mine."

She stood and took both books. The diary went under her arm. The cookbook stayed in her hands, three rubber bands taut around its ruined spine. One page missing. She opened her thumb across the worn plastic cover, feeling the place where Nana's blue writing showed through. A book shouldn't need so much holding together. Then again, she'd never minded doing it.

"Thank you," she told the boy.

He nodded once and immediately forgot the interaction. "Archivist wants the rest of the cart," he told Mama Baga.

"The archivist has the rest of the cart."

"He says Pathwell has organized it."

Mama Baga looked toward the door. "That is different."

The boy nodded solemnly and disappeared.

Elizabeth sat again with the cookbook on her knees. Her thumb found the torn edge of the missing sugar-cookie page.

Across the wagon, someone breathed in sharply.

Elizabeth looked up. Behind the curtain was a cot she hadn't noticed.

A young woman lay beneath two blankets, maybe twenty-five, maybe younger. Her skin had the washed-out color of someone whose body had spent too long deciding which systems were still essential. One arm rested on top of the blankets. The fingers were curled and still.

Beside her sat a slight woman with her sleeves rolled past the elbows. Camp healer, Elizabeth guessed before anyone said it.

The healer pressed two fingers to the woman's wrist, waited, then moved a hand against her ribs. The woman on the cot was breathing, shallow and slow.

"What happened?" Elizabeth asked.

"Wagon slipped on the north grade," Mama Baga said. "She was between it and a tree."

Elizabeth looked at the young woman again. "That sounds as bad as she looks."

"It was."

The healer sat back. "I can keep her where she is. What we had that would help, I've already used. I don't have enough here to move her forward."

Mama Baga nodded.

Nobody looked at Elizabeth. Somehow that made the cookbook heavier.

The healer noticed it only when Elizabeth shifted her hands. Her eyes moved to the cover, then to Elizabeth.

"Family?"

Elizabeth looked down. "Yes."

"Handwritten?"

"Yes."

The healer didn't ask for it. She looked back at the woman on the cot.

Elizabeth waited for Pathwell to appear in the doorway and turn the whole thing into a problem he had already solved.

He didn't.

The young woman took one shallow breath. Then another.

Elizabeth opened the cookbook. The pages made the soft, dry sound of something that had been opened thousands of times.

Nana's handwriting started each recipe as if she had all the room in the world. By the ingredients it leaned rightward; by the instructions it hurried; at the bottom it crowded the margins with things she'd remembered only once the dish was already in the oven. Elizabeth could almost hear her saying, Oh, and another thing.

"Did she make anything when someone was sick?" the healer asked.

Elizabeth almost said sugar cookies. The answer hurt before she reached it.

She turned three pages instead.

Chicken and dumplings. Of course.

There was a grease mark beside the title and a penciled note in Nana's handwriting beneath the broth:

MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH.

Elizabeth laughed once through her nose.

"She believed soup was an argument you could win," Elizabeth said.

"Was she right?"

"Frequently."

The young woman on the cot breathed in, carefully, and out.

Elizabeth ran one finger down the edge of the recipe.

A different memory this time. She was seventeen, perhaps, at Nana's table with the flu, furious that the day was being wasted and more furious that Nana was right about resting. She'd kept both hands around the bowl, refusing to admit she liked the smell. Nana had watched from the stove and pretended not to notice when Elizabeth took the first bite.

The page was ordinary paper.

It had once been the sort of thing Nana would hand across a counter without a thought, with a smear of flour on one corner and an instruction to mind the pot. Elizabeth knew what that page could do now. She also knew she could never ask it to do the same thing twice.

Elizabeth closed the book.

"No," she said.

Mama Baga didn't move. The healer didn't move.

Elizabeth looked at the woman on the cot. Then she opened the book again.

"No," she repeated, quieter. "Sorry. That was for me."

She held the cookbook out toward the healer.

"The whole book."

The healer looked at her. "We only need the page."

"I know." Elizabeth's arms stayed out. "If I keep holding the rest, I'm going to spend the next ten minutes finding a reason this page is the wrong one."

Mama Baga's eyes stayed on her.

Elizabeth swallowed. "The whole book."

Mama Baga took the book with both hands under the spine. She did not praise Elizabeth for giving it or ask her to explain. She waited until Elizabeth let go, and only then turned toward the healer.

For one second Elizabeth nearly pulled it back.

She didn't.

Mama Baga passed it to the healer, and the book left Elizabeth's hands.

That was the choice.

Everything afterward was consequence.

The healer laid the cookbook beside the young woman and found the chicken-and-dumplings page. She read it once. Then again. She put one hand flat on the paper and the other lightly against the woman's sternum.

The ink warmed.

No flash. No burst of gold. The page simply began to give off the particular heat of a kitchen in winter.

Broth. Pepper. Flour dust against the back of a hand. The dense, soft smell of dumplings when the lid first came off.

Elizabeth heard Mama Baga breathe in behind her. She'd smelled it too.

The young woman's shoulders loosened. The healer said something too quiet to catch.

Nana's handwriting darkened on the page. Then thinned. Then disappeared one line at a time, and the paper itself followed.

Not burned. Spent.

The woman on the cot took a breath deep enough to move the blankets. Then another. Her fingers uncurled.

The healer kept her hand in place for several seconds after the page was gone. Nobody spoke.

Finally she sat back. "She'll sleep."

Mama Baga nodded.

"And then?" Elizabeth asked.

The healer looked at the woman. "Then she wakes up sore and hungry."

Elizabeth let out a breath. "That seems fair."

The healer closed Nana's cookbook. A clean gap remained where the page had been. The room smelled like chicken and dumplings for a few more seconds, and then it was a wagon again.

Mama Baga put a hand on Elizabeth's shoulder and left it there a moment.

Elizabeth stared at the cookbook. She expected the healer to hand it back. The expectation arrived automatically, the way you hold out your hand for change after paying.

Then she remembered what she'd said.

The whole book.

The healer looked at Mama Baga. "Archive?"

Mama Baga looked at Elizabeth.

Elizabeth's hand was still halfway raised. She lowered it.

"Archive," she said.

The healer tucked Nana's cookbook under her arm and went out to find someone to take it.

Mama Baga looked from Elizabeth's face to the coat hanging around her and the pajama legs sticking out beneath it. Elizabeth followed her gaze. Of everything she had survived tonight, apparently being dressed for bed was the detail that might finally embarrass her.

She went to a chest under the quilts and came back with an armful of clothes. "Try these. Take what you like."

Mama Baga hung a quilt from a hook to make a corner, and put the clothes into Elizabeth's hands.

When Elizabeth came out from behind the quilt, she was dressed in a mix of pastel linen and a short blazer, with a bandana holding her hair back. Her pajamas were balled in one hand.

Mama Baga looked her over, then looked again. "Something's missing."

She reached behind her neck and unclasped the thin gold chain.

"Oh, I can't take that," Elizabeth said. "It's yours."

"I know it is." Mama Baga patted her arm. "Hush now. You're taking it, and I won't hear another word about it."

She fastened the chain around Elizabeth's neck and stepped back. "There. Now it's complete."

Elizabeth looked down at the pastel linen, the blazer, the bandana. Nothing matched. A sleeve was a little long, and she had to push it off her wrist to see her hand. She couldn't remember when she'd last been this comfortable.

Mama Baga reached up and tucked a loose strand of hair beneath Elizabeth's bandana. "You'll fit in here."

Elizabeth left the pajamas folded on the onion crate and pulled her coat back on.

Outside, Camp was still up.

The fiddle had acquired a second fiddle. Somebody had started cooking onions near the central fire.

Pathwell was crouched beside the handcart, examining one of its wheels.

Elizabeth stopped beside him.

Word had gotten there before her. "She'll be fine," he said, eyes on the wheel.

"I know."

"That was—"

"I know."

He nodded. "This wheel is new."

"Newer," Pathwell amended. "Relatively."

She sat on the edge of the cart. For a while they looked at the wheel.

Then the boy with the turnips appeared again, carrying Nana's cookbook wrapped loosely in a square of clean muslin. "Archive," he said.

"I'm going that way," Elizabeth said.

The boy shrugged, which apparently counted as permission, and turned. Elizabeth followed him, the diary still under her arm.

Pathwell stayed by the wheel.

The archive was one of the nearest wagons on the ring, close enough to the central fire that people could reach it without thinking and far enough away that nobody had to sleep beside several generations of other people's feelings. It was larger than Mama Baga's wagon and considerably less decorative.

The door stood open. Inside, shallow drawers climbed both walls. Shelves held wrapped books, boxes, bundles of letters, ledgers, loose paper between boards, recipe notebooks, notebooks that had once been ordinary school notebooks, and a child's drawing of a horse.

Nothing in it glowed. After the doorway and the Space Between and the terrifying blue tree, Elizabeth was beginning to appreciate rooms that allowed their contents to remain ordinary.

The boy stepped inside.

"New one," he said.

A man at a narrow desk didn't look up. "Everything is new when you are twelve."

"I'm eleven."

"That explains the filing."

The boy looked at Elizabeth. "Archivist," he said, as though this explained the man.

The archivist finished the line he was writing before he looked up. He was somewhere past sixty and had the stoop of a person who'd spent most of those years leaning over other people's handwriting. His eyes went to the wrapped cookbook with the efficient interest of somebody who had never been much impressed by an object until he knew where it belonged.

"Donated?"

The boy pointed at Elizabeth, and she nodded.

"Elizabeth."

"Family name?"

She gave it. He wrote it down.

"Original writer?"

"My grandmother. Nana Jones."

"Jones first name?"

Elizabeth opened her mouth and stopped. She'd called her Nana for so long that for one terrible second the actual name didn't come.

Then it did. She told him, and the archivist wrote that down too.

"Pages used?"

"One tonight."

"Before that?"

"One. Also tonight."

The archivist nodded and added two small marks in the margin. Then he unwrapped the book. His hands were clean.

He opened the cover, looked at the spine, checked the two missing places, then wrapped the book again in fresh paper that didn't quite cover the grease stains.

"Reserve shelf," he told the boy.

The boy carried the cookbook only three steps and placed it on the front shelf. Elizabeth knew this ought to be reassuring. She could see it. She knew where it was. The distance didn't feel like three steps.

Not hidden. Not displayed. Available.

A woman appeared at the archive door holding a narrow blue notebook. "Tell Olan he was wrong."

The archivist didn't look up. "About which thing?"

"The creek crossing."

"That narrows it."

She set the notebook on his desk and opened it to a page marked with a strip of cloth. The handwriting changed three times down the page. Dates. Rainfall. Notes about a bridge. A line written sideways in the margin: DO NOT TRUST THE SOUTH BANK AFTER THREE DAYS OF RAIN, NO MATTER WHAT HESS SAYS.

The woman tapped it. "Three days."

The archivist read the line. "Hess has been dead twenty-two years."

"He'd still argue."

"That is true." He turned the notebook around. "Take it back to Olan. Then bring it back here."

The woman tucked it under her arm. "I always bring it back."

The archivist looked pointedly at the boy, who had found a loose button on the floor and was testing whether it rolled straight.

"It wasn't me," the boy said.

"No one accused you."

"You were going to."

The archivist sighed. The woman left with the notebook.

The archivist was already filling in another line.

"What happens to it?" Elizabeth asked.

She nodded toward Nana's cookbook.

"Whatever happens to any record people still need."

"That's not very specific."

"Specificity is expensive."

Elizabeth stared at him. He almost smiled.

"We keep the original," he said. "Someone may copy the recipes for information. The copy won't be the same thing."

"I know."

He nodded once, accepting that she did. "If a page is needed badly enough and fits the work, a healer may request it. If it survives long enough, someone may spill tea on it. Someone may argue about a measurement. Someone may cook from it. Someone may not touch it for thirty years."

Elizabeth looked at the reserve shelf. "So that's it."

"No." The archivist dipped his pen. "That is generally the opposite of an archive."

The boy laughed. Elizabeth did too, quietly.

The archivist returned to his ledger, and Elizabeth stepped outside. The fiddle had changed songs. The boy ran past her toward the central fire, one sock still down, the loose button forgotten.

Behind her, Nana's cookbook stayed on the shelf, and Elizabeth didn't go back for it.

Pathwell was no longer by the handcart.

She found him at the edge of Camp, looking into the trees. He'd gone still, the way he had in her apartment hallway before declaring the place marked.

Mama Baga stood beside him with her arms folded, watching it too.

"It was already at the door before you used the cookbook?" she asked.

"Yes."

"And when the working broke?"

"It changed targets. Fresh waste. That part made sense."

"The first part did not."

"No."

Pathwell looked toward the trees again.

"Stansbury."

Pathwell sighed. "I know."

"You should have gone to him before this."

"I had a delivery."

"You appointed yourself a delivery."

"They were down to six sheets."

"That does not make you less annoying."

"It makes me correct."

Mama Baga reached up and straightened Pathwell's collar as though he were twelve and badly assembled. "There it is."

Elizabeth came closer. "Who's Stansbury?"

Pathwell looked at Mama Baga. Mama Baga looked at Pathwell.

"My brother," Pathwell said.

Elizabeth waited. "That's all I get?"

"For now."

"Older or younger?"

Pathwell's expression soured. Mama Baga smiled.

"Older," she said.

"Traitor."

"Go see your brother."

Pathwell looked once more into the trees. "Fine."

He turned to Elizabeth. "You can stay here."

Elizabeth looked toward the fire. Someone had discovered the boy's turnips and was peeling them badly; he hovered nearby with the anxious pride of a person who had been given a task and wasn't sure anyone appreciated its difficulty. The fiddle kept going. The woman with the blue notebook was still arguing, using the notebook to make a point at a man who seemed determined not to take it. The archive door stood open.

For a minute she considered staying. There was tea and a warm wagon and a woman who had not asked her to earn either. Back in the city, her meeting was still at nine. She hadn't thought about it for an hour.

Then Elizabeth looked at Pathwell.

Not because he had her things. He didn't.

She wanted to know why something impossible had come through her door before the magic went wrong. And, against her better judgment, she wanted to meet the brother whose existence could make him look personally offended.

"I'm coming."

Pathwell nodded as if he'd expected that.

Elizabeth pointed at him. "Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Look pleased with yourself."

"I wasn't."

Mama Baga laughed behind them.

Pathwell started down the path. Elizabeth followed.

Behind her, Camp Cunnan kept being a place without asking permission.


Chapter Five

Pathwell wouldn't open anything near the wagons, so they walked until the trees thinned and the sky went gray. At the edge of a field Pathwell lay down in the grass and slept until well past noon, the way she imagined people slept after giving blood. The Space Between, Elizabeth guessed. She sat with one hand on the diary in her coat pocket and didn't sleep at all. Thumper's paw print was in there somewhere.

When he woke, he rolled his sleeves up and surveyed the field as if he'd been asked to appraise it. Elizabeth searched the grass for any sign of the road they'd left. There wasn't one. Pathwell, meanwhile, seemed satisfied by the amount of empty space available.

"Stay close, Lizzy. This next part is going to be fun."

He reached behind him and pulled out a handful of folded pages, stacked together in a mess. He gripped them tight, crumpling them in his hand, and a yellow light danced out in every direction from the edges of the paper. The fingers of his left hand turned in a circle, twisting and spinning. He opened that hand wide and snapped it shut, catching the light. A few more twists of his palm, a few flicks of his fingers, and he drew a circle on the ground around them.

From the circle, a golden line burst into the center, made a small bead there, and roped out in wild directions. The light burned Elizabeth's eyes and the ground beneath it, and the grass steamed. The circle spun around them, gaining speed. A wind kicked up from the ground. Elizabeth's new clothes filled with it, the loose hair behind her bandana danced in the swirling air, and Pathwell's coat took flight behind him and flapped. He tucked his arms into his chest. The wind stopped, and the circle stopped spinning.

"What was that all about?"

But as the words left her mouth, his arms shot out, palms open, fingers splayed. The circle took off again, spinning wildly, air shooting around them, and it was all Elizabeth could do to close her eyes. The wind whipped around her, gaining fury, and pulled the breath out of her. Her eyes watered. Her joints ached. Her bandana flew off her head, and she reached blindly to grab it before it was lost. Her hand was snatched back just as her fingers closed on it. Pathwell hugged her tight, and she shut her eyes again.

The wind let up. When she opened her eyes, the bandana in her hand had been singed in half.

Pathwell let go of her. The pages in his hand were gone. "You okay? Still got all your fingers and toes?"

She held the bandana up to his face. What remained of it was still warm at the edges.

"You mean that would have done this to my fingers?"

"Oh, most certainly," he said.

"What was that?"

"That was a portal."

"To where?"

"Here."

"Where is here!?"

"I don't know," Pathwell said cheerfully. "Let's find out."

Gathering her wits, Elizabeth found herself in a closet barely big enough for two people and a small collection of mop buckets. The air smelled of bleach and an old wet rag. A shelf of cleaning supplies occupied most of the wall, and she bumped it with her elbow. Of all the places to land after the world caught fire around her feet, she had arrived somewhere whose greatest mystery was whether anybody ever put the mops back.

"Looks clear."

"What do you mean, 'looks clear'? Where did you take me? Are we not supposed to be here?"

Pathwell shrugged. "Don't know. But it looks clear, and we aren't going to figure out much standing in here."

He opened the door, went out, took a few quick breaths and sniffed the air like someone assessing a doubtful casserole. Then he leaned back in. "Air's fine."

"Fine compared to what?" Elizabeth yelled in a whisper.

He shrugged again. "You never know with these sorts of things."

Through the open door, the hall was empty. White speckled tile, with a blue tile here or a red one there at random, sometimes next to each other and sometimes diagonal. The doors had numbers on them and names below that. One wall was lined with posters, and the other with double-stacked lockers.

"Why are there lockers on the wall?" Pathwell asked.

"What?" Elizabeth poked her head out the door. She saw the lockers, the posters, the names on the doors and the numbers above them, and she shrank back into the closet. In the quietest yell she could manage: "You brought us to a school!?"

"What?"

"A school, Pathwell. You know, children? Desks? People who ask you questions until you answer them?" She looked at him. "Surely you've been to one."

"Oh, that explains the posters." He walked up to one on the wall.

"What are we doing in a school, Pathwell?"

He looked back at her, still hiding in the closet. "Looking for my brother."

"In a school?"

"Apparently."

"What do you mean, apparently?"

"We went where we needed to go. That doesn't mean I know where that is. Sometimes where you want to go and where you need to go aren't the same place."

"That must be why I'm not at home, and I'm currently hiding in a freaking closet in an elementary school."

"How'd you know it was an elementary school?"

"It was a lucky guess," she snapped.

"You can come out of there. There's no one around. It's Saturday." He lifted both hands in assurance. Elizabeth looked past him at the silent hall. She had enough experience with his assurances to stay near the closet door.

Elizabeth opened the door, looked slowly around, and stepped out of the closet. As she closed it behind her, a bell rang, loud and long. She tried the door. It had locked behind her. Children came pouring out of the classrooms in a tidal wave of waist-high backpacks.

Elizabeth stared at Pathwell, wide-eyed and angry.

Pathwell stopped one of them by the loop at the top of his backpack. "Small child. What is today?"

"Friday."

Friday. Somewhere back in the city, her meeting at nine had already happened without her. There was something particularly irritating about picturing everyone taking their seats, apologizing for her absence, and getting on with things while she stood in a school closet with a stranger who couldn't read a calendar.

"You're unbelievable," Elizabeth said.

The pint-sized siege of chaos raged around them, but as all storms do, this one passed too. They had drawn the attention of a few teachers on their way by, but not enough to warrant an inquisition. So Pathwell started walking the halls, peeking through the glass in the classroom doors.

"Which room is he in?"

"I don't know."

"What does he teach?"

"I don't know."

"Does he even work here?"

Pathwell sighed. "You know, I'm going to run out of ways to say 'I don't know' if you keep asking the same question."

He turned toward the classrooms and sauntered along the hall. Elizabeth followed, trying for the expression of a woman with a perfectly good reason to be there. It might have worked better if she hadn't been carrying her coat over clothes Mama Baga had found in a wagon.

"What's wrong? Or don't you know that either?"

Pathwell cupped his hands around his eyes and pressed them against the glass of a darkened classroom. "I don't see anyone here," he said, disappointed. He straightened up. "And sarcasm will get you nowhere in this world."

"Can I help you?" a voice said behind them.

The man had a badge on a lanyard and the settled expression of someone who had spent a long time asking adults to do things they ought to know to do for themselves. Pathwell visibly considered walking away.

Pathwell waved him off. "No, no, we're good here. Thank you."

The man paused and looked at them. "Could you please put on your IDs? They're required to be worn at all times in the building."

"Well, you see…" Pathwell patted his pockets and looked around him. "I don't know what happened to them. I could have sworn we had them."

The man moved closer. "Was there someone you were looking for?"

"No, not at this time."

"Then what are you doing here? Who are you?" He reached for his walkie-talkie. "Can someone get me eyes on two guests in the north wing?"

"Oh, there's no need for that." Pathwell raised a hand and began to reach into the back of his coat.

"There you are! Honey, you just wandered off. I told you I was only going to be a minute." Elizabeth appeared at Pathwell's side and caught his arm before his hand disappeared into his coat. She smiled brightly at the man with the lanyard. Pathwell was still trying to decide whether he had been rescued or captured.

The man smiled politely, slightly confused. "You can just call me Ken. It's a pleasure to meet you. I'm sorry, I didn't get your name."

"Mrs. Pathwell. And this is my husband, Mr. Pathwell." She gestured at him with an elbow to the side. "Don't be rude, honey."

Pathwell jolted as the elbow landed a little more intentionally than he'd expected. He forced out a smile and leaned forward quickly with his hand out.

Ken shook both their hands, wary and confused. "Nice to meet you. Do you have a student here currently?"

They paused. Elizabeth felt Pathwell waiting for her to invent a child. She waited for him to do the same. It was the first time they'd cooperated on anything, and it was going badly. Then a voice sounded behind them.

"They're here to see me, Ken."

A tall man stepped around Elizabeth, a walkie-talkie still in one hand. His shoulders were wide and his hair was slicked jet black, and he was well dressed, in a button-down under a vest. "Sorry, I was running late, Pathwell. Hope I didn't keep you waiting long."

Pathwell locked eyes with him. "Stansbury." He forced a smile. "It's been too long."

Stansbury smiled back with a light chuckle. "Sorry about the confusion, Ken. I'll make sure they check out when they leave." Then, to the two of them: "Maroon car by the loading dock. Give me twenty minutes."

Pathwell was spread across the hood of a maroon Cadillac beneath the only tree in the lot. He looked up through its branches at the gray sky. The car wore its age without apology; the tree was doing rather less well at providing shade.

"My god. Is this what waiting is like?"

Elizabeth looked up from scratching at the little space beside her fingernail. "It's been twenty minutes, Pathwell. You can't tell me you've never waited for anything before."

Pathwell lifted his chin and looked at her upside down. "If I have, it certainly wasn't this boring."

"So what's he like?"

"Who?"

She nodded toward the school. "Your brother."

"Oh. Right." Pathwell turned over on the hood and propped his head in his hands. "Well…" He paused in thought. "When you've been at it long enough, friends become enemies and enemies become friends, blah blah blah. You get the idea. Stansbury just likes to be a thorn in my side. And everyone's side, whenever he gets the chance." He sat up. "There was this time I was dealing with a particularly annoying Bakhtak—"

"Oh, that was your fault, and you know it. You even told me afterward." Stansbury dropped a cardboard box onto the hood. He looked at Elizabeth. "He lies, you know. Don't listen to him when it comes to old stories. He only partially remembers his own half-truths."

"I do not," Pathwell said.

"See?" Stansbury rolled his eyes. Then his eyes went to Pathwell's right hand, which hadn't quite stopped shaking since the Space Between, and stayed there.

"You pruned."

"Hello to you too."

Stansbury leaned back against the Cadillac. "Mama Baga sent word that something followed you into a city apartment before you started a working."

Pathwell slid off the hood. "That's the short version."

"It's the useful version. Tell me the order."

"It was already at the door before I used anything," Pathwell said. "Then the working broke, and once the thing got through the door, it went for the waste."

Elizabeth raised a hand. "I broke it. I grabbed the cookbook while he was using it."

"So after it arrived, it behaved normally."

"That's what I said."

"No. You said something came through a door and tried to kill you."

"That is a more compelling version."

"It's also a worse description."

Elizabeth looked between them. "Normally?"

Stansbury looked at her rather than at Pathwell. "Things like that clean up loose magical waste. Failed workings. Unsettled messes. If you don't stand between them and what they're after, they're unpleasant rather than murderous."

"That would have been useful information yesterday."

"Yes." Stansbury tapped one finger against the Cadillac's paint. "The part that doesn't fit is why it was already at your door."

"Her door," Pathwell said.

Stansbury looked at him. "Was it?"

Pathwell's expression flattened. Elizabeth almost asked whose door it was, if it wasn't hers. She didn't.

She poked at the box instead. Foam swords lay packed inside, bright handles and blunt gray blades, with smaller pieces underneath: daggers, short batons, two round shields with smiling dragons printed on them.

"What's with the swords?"

"Those are his weapons," Pathwell said, with an extra dismissive tone on weapons.

"That you need," Stansbury corrected. "Out of this week's stock."

"What are we going to do with a bunch of foam swords?" Elizabeth reached into the box, and Stansbury smacked the back of her hand. She snatched it back.

"We are going to do nothing. These aren't toys for you to play with."

Elizabeth rubbed the back of her hand, confused and annoyed. "They're foam."

"Stansbury is very sensitive about his swords, Lizzy. Be nice."

"We used to play with these as kids. What are they going to do for us now?"

"They're magic," Stansbury said. "They don't need to be pointy."

"Oh, come now, Stansbury, the girl didn't know your noodles are actually very dangerous," Pathwell said, poking at the box, and Stansbury snatched it back from him too.

He took one of the swords and stepped out from under the branches. The foam glowed a bright orange, interlocking plates of light wrapping it from the base up. He wound up and struck the trunk. The tree shook, its leaves wobbling, and every drop of the morning's rain came loose from its branches and poured down on Elizabeth and Pathwell. Stansbury stood there dry, with the look of a man who expected them to be impressed.

"What was that for?" Elizabeth said, while Pathwell laughed.

"These can do a lot more than you think." Stansbury pointed the sword at her. "You don't need to know any magic to use one. You hit the thing you mean to hit, and it does what it was made to do. They aren't indestructible. If one cracks, splits or starts heating when it shouldn't, stop using it."

He put the sword back and lifted one of the daggers, short enough to hide under a coat, gray blade, black handle, a loop at the pommel.

"This one cuts separation into things that don't separate easily. Adhesions. Clinging effects. Certain magical bindings."

"People?" Elizabeth asked.

"It's designed not to." He laid it back in the box.

"What do you do here, anyway? Do you teach?"

Stansbury laughed. "Oh, heavens no. I haven't actually taught a class in years."

"Then what do you do all day?"

"This and that," Pathwell said dismissively. "He steals children's energy."

Stansbury's lips pursed and his eyes narrowed. "That feels a bit reductive, even for you." He turned to Elizabeth. "I harness the latent magic energy created by the children's work and redirect it into items. Then I sell those items to interested buyers. I don't steal anything. The kids get their work back, the parents get to put it up on the fridge and then toss it in a box in the attic for a decade. It would never be used otherwise."

"That feels… harmless… I guess?" Elizabeth let the words mumble out, looking to Pathwell. "It doesn't really feel any different from what you did at the bookstore. Selling off potential nobody was going to use."

Pathwell slid the box across the hood toward his brother. "All right, Stansbury, you can stop showing off and put your noodle away."

"Always so crass, Pathwell." Stansbury collected his things, walked around to the driver's side and put the box in the back seat. "Well?" He gestured to the doors.

"Shotgun!" Pathwell called, moving past Elizabeth so quickly he bumped her against the Cadillac at the waist. He had the door open before she finished regaining her balance.

"Pathwell!" She gathered herself, walked around to the back of the car, and slammed the door.

Her sleeves were still damp from the tree. They rode in silence through the small town, mostly because the radio only worked when it wanted to, and partly because that annoyed Pathwell.

Stansbury glanced once at Pathwell's right hand before he pulled away. "How thin?"

Pathwell looked out the window. "He said I didn't have much left to give."

"He who?"

"The shopkeeper."

Stansbury's jaw moved once. "And you bought what?"

"Books."

"Of course you did."

"Paper too."

"That fixes it."

You could have driven through the town without noticing it, and perhaps most people did. The shops were plain, the sidewalks patched, and the houses had the determined look of buildings that planned to be there long after everyone had forgotten to remark on them.

Stansbury parked in the kicked-dust lot.

"What are we doing here?" Pathwell said, sticking just his head out of the window. "You brought us to a bar."

"I brought myself to a bar. You happen to be in my car. I just got off work, and I wouldn't mind a refreshment, Pathwell. Besides, I'm sure the poor girl could use a break after you've been dragging her around." Stansbury turned off the car and stepped out, looking back at Elizabeth in the back seat. "You're welcome to come in. Just bring one of those little foam daggers with you." He smiled.

Pathwell got out of the car through the open window and stretched his legs.

Elizabeth sat in the back, looking at the two of them, then at the poorly glowing neon sign in the bar's window, advertising a beer they no longer sold and she doubted anyone still made. It was late in the afternoon, and the parking lot was half full. It was hard to tell for which side, or whether the cars would just migrate from one to the other as the day went on.

The screen door slapped shut behind Stansbury.

Pathwell had stopped at the foot of the steps. He looked toward the bar, then past it, at nothing she could identify. Then the moment passed, and he came back to the car and noticed her hesitating.

"Hey. You're gonna be fine. Look, if you're worried, just bring one of Stansbury's little swords."

She heard Stansbury's voice in the back of her head. He lies, you know.

Pathwell looked at her, perplexed. "I don't lie."

That settled it. She took one of the foam daggers from the box beside her, the gray one Stansbury had held up at the school, and climbed out, cracking her head on the door frame. She felt around her clothes for somewhere to put it and settled on the back of her waistband, under her coat.

The air smelled of old beer and a sunbaked dumpster. The low sun scattered itself through the single tree that shaded parts of the lot.

The bar's thin screen door sat on a weathered deck, up a few short steps. It looked like the sort of door that had spent its entire working life being pushed open with somebody's elbow. Elizabeth looked around with one foot on the bottom step. Rows of corn across the road to her left, the scattered shelter of the town to her right. Pathwell was already up the steps, holding the screen door.

Elizabeth followed him inside.


Chapter Six

The bar wasn't nearly as dingy as she'd imagined. It sat as a point of pride for the owner and the patrons, neater than it should have been, despite showing its age. The plastic on the seats was worn from years of supporting customers too inebriated to do it for themselves. Most things in the bar looked about as worn. The television sat in the top corner of the room, to the right of the door, playing a baseball game with the sound off. The dartboard had been hit so many times it was more hole than board, and someone had patched the worst of it with a different color of cork.

A mounted fish glowered above the register. Whatever victories it had enjoyed in life, its last job seemed to be listening to people complain about their drinks. The bartender polished a glass beneath it.

Elizabeth found a seat at the bar.

"Good afternoon, ma'am. What will it be?"

She looked at him blankly. "I'm not too sure."

"Well, what do you normally have?"

She didn't have an answer for that either.

"Appletini for her," Pathwell said, "and a pair of something domestic for my brother here and I, if you will."

Elizabeth turned. "No."

Pathwell stopped mid-turn. "No?"

"I don't want an appletini."

"You haven't tried it."

"That hasn't stopped me from forming an opinion."

Stansbury sat down on her other side. "She's learning."

Pathwell looked from one to the other. "Fine. What do you want?"

Elizabeth looked at the bottles behind the bartender. "Ginger ale."

Pathwell stared at her. "We came to a bar."

"You came to a bar. I happen to be in it."

Stansbury smiled down at the bar.

Pathwell pointed at him. "This is spreading."

The bartender set down Elizabeth's ginger ale and the brothers' bottles. Pathwell reached his beer across her to clink Stansbury's, making her duck the neck of it. He appeared to consider that a successful toast.

"No idea what's up with him," Pathwell said. He drank, and turned away himself, and found the dartboard.

Stansbury watched his brother throw a dart without looking at the board. It landed two inches from the center.

Pathwell threw another dart. Bullseye. He immediately looked disappointed with it.

"He's going to pretend that was luck," Stansbury said.

"I was not."

"You were about to."

Pathwell pulled the darts free. "You two have become unpleasantly collaborative."

Elizabeth drank her ginger ale. Minutes passed without anyone rushing to close a magical doorway or asking her to flee. A man missed an easy pool shot and blamed the cue. For a little while his mistake was the worst thing happening in the room.

She watched the brothers. They could argue without paying attention and pay attention without looking. Stansbury knew when Pathwell was reaching for his fish before Pathwell himself had quite decided to do it. Pathwell knew which of his brother's answers were going to be no and asked anyway.

Stansbury ordered fried fish. Pathwell objected to the batter on philosophical grounds, then appeared at his brother's elbow the moment it arrived.

"How is it?"

"Good."

"Really?"

"No. I ordered it because I enjoy disappointment."

Pathwell took a piece from the basket. Stansbury slapped his hand with a napkin.

"Order your own."

"We're family."

"That has never entitled you to my fish."

"What, then, is family for?"

"Usually this argument."

Elizabeth laughed.

Pathwell looked at her. "You see what I endure."

"I've been with you for one night and I already think he deserves the fish."

"Betrayal."

The bartender put a second basket in front of Pathwell without being asked.

Pathwell looked at him. "You understand me."

"You ordered this last time."

"Less romantic, but acceptable."

Elizabeth looked at Stansbury. "How long ago was last time?"

"Don't ask questions that encourage him."

"Seventeen years," Pathwell said.

The bartender shrugged. "You complained about the batter then too."

"Consistency," said Pathwell.

"Stagnation," said Stansbury.

"Branding."

Down the bar, a thud. Elizabeth turned so sharply that her glass rocked under her hand. The bartender was crushing mint for somebody's drink. He raised the muddler, glanced at her, and brought it down again, this time rather more gently.

She decided she was tired, and turned back to her glass.

It was a decent time. The fact surprised her enough that she nearly spoiled it by thinking about why. She took another drink instead.

Stansbury waited until Pathwell went to get his darts back before he spoke, quietly.

"You chose to come with him from Camp?"

Elizabeth looked at him. "Yes."

"He didn't still have something of yours?"

"No."

"Nobody was chasing you?"

"No."

Stansbury nodded. "All right."

Elizabeth waited for the rest. There wasn't any.

"That's it?"

"What else did you want?"

"Most people seem to have an opinion about what I should do next."

"I have several."

"And?"

"You didn't ask for them."

Elizabeth looked at him for a second. "That's a surprisingly rare skill tonight."

Stansbury took a drink. He had managed to ask a question, listen to the answer, and leave her alone with it. Elizabeth found herself absurdly grateful.

"I can hear you," Pathwell called from the dartboard.

"That was deliberate."

Pathwell threw another dart. It missed the board completely and stuck in the wood paneling beside it.

Everyone looked at it. Elizabeth looked at Pathwell's hand. He flexed his fingers once.

"That," Stansbury said, "was not deliberate."

"The wall had been arrogant all day."

Pathwell crossed to the dartboard as if nothing had happened. At the last step he flexed the fingers of his right hand, then curled them around the dart so the movement became part of reaching for it. Elizabeth wasn't sure he'd meant her to notice.

Some time later Pathwell disappeared toward the back hall. Elizabeth assumed the bathroom, until a few minutes became longer than a bathroom should take unless something had gone badly.

Stansbury didn't look toward the hall, but he stopped eating.

"Does he do that?" Elizabeth asked.

"Do what?"

"Go quiet and disappear."

"He disappears constantly. Quiet is newer."

Stansbury put down his fork. He still didn't go after him.

The back hall was narrow, lit by one yellow bulb. A dead pay phone hung on the wall beside a mirror spotted dark around the edges where the silver had failed.

Pathwell stopped in front of it.

For a moment he saw exactly what he expected: his own face. Older than it looked. Tired around the eyes. Hair refusing several decades of advice.

Then the tension came back.

It had been with him in small ways for longer than he cared to count. A pull without a destination. A thread caught somewhere behind the ribs and drawn taut at odd intervals. Easy to dismiss as pruning damage, or exhaustion, or age, or one of the many consequences he had collected and declined to inventory properly.

This time it tightened hard enough to stop him.

He turned his shoulders slightly to the left, and the tension eased. He turned right, and it sharpened. Left again: loose. Right again: taut. Not a direction he trusted. Barely a direction at all. More the difference between pulling against a snag and moving with it.

In the mirror, his reflection looked ordinary.

Then, for one heartbeat, it didn't.

Nothing changed that he could name. Same coat, same face, same eyes. But the arrangement was wrong in the smallest possible way, like hearing your own voice on a recording and recognizing every word without recognizing the man who said them.

Pathwell leaned closer. His reflection leaned closer, perfectly. Of course it did. He felt ridiculous.

The tension tightened again when he angled toward the far wall, and he stopped testing it.

The apartment door. The blob already there before the cookbook. The way it had only made sense after Elizabeth broke the working. The long, intermittent tension he had filed under damage, because damage was easier than mystery.

His own face in the mirror suddenly felt less like evidence than like an accusation.

"No," he said quietly.

The mirror offered no opinion. Pathwell stood with his hand on the edge of the sink. He'd always considered silence one of his more reliable ways of ending a conversation. It was considerably less useful when he was the only one speaking.

For several seconds he stood alone under the yellow bulb. Then he straightened his coat and went back to the bar.

Elizabeth knew something had happened because Pathwell came back without talking.

He sat beside Elizabeth, moved his beer an inch to the left and then returned it. His right hand started toward his sleeve. He caught it by reaching for the bottle instead. Stansbury saw both movements.

Stansbury looked at it. "What?"

Pathwell looked toward Elizabeth, then toward the television. "Later."

Stansbury looked toward the back hall, then back at him.

Stansbury's face changed very little.

"Your brother said something didn't fit," she said.

Pathwell looked at her. "Yes."

"Does something fit now?"

He considered lying. She watched him consider it.

"Something points in an unpleasant direction," he said.

"Which direction?"

Pathwell looked down at his hand. "Mine."

Stansbury went completely still. Around them, the bar went on. Pool balls struck. The bartender laughed at someone near the register. The mounted fish went on withholding judgment.

"What does that mean?" Elizabeth asked.

"I don't know yet."

It sounded strange coming from Pathwell.

Stansbury pushed his basket of fish away. "Tell me."

Pathwell looked at Elizabeth again, then back at Stansbury. "Not here."

Not here. Not yet. She was beginning to understand the difference, and she didn't like how much practice she'd been given. For the moment she let him keep his silence.

Pathwell picked up a dart and didn't throw it. He turned it between his fingers instead.

Stansbury paid the tab. Two beers, one ginger ale, two baskets of fish, and a bowl of peanuts nobody remembered ordering.

Pathwell looked at the receipt. "You tipped too much."

"You tip too little."

"I tip historically."

"That doesn't mean anything."

"It has meant something in several currencies."

The bartender took the receipt away before the argument could become archival.

Outside, evening had settled over the parking lot. The sky was streaked with wisps of orange and red, and a bank of heavy clouds stood tall and wide at the open horizon, dropping long shadows over the far-off fields.

Elizabeth stopped at the bottom of the steps. "Red sky at night, sailors' delight," she said. "My mom used to always tell me that. It means tomorrow's going to be nice."

"We have to get there first, Mrs. Elizabeth," Pathwell said, skipping down the steps with a flourish. "We still have the night."

At the bottom he looked toward the Cadillac, then past it, to the far edge of the lot. Stansbury saw where he was looking. He took the keys from his pocket and held them out to Elizabeth.

She looked at them. "Why me?"

"Because I want the passenger seat."

"That's not an explanation."

"It's the useful version."

Pathwell looked at him. Stansbury looked back.

Elizabeth understood enough. He wanted to watch his brother. Possibly question him. Possibly stop him from opening a door in a moving car.

"You know how to drive?" Stansbury asked.

Elizabeth took the keys. "Yes."

"Good."

Pathwell pointed toward the Cadillac. "For the record, I am an excellent driver."

"No one asked," Elizabeth said.

Stansbury smiled. Pathwell looked betrayed.

Elizabeth closed her hand around the keys. Behind her, the screen door slapped shut. She looked at the Cadillac, then at the brothers, neither of whom seemed inclined to ask whether she wanted to drive. It was a small, ridiculous improvement that she had the keys at all.

Then, from the far side of the Cadillac, a thud.

Wet. Heavy.


Chapter Seven

The smell reached Elizabeth before the shape did.

Low tide. Wet rot.

The same low-tide stink that had crawled through her apartment door. She'd thought she was finally getting it out of her coat. Apparently it had come looking for the rest of her.

She shoved the keys into her coat pocket, and her hand went to the foam dagger at the back of her waistband.

Pathwell stopped halfway across the lot. No flourish, no half turn that invited a question. He simply stopped, and the way he did it told Elizabeth more than anything he'd said about being safe.

Stansbury saw it too. "Where?"

Pathwell looked toward the far side of the Cadillac. "I don't know."

Something black moved beneath the Cadillac. Not under it exactly. Around it. A dark wet fold gathering itself from the shadow under the chassis, too large to have been hiding there and too shapeless for the word hiding to help.

The blob rose beside the rear bumper.

Elizabeth took one step back. The thing didn't turn toward her.

It turned toward Pathwell, with no hesitation at all.

Pathwell's face changed. "Oh," he said.

The blob crossed the lot toward him.

Stansbury moved first. He yanked open the Cadillac's back door and had one of the foam swords out of the box before Elizabeth saw him reach for it, and he struck across the blob's leading edge. The sword sank halfway into blackness. The thing shuddered. It didn't stop.

Pathwell patted one pocket and then the other. His hand returned to the first pocket as though a second inspection might make a book appear. It didn't.

"You mean to tell me you brought zero books with you!?" Stansbury shouted.

"Yes! It slipped my mind! Leo, would you please focus!?"

The blob hit him at the waist and folded upward. One second he was there. The next there was only black shape and one hand still visible at the edge of it, fingers closing on nothing.

"Pathwell!" Elizabeth moved, and Stansbury caught her shoulder.

"Don't get close."

She looked at the mass swallowing his brother. "That's useful advice late."

The blob climbed over Pathwell's chest. His visible hand disappeared.

Stansbury struck again. The foam sword drove into the mass and came back slick with nothing Elizabeth could see. The blob pulled away from the impact, then closed again around the gap.

"It's not holding shape," Stansbury said.

Elizabeth had the dagger in her hand. A gray foam blade, black handle, ridiculous in a parking lot with something rotting its way toward a man. Her fingers tightened on it. The dagger didn't bend.

Cuts separation into things that don't separate easily.

Stansbury had said those words at the school as if everybody carried something for cutting separation. At the time she'd been too busy being annoyed with him to ask what he meant.

Elizabeth ran toward the blob.

"Elizabeth—"

She ignored him.

The smell hit hard enough to make her eyes water. Up close the thing had no surface she could focus on. Every edge slipped sideways when she looked directly at it. She could see Pathwell only in fragments: the sole of one shoe, the corner of his coat, a pale patch of wrist that vanished before she could reach it.

She drove the foam dagger into the line where blackness met the edge of his coat.

The dagger should have bent. It didn't. Something opened. Not flesh. A seam.

The blob recoiled from it in one violent ripple.

Elizabeth cut downward, and the seam followed the dagger. For one second she saw Pathwell's shoulder inside the black mass. Then his face. Eyes shut. Mouth open. Skin gray in the last of the red light.

She cut again. "Come on."

Pathwell didn't answer.

She caught the front of Pathwell's coat and pulled. Nothing. Her shoes slid on the damp grit. She reset her feet, dug in, and pulled again. His shoulder moved an inch before the blackness tightened around it.

She cut once more, across the band holding his ribs.

The thing released him all at once. Elizabeth fell backward, Pathwell came with her, and they hit the dirt together. His weight drove the breath out of her.

For half a second neither of them moved.

Then Pathwell coughed. Once. Twice. A wet, ugly sound that was still considerably better than silence.

Elizabeth rolled him off her. "Breathe."

Pathwell opened one eye. "That was the plan."

"Do it quieter."

"Criticism received."

His voice barely worked.

Elizabeth stayed on one knee beside him. Feeling was coming back into her fingers in pins and needles. A few yards off, Stansbury had changed tools. The sword lay in the dirt. In his hand was one of the short foam batons from the box, orange from end to end and ridiculous enough that on another day Elizabeth might have laughed.

The blob rolled toward Pathwell again. Stansbury stepped between them and struck the ground once with the baton.

Heat hit the parking lot. Dry, immediate, furnace-door heat. A line of orange-white light ran through the cracks in the dry ground beneath the blob, and the black mass convulsed.

Stansbury struck it directly.

For one instant flame moved through the thing without catching on anything else. The blob collapsed inward. Its shape broke into heavy wet sections that flattened, hissed and thinned until only black streaks were left on the dirt.

The smell stayed.

The parking lot went quiet. Inside the bar, someone sank a pool ball.

Someone had sunk a pool ball inside the bar. Elizabeth heard the satisfying little knock of it as she knelt in the dirt, trying to decide whether Pathwell was breathing properly. The game went on; nobody opened the screen door.

The screen door never opened.

Elizabeth looked at the place where the blob had been. "Is it dead?"

Stansbury set the baton down carefully. "That one is gone."

"That wasn't the question."

"It's the answer I have."

Pathwell pushed himself upright. His hands shook. Not the small tremor from the Space Between. Both of them now.

Elizabeth reached for his arm. He drew back a fraction, the beginning of his familiar objection, then put his weight on her. She didn't comment. There wasn't breath enough in either of them to make a worthwhile argument.

Stansbury came over and crouched in front of him. "Look at me."

"I'm familiar with the face."

"Pathwell."

Pathwell looked. Stansbury watched his pupils for a second, then pressed two fingers against the side of his neck.

"Anything missing?"

"Several dignities."

"Anything important?"

Pathwell looked toward the black streaks on the dirt. "No."

Stansbury followed his eyes. "It went for you."

Pathwell said nothing. Elizabeth was still holding his arm.

"Not me," she said.

Pathwell looked at her. "No."

He said it quietly. Not a dismissal this time, and not the beginning of a clever answer. Just no.

The first blob had come through her door. Pathwell had told her the apartment was marked. He had pulled her out of it because he was certain the danger belonged to her. This one had crossed a parking lot and swallowed him.

Elizabeth looked at the remains again. "So what does that mean?"

Stansbury looked at Pathwell. Pathwell looked away. Elizabeth had heard enough conversations conducted over her head to recognize one that didn't need words.

"The back hall," Stansbury said.

"The mirror," Pathwell said.

Elizabeth turned. "What mirror?"

Pathwell's jaw tightened. Stansbury stood.

"Not here," Pathwell said.

"You said that inside."

Pathwell got slowly to his feet. Elizabeth let go only when she was sure he could stand without her.

"I know," he said.

"Then where?"

Pathwell glanced toward the road. "In the car."

"Good. Then tell me in the car."

Neither brother answered.

Stansbury bent to pick up the foam sword and the baton.

"Stansbury."

He paused.

"I just cut him out of that thing."

"I know."

"Then stop talking around me."

Stansbury's expression changed, though not enough. "You're right," he said.

Pathwell looked at him. Stansbury looked back at Pathwell.

Then he said, "Let's get away from here first."

It was reasonable. That was the irritating part. Even angry, Elizabeth could see why they ought to get off a road where another of those things might appear.

Elizabeth looked down at the foam dagger in her hand. Black moisture clung to the blade and vanished before it reached the handle. She wiped it on the grass anyway.

Pathwell reached for the Cadillac's passenger door. Stansbury stopped him.

"Back."

Pathwell frowned. "I dislike the back."

"Survive it."

Stansbury opened the passenger door for himself.

Elizabeth looked at him. "You gave me the keys because you wanted that seat."

"I still do."

Of course he did.

Elizabeth got behind the wheel. The engine caught on the second turn. Pathwell sat behind her, and for several seconds nobody said anything.

She pulled onto the road the way the car was already facing. The bar disappeared behind them. Fields opened on either side, black beyond the headlights.

"Mirror," Stansbury said.

Pathwell shifted in the back seat. "The pull got stronger in the hall."

Elizabeth looked at him in the rearview mirror. "What pull?"

Pathwell's eyes met hers in the glass, then moved to Stansbury. "I've felt it before."

"How long?" Stansbury asked.

"A while. Off and on. Long enough that I stopped treating it as new."

Elizabeth tightened her hands on the wheel. "What pull?"

Stansbury turned slightly in his seat toward Pathwell. "Since the prune that went wrong?"

"Maybe."

"Which prune?"

Pathwell was quiet.

Elizabeth looked at the road. "Hello?"

"Drive," Pathwell said.

Her hands steadied on the wheel. He'd said drive the same way he'd said shoes back at the apartment: a quick instruction that made sense if she agreed to let him decide what mattered. She drove, because the road required it. The answer could wait.

She drove.

Stansbury went on. "And the mirror?"

"Nothing clean. My face felt wrong. The tension had direction if I tested it. Barely."

"Toward what?"

"I don't know."

"But then this one came for you."

"Yes."

"And the first one found you in her apartment before there was fresh waste."

Pathwell didn't answer. Elizabeth did.

"My apartment."

Neither brother responded right away. The Cadillac moved through the dark, and the road hummed under the tires. She had become very aware of how much machine she was responsible for.

"If whatever it keeps finding is close enough to you that it can't tell the difference—" Stansbury began.

"Not me," Pathwell said.

"Then tell me what is different."

"I can't yet."

Elizabeth laughed once. Both men stopped. Nothing was funny.

"There she is," Elizabeth said.

Pathwell looked at her in the mirror. "What?"

"The person driving the car."

"Lizzy—"

"Elizabeth."

"Elizabeth."

"I was apparently important enough to get dragged out of my apartment. Important enough to follow your cart through the woods. Important enough to give up Nana's cookbook. Important enough to bring along while you figure this out. Important enough to cut you out of that thing."

Pathwell's mouth opened. She kept going.

"But somehow every time we get to the part where somebody explains what the hell is happening, I become the driver."

Silence. Stansbury looked forward again.

Pathwell leaned between the seats. "We're trying to understand it."

"So am I."

"There are pieces you don't have."

"Whose decision was that?"

Pathwell stopped.

Stansbury rubbed one thumb along the edge of his seatbelt. "She's right," he said.

Pathwell sat back. "Yes."

Elizabeth waited. Nothing followed. The brothers didn't pick the conversation back up.

Elizabeth drove. The dagger pressed against the back of her waistband. Her hands were steady on the wheel. The headlights found a reflector, then a mile marker, then a long empty stretch of road with nothing coming the other way.


Chapter Eight

Elizabeth chose the ditch.

It was broad and shallow, cut along the edge of an empty field, with enough grass in it to suggest that other bad decisions had ended there before. The telephone pole farther up the road would have been worse. So would the trees beyond it. She had time to choose.

The Cadillac hummed under her hands. For almost a mile the brothers had been quiet, but it was not the easy quiet of people getting along. Stansbury watched the road. Pathwell watched Stansbury. Every time Elizabeth tried to catch either of their eyes in the mirror, somebody found a reason to look elsewhere.

Elizabeth watched the road unwind under the headlights.

"Which prune?" she asked.

Neither brother answered.

"Stansbury said 'the prune that went wrong.' Which one?"

Pathwell shifted in the back seat. "We don't know that it matters yet."

"That wasn't my question."

Stansbury looked out the passenger window, where there was nothing of particular interest except the dark. The Cadillac continued down the road, perfectly willing to do this for hours.

"Pathwell."

"Elizabeth."

"Which prune?"

"Not while you're driving."

Her hands tightened on the wheel. "Then we stop."

"There isn't anywhere to—"

Elizabeth took her foot off the accelerator.

Pathwell leaned forward between the seats. "Elizabeth, keep driving."

He'd finally gotten her name right. It didn't help.

"Or pull over and let me drive," Pathwell said. "I am an excellent driver."

Ahead, the road ran straight and empty for half a mile. A shallow ditch paralleled it on the right, with a gravel entrance over a culvert. Elizabeth looked at the two surfaces—the narrow strip of road where the car belonged, and the grass where it did not. Then she looked at the brothers again.

Elizabeth moved both hands higher on the wheel.

Stansbury looked at her and understood first. "Elizabeth."

"Seatbelt."

His eyes dropped to her seatbelt, already across her chest, then went to the back seat. "Put yours on," he told Pathwell.

Pathwell looked at both of them. "Why?"

Elizabeth turned the wheel.

The Cadillac left the asphalt. Gravel clattered beneath it. Stansbury threw a hand toward the dashboard while Pathwell swore in a voice that suddenly had no patience for discussions of responsible driving.

Then the right front wheel dropped into the ditch beside the culvert, and metal struck concrete.

The sound was enormous.

The front end stopped. Elizabeth's shoulder hit the belt, and Stansbury's hand came off the dash. In the back, the box of foam swords went over, and something struck the roof with a noise Pathwell would later insist had been the car and not him.

The engine coughed twice and stopped.

Dust drifted through the headlights. A hubcap rolled past the windshield, crossed the gravel entrance and fell into the grass. It made one last wobble on its edge, like a coin that couldn't decide which side it preferred.

Her heart was beating hard enough to make the steering column feel alive under her palms.

It was the crash that woke her.

Stansbury looked at her, then at the ditch, then at the front of his Cadillac, which was sitting about six inches lower than the rest of it.

"You broke my car," he said.

Elizabeth swallowed. "Yes."

Pathwell pushed himself upright in the back seat, from under several foam swords, one hand at the back of his neck. "You crashed the car."

Elizabeth turned around. "Yes."

"On purpose."

"Yes."

Pathwell stared at her. She'd prepared herself for anger and was almost disappointed by his open astonishment. Stansbury, by contrast, was looking at the front of the car as though he might still persuade it to climb out by itself.

Elizabeth unfastened her seatbelt. "Now we're stopped."

Stansbury leaned over and turned the key. The engine gave him nothing. He tried once more and got a dry click.

"Very stopped," he said.

"I'm sorry about the car."

Stansbury looked through the windshield at the grass where the hubcap lay. For a moment Elizabeth thought he was calculating the cost. Then she saw that he was just looking at his car.

"I believe you."

"I can pay for—"

"You cannot."

"Fair."

Pathwell shoved at the rear door. It stuck halfway against the slope of the ditch, and he pushed harder. "Okay. I understand that you might be upset, but this is an extraordinary overreaction."

Elizabeth got out on the driver's side. "So was pulling me out of my apartment because you decided a monster was after me."

"That seemed reasonable at the time."

"So did the ditch."

Stansbury made a sound that might have been a cough.

Pathwell looked at him. "Do not encourage her."

"My axle is in a drainage culvert. I'm evaluating several positions."

Elizabeth climbed onto the road. Her knees were beginning to shake, late to the business of being frightened. She took a step to prove she could and very nearly had to take a second one to stay upright.

The night was bigger outside the car: fields on both sides, a line of trees far back from the road, stars starting to show through breaks in the cloud.

Behind her, Stansbury climbed carefully out of the passenger side with one hand pressed to his ribs.

"Are you hurt?"

"Bruised."

"I really am sorry."

"I know."

At the farm entrance, the post the Cadillac had clipped leaned out over the gravel, dragging its barbed wire with it. For a moment all Elizabeth could think about was how upset the farmer was going to be when he saw it.

Pathwell finally got the rear door open far enough to crawl out. He stood on the slope beside the Cadillac and looked at her, and for a moment nobody was talking around her.

Elizabeth pointed at him. "The prune."

Pathwell looked toward Stansbury.

Elizabeth let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "You really can't help yourself."

"I'm thinking."

"You always seem to do that in someone else's direction."

Pathwell looked back at her. Then he went still so abruptly that she stopped talking. He wasn't looking at her anymore. He was looking past her, down the road.

Stansbury followed his eyes. "Again?"

Pathwell nodded once.

Elizabeth turned. Headlights had come around the bend behind them. One car, moving slowly, on an otherwise empty road.

"Perfect," Elizabeth said.

The car slowed further when its headlights found the Cadillac in the ditch. An old dark sedan rolled onto the shoulder fifty yards back and stopped. For several seconds nobody got out. Then the driver's door opened, the dome light came on, and a man stepped out at the edge of his own headlights.

Elizabeth's first thought was that Pathwell had somehow gotten down the road without her seeing. Her second was that the idea made no sense. Her third arrived in her body before either of the first two had finished.

Wrong.

Not dangerous, not yet. Wrong in the peculiar way a familiar room changes when somebody moves one thing and insists nothing is different. Elizabeth tried not to stare, which made the staring worse.

He was the same height, and near enough the same face that the distance swallowed the differences.

He shut the sedan door. His right arm lifted away from his side and the hand opened toward Pathwell. It wasn't a wave. His jaw tightened, and he caught his own wrist with his other hand and pushed it down.

Pathwell took a step forward, staring at him, and stopped.

Elizabeth looked from one to the other. "No."

Nobody answered.

"Absolutely not."

The man walked up the shoulder toward them and stopped a car's length away. Up close, he wore a plain dark coat, buttoned correctly. His hair lay flatter. He carried less of Pathwell's forward momentum, as though he'd learned to stop before he walked into rooms.

Stansbury moved closer to Elizabeth, though not in front of her.

The man looked at Pathwell. Not at Elizabeth, and not at Stansbury. His face held recognition without any welcome in it.

"You," he said.

Pathwell's face had gone very still. "Apparently."

The man looked down at his right hand, closed it into a fist and put it in his coat pocket. Pathwell watched it go.

Stansbury broke the silence. "You know him?"

"No," Pathwell said.

The other man said, at almost the same time, "Not exactly."

Elizabeth closed her eyes for a second. "That's a terrible start."

The stranger looked at her for the first time: at her face, at the Cadillac in the ditch, at her again. No recognition.

"Are you hurt?" he asked.

"No."

"Bruised," Stansbury said.

The stranger gave a small nod, just enough to show that he'd heard Stansbury. He wasn't asking for a follow-up, and he wasn't presenting himself for anybody's inspection.

Pathwell stepped up onto the road. The tension in his posture changed with the distance between them. Not compulsion. Awareness.

"How did you find me?" he asked.

The man's mouth tightened. "I didn't."

Pathwell waited.

"Something keeps telling me which way you are."

Elizabeth looked at Pathwell. He didn't look back.

"Not who. Not where. Which way," the stranger said. "I can ignore it for a while. Then it gets worse." His right hand moved inside his coat pocket, as if to prove it. "So I drove."

Stansbury's attention sharpened. "How long?"

"Long enough to be tired of it."

"Days? Weeks?"

"Long enough."

Pathwell almost smiled. The stranger saw it. "Don't."

The smile went.

"Name?" Stansbury asked.

A pause. "Shade."

"First name?"

"Enough name."

Pathwell made a small sound through his nose.

Shade looked at him. "You have opinions about naming?"

"Several."

"Keep them."

Elizabeth looked back and forth between them. If you had drawn Pathwell from memory, got distracted halfway through, and finished with someone else's idea of him, you might have made this man.

Same ingredients. Different recipe.

The thought made her unexpectedly angry.

"The prune," she said.

Pathwell looked at her. Shade looked at Pathwell. Stansbury looked at the road.

Elizabeth spread both hands. "Really? Again?"

Shade's attention came back to her. "What prune?"

Elizabeth laughed. "Excellent. The one person here who looks like he should know doesn't."

"I know pieces," Shade said.

Pathwell's eyes snapped to him. Shade noticed, and looked almost pleased, though not kindly.

"Pieces of what?" Stansbury asked.

Shade took his right hand out of his pocket. It opened a few inches toward Pathwell at once, and he shoved it back in. "Him."

The word sat on the road between them.

Pathwell took another step closer. Shade stepped back. "Don't."

Pathwell stopped, for once immediately. Shade watched him long enough to be sure the stop was real.

"I remember things I did not live," Shade said. "Places I have never been. People I have never met. Habits that arrived before I had anything to be habitual about."

Pathwell's face had lost whatever was left of its performance. "Current memories?"

"No."

"Since when?"

Shade looked irritated. "I don't know your current life. I don't know what you did yesterday. I don't know her." He nodded once toward Elizabeth. "I know things that were already there when I started."

Stansbury looked at Pathwell. "Started."

Shade's jaw set. "Existing. Pick a word."

Nobody did.

"Do you know what he did?" she asked Shade.

Pathwell turned toward her. Shade didn't answer right away.

"Some of it."

"Enough to explain?"

"Enough to know he won't do it cleanly."

Pathwell's expression hardened. "You don't know me."

Shade looked at him. "I know old versions of your excuses."

Stansbury stepped between the sentence and whatever might have come after it. "Nobody knows enough yet."

Shade nodded. "Correct."

That seemed to annoy Pathwell more than the insult had.

Elizabeth rubbed the place where the seatbelt had caught her shoulder. The adrenaline was thinning, and the cold was moving in underneath it. Her hands had started to shake.

Shade noticed her rubbing the bruise where the belt had caught. He looked once toward the wreck, then toward the road. "There's coffee somewhere along this road."

Elizabeth looked at him. "Is that an invitation?"

"No. I'm getting coffee."

He turned back toward his sedan.

Pathwell moved, not toward Shade but toward Elizabeth. "You're not going with him."

It came with such familiar certainty that for a second she nearly obeyed it out of reflex. Then she heard it.

"Was that a question?"

Pathwell stopped. "You don't know who he is."

"Neither do you."

"That is exactly the problem."

"No. It's one of them."

"Then I'm coming with you."

Shade had reached his car. He looked back over the open door. "No."

Pathwell turned to him.

"Not in my car." Shade's right hand was back in his pocket. "Not you."

He got in and waited for no one.

Elizabeth looked at Stansbury. He was standing beside the wreck of his Cadillac with one hand against his ribs, and he didn't tell her what to do.

She reached back for the foam dagger and held it out to him, handle first.

Stansbury looked at it, then at the sedan. "Keep it."

She tucked it back into her waistband and looked at Pathwell. "You had all night to tell me what you knew."

"I don't know what he knows."

"Then I guess one of us should ask him."

Pathwell's face tightened. "Elizabeth."

She turned toward Shade's car. The wrongness was still there. She didn't trust Shade. She didn't even particularly like him. But she was tired, the road was cold, the Cadillac was dead, and the man in the sedan had pieces Pathwell wouldn't give her.

Shade started the sedan.

Elizabeth reached the passenger side, and her hand stopped on the handle. The front seat was too close. She opened the rear door instead.

Shade glanced at her in the mirror. "Front is empty."

"Back is fine."

He shrugged.

Elizabeth got in. The dagger pressed into the seat at the small of her back.

Through the windshield, Pathwell stood beside the wreck, his face caught in the sedan's headlights. The man who always seemed to be moving his hands had apparently forgotten what to do with them.

He looked afraid. Elizabeth almost got back out.

Then she remembered his voice in the Cadillac.

Drive.

Elizabeth shut the door.

Pathwell stepped back onto the shoulder to let them by. Beside the Cadillac, Stansbury, one hand still on his ribs, raised the other. Elizabeth raised hers.

Shade pulled out around them and past the wreck, and Elizabeth watched it through the rear window until the dark took it. Only then did she realize she was following another man who sounded certain.

Too late to call it an accident.


Chapter Nine

The radio glowed blue through the dark.

Elizabeth sat in the back because she'd chosen the back. The front seat was empty and available and probably more comfortable. She stayed where she was, watching Shade's eyes meet hers now and then in the mirror without asking her to move.

The road ran through fields and small towns that seemed to have closed hours ago. A gas station with one light still on, as though someone inside had decided it wasn't worth the trouble to turn off the last one. A church sign announcing last week's fish fry. A motel vacancy sign missing the V. Elizabeth liked that somebody had left the sign up anyway. A sign could only do so much for a place.

Shade drove with both hands on the wheel when he could. Every few miles his right hand loosened and lifted slightly toward the side window, as though somebody just outside the car had requested his attention. He closed it again, sometimes on the wheel and once under his thigh. Elizabeth waited until she'd watched it happen three times.

"That him?" Elizabeth asked.

Shade looked at her in the mirror. "What?"

"Whatever your hand keeps doing."

His eyes went back to the road. "Yes."

"And right now?"

His right hand flexed once on the wheel. "Behind us."

Elizabeth looked through the rear window. Nothing but dark road.

"Is he following us?"

"No idea."

"Do you know what he's thinking?"

Shade glanced at her again. "No."

"Okay."

That nearly got a smile out of him. "You ask efficient questions."

"I've had an inefficient night."

He nodded as if that made sense.

A song came on the radio, an old one from when she was a kid, and Elizabeth knew the words without remembering when she'd learned them. The melody had kept its place in her head all these years without ever asking whether she still wanted it there. She sang them in her head until her humming gave her away. Shade was tapping the wheel along with the beat, singing under his breath, and when he lost the words in the second verse he heard her and stopped. He didn't say anything about it.

After another mile he said, "You don't have to sit back there. Front seat's still empty."

"So is the seat I'm in. Mostly."

"Fair."

He checked the mirrors at every turn and tapped the wheel twice before making it. She caught herself waiting for the taps. The face was Pathwell's; the habits were beginning to belong to someone she had met tonight.

The diner sat on the far side of a gravel lot under a yellow sign that said DINER 24/7. Shade pulled in and turned off the engine.

"Coffee," he said.

"You really meant that."

"I usually do."

Warm windows. Three cars. A waitress in a blue-and-white uniform behind the counter. Elizabeth could stay in the sedan. She could walk down the road. She could go inside and sit somewhere with a door that, so far as she knew, stayed a door. Each possibility appeared equally ordinary, which felt novel enough to consider.

Shade was already halfway to the door. He didn't look back to see which one she chose.

She got out.

The bells above the diner's door gave a weary little jangle when she went in. They sounded as if they'd already greeted everyone they intended to greet that evening and were fulfilling an obligation.

"Sit anywhere."

Shade had taken a booth by the window. Elizabeth took the seat across from him, close enough to the aisle that standing up would need no negotiation.

The waitress arrived with menus under her arm and a pot of coffee in her hand. She balanced both while pulling a pencil from behind one ear, performing three small tasks at once without making any of them look clever.

"Coffee?"

Shade turned his cup upright. Elizabeth hesitated, then turned hers over too.

The coffee arrived black and steaming. Elizabeth tasted it. Burnt grounds, an old pot and water that had spent a long time traveling through a machine nobody loved enough to clean. She set down the cup, then looked at it again.

It was terrible, and it was the Space Between's coffee all over again. Late nights spent studying. Something in Elizabeth's shoulders came down anyway.

Shade noticed. "Bad?"

"Impressively."

"Good."

"That's not the normal response."

"You looked less tired after you drank it."

Elizabeth stared at him.

Shade lifted both hands slightly. "Observation. Not memory."

She believed him, mostly because he'd bothered to make the distinction.

The waitress came back with a pencil behind one ear. "What can I get you?"

Shade ordered eggs, toast, and the coffee he already had. Elizabeth opened the menu and ordered cheesy grits with extra pepper, bacon, and two eggs sunny side up. The waitress wrote it down and left.

Elizabeth folded the menu shut. "So."

Shade looked out the window. "So."

"You don't know me."

"I met you on the road twenty-five minutes ago."

"Keep remembering that."

"I'll make a note."

Pathwell would have smiled at the joke and waited for someone to admire it. Shade went on holding his cup. It took her another second to realize he wasn't interested in whether she'd laughed.

"What do you remember?"

Shade turned the coffee cup between his hands.

"Fragments."

"Try some nouns."

Shade looked down at the coffee. "A room in Prague I have never entered. A woman named Celia who hated pears. Three ways to cheat at a card game I have never played. The smell of a stable before rain. A staircase with a loose fourth step."

Elizabeth waited. "Useful things?"

"Sometimes."

"A life?"

Shade's hand stopped on the cup. "No."

He looked toward the window again. "There are too many pieces and not enough between them. I don't remember being him. We used to—"

He stopped. His jaw tightened.

"He used to," he corrected.

He used to. Elizabeth looked at the fork in his hand, the plate waiting under it, the coffee cooling beside it. There was a whole person at this table whose memories kept trying to introduce themselves as somebody else's.

"Do you want to remember being him?" Elizabeth asked.

Shade's eyes came back to hers. "No."

The waitress brought their food, the plates landing with a tap and a clank. After an evening of asking questions nobody wanted to answer, Elizabeth appreciated a plate that could simply be put down in front of her.

She went for the bacon first. It was burnt at one end and nearly right at the other, and she chewed, and chewed, and closed her eyes for a second. It was exactly how she'd have made it.

Shade salted his eggs before he tasted them. Pathwell would have done that. Probably.

"The blobs," Elizabeth said.

Shade looked up. "What about them?"

"One came through my apartment door while he was standing in my living room. Another one swallowed Pathwell in a parking lot."

Shade's fork stopped. "Swallowed him?"

"Yes."

"And he got out?"

"I cut him out."

Shade looked at the foam dagger where it lay beside her in the booth, then back at her. "Good."

Elizabeth frowned. "That's all?"

"Would you prefer applause?"

"No."

"Then good."

He went back to his eggs. Elizabeth let him have two bites.

"You drove toward him because your hand told you to."

Shade's jaw tightened. "I drove because it wouldn't stop."

His right hand opened against the tabletop, palm turning toward the window and the road behind them. He closed it into a fist.

"No," he said quietly.

It was for the pull itself. He kept the fist closed until it passed.

"You can resist it," she said.

"Yes."

"Does it hurt?"

"Eventually."

"Then why not go back?"

Shade looked at her as if the answer were obvious. "Because I don't want to."

Elizabeth looked down at her grits. An hour ago she'd steered a Cadillac into a ditch because she was tired of being told what she was allowed to know. She couldn't decide whether she understood Shade's answer or was only pleased to hear it.

"Why did you come toward Pathwell in the first place?"

"I told you. The pull."

"Nothing about me?"

Shade's expression changed, closer to irritation that the question was necessary than to sympathy. "I did not know you existed."

Elizabeth sat very still.

"Pathwell brought me along because he thought the thing at my apartment was after me."

Shade put down his fork. "Why?"

"It came through my door."

"With him inside."

"Yes, with him inside." Elizabeth looked at him. "You think it was after Pathwell?"

"I know what keeps pulling me toward him." Shade glanced at his closed hand. "I don't know what brought that thing to your door."

She replayed the parking lot. The second blob had gone straight for Pathwell. At the school he'd insisted, Her door; Stansbury had replied, Was it? Pathwell had told her that her apartment was marked, and she'd been busy trying to decide whether she believed in marks at all.

Pathwell saying marked, and pulling her into the hallway because he'd already decided the danger belonged to her.

"What if the first one wasn't after me either?" she asked.

Shade took another drink of bad coffee. "Then you were never the point."

Elizabeth looked at him.

She leaned back against the booth. "So what was?"

"I don't know."

"You?"

"Maybe."

"Pathwell?"

"Maybe."

"Both?"

"That would explain why the cleanup keeps making bad decisions."

"Cleanup?"

"That's Stansbury's word."

"Of course Stansbury has the sensible noun." Then it caught up with her. "You know Stansbury."

"I remember him." Shade corrected himself at once. "Fragments of him. Before me. Not now."

"Does he know you?"

"He met me on the road twenty-five minutes ago."

Elizabeth pointed her fork at him. "Don't start doing that."

Shade smiled, but only briefly, with one side of his mouth. For the first time Elizabeth stopped trying to determine which part of the expression he'd inherited.

Elizabeth put the fork down. "What did Pathwell do?"

"I don't know all of it."

"What do you know?"

"That I started with pieces of him already inside me."

"Because of a prune."

"Probably."

"Probably?"

Shade looked down at the table. "I remember the edge of one. The shape of work. His certainty. A problem he had decided belonged to him. Then the pieces stop lining up."

"What was he certain about?"

"I don't know."

"What did he choose?"

Shade looked up. "That's his answer."

"Convenient."

"Accurate." His voice had gone flatter. "The part I remember clearly is the feeling before it."

"What feeling?"

"Certainty."

Elizabeth stopped.

"Not that he was right," Shade said, to the table. "That he had reached the point where being uncertain no longer mattered to him."

The coffee machine clicked behind the counter. The waitress laughed at something a customer said. Elizabeth sat there for a moment with all the things she could ask and none of the reasons to trust their answers.

Elizabeth thought about Pathwell in her apartment saying marked. Pathwell at Camp, arriving with books nobody had asked him to buy. All of it could look generous from one angle.

"Why are you telling me?"

Shade's fingers tightened around the cup. "Because he'll listen to you longer than he'll listen to me."

Elizabeth let out a short laugh. "That's an extremely low bar."

"Still a bar."

"So I'm leverage."

Shade didn't insult her by pretending otherwise. "Partly."

"And the other part?"

He looked toward the road. "I'm tired of carrying pieces of an answer that belong to a question I never asked."

Elizabeth looked at him for a long moment. "You want something from him."

"Yes."

"What?"

"For him to stop deciding what I am before I get to."

Elizabeth looked down at her own hands.

"And what am I supposed to ask?"

Shade met her eyes. "Don't ask me for his confession."

"Then what?"

"Ask him what he chose."

Elizabeth waited.

Shade looked at the window, at his own dim reflection laid over the parking-lot lights. "He knows what he did. Ask him."

Elizabeth sat with that.

The waitress came by with the pot. "Warm you up?"

"No, thank you," Elizabeth said.

Shade held his cup out, and the waitress filled it.

Elizabeth looked at him. "I'm going back."

Shade nodded. "All right."

"I'm not doing it for you."

"I know."

"You don't get to know that yet."

Shade considered the correction. "Fair."

Elizabeth slid out of the booth. "I assume you're paying for this."

Shade already had his wallet out. "I was going to pay for mine."

"I don't have any money. All I have to my name right now is my diary and a foam dagger."

Shade looked at her plate, then at the dagger she'd laid beside her in the booth, and produced his wallet. He counted out enough for both meals and the tip, checking the total twice with the care of a man who had no intention of owing the diner anything after he left.

Elizabeth took the last triangle of toast off his plate. Shade let her.

Elizabeth asked the waitress the way to the bar back toward the fields, the one with a fish over the register.

"That'd be the one out on the county road." The waitress gave her the road number and three turns, then repeated the second one because Elizabeth's face apparently asked her to. She wrote them on the back of the receipt in blocky capitals, underlined the second left after the water tower twice, and handed it over.

Shade watched, and didn't offer to drive her.

Elizabeth tucked the foam dagger into the back of her waistband and went out.

Outside, it was still full night, and the cold got into her sleeves. She stood under the yellow sign with the receipt in her hand. She didn't know where Pathwell was. She did know where the night had started.

Behind the glass, Shade sat alone with his coffee. His right hand opened on the table, and he closed it again.

Elizabeth started walking toward the road. She had a question now, and this time she intended to make him answer it.


Chapter Ten

She'd walked a little over an hour. The empty road ran between fields dark enough to hide the fences, and the trees were still some distance away. Elizabeth kept to the shoulder facing traffic, a rule she'd learned at nine and never had occasion to be so thankful for until now. Her shoes were less grateful.

The receipt from the diner was folded in her pocket: three turns in the waitress's blocky handwriting, the second left after the water tower underlined twice. It was the most reliable set of instructions anybody had given her since the night began.

The foam dagger rested against the back of her waistband. She'd moved it there before leaving the diner and checked for it twice since, which was more attention than she'd ever expected to give a toy weapon before sunrise.

Her shoulder ached where the seatbelt had caught it. The shoes had developed complaints of their own, and the diner's coffee still sat unpleasantly at the back of her tongue. There were several things she might have liked to go back and collect, starting with a night's sleep.

There was only cold road, bad shoes, the folded receipt, and the question Shade had left with her.

What did Pathwell choose?

She walked toward the bar because it was where the night had started, and the wreck was somewhere on the way. If Pathwell wasn't there, she would decide what came next when she got to that.

While Elizabeth was still at the diner, Stansbury and Pathwell were walking home. Stansbury lived two miles from the ditch. This hadn't saved the Cadillac. It had only made the walk home shorter.

They started after the sedan's taillights vanished, Stansbury with the box of foam swords under one arm and the other hand pressed to his ribs. He changed the box from one side to the other twice before finding an arrangement that was merely painful.

"We have to find Elizabeth," Pathwell said.

"Okay. And?"

"She is our responsibility, Leo."

"She's your problem, Pathwell. I'm just here for you."

"I'm well aware."

Stansbury looked at him sideways. "Can you find her?"

Pathwell looked back down the road. The weak tension was still there, pulling the way the sedan had gone. Shade, not Elizabeth.

"No."

"Good."

"That was a strange response."

"I wanted to make sure you knew the difference."

"The draw finds him."

"Barely."

"Barely is still him," Stansbury said. "And not her. So don't use it like it is."

Pathwell looked offended. "I had not planned to."

"So what's your plan?"

Pathwell pinched the inside corners of his eyes. "I don't have one."

Stansbury snorted.

"Yet. I don't have one yet. I do my best work on the fly. You know that."

Stansbury laughed to himself.

"What?"

"I've seen you work. I didn't know that was your best." He shifted the box to his other arm. "What you need is to sit down until your hands stop doing that. Plus, I'm pretty sure I have a letter of longing stacked away somewhere, and a couple of grocery lists."

Pathwell stopped in the road. Then he smiled.

"Leo, the next time I call you an idiot, you smack me right in the face."

"The next time you what?"

"Call you an idiot."

Stansbury's open hand caught him across the cheek.

Pathwell pursed his lips. "I suppose I should have seen that coming."

The second slap came from the other side.

"And what was that for?"

"Bank it."

"That's not how it works."

"Let's roll, big cat," Stansbury said.

"Please don't start calling me that again."

"Too late, grande gatto."

Stansbury's house was unlocked, as usual. People in town knew one another, which made the lock less of a practical obstacle and more of an expression of faith in the neighbors. Pathwell followed him inside without asking permission. Some things, apparently, were more reliably known than others.

Stansbury set the box down in the hall and opened the freezer.

"Peas."

Pathwell was leaning on the kitchen table with both hands. "Is that a diagnosis?"

"It's what I have." Stansbury tossed him the bag.

Pathwell caught it against his chest and winced. "Your bedside manner has declined."

"My car is in a drainage culvert."

"She did apologize."

"I know."

"You sound remarkably calm."

"I have not reached the axle emotionally."

Pathwell put the peas against the back of his neck and followed him down.

The stairwell divided the house into two half flights, one rising and one descending. Downstairs was Stansbury's library. He opened the door and reached for the light; the room was already crowded with bookcases, boxes and the particular order of someone who knew where every item belonged, even when it appeared to be blocking the path.

"Children's stories? Leo, you mustn't be so desperate."

Stansbury snatched it out of his hand. "Copies. Not all of us are willing to mortgage our potential to that shopkeeper of yours."

He set a flat archival box on the table instead. Pathwell read the label.

ELLISON ESTATE — PERSONAL CORRESPONDENCE — ORIGINALS COPIED

"You kept those?"

"I keep many things. Quietly."

"That's my line."

"No. You keep receipts until they become archaeological sites."

Stansbury opened the box. The letters inside were separated by sheets of thin paper. He passed over several and stopped at one folded into quarters, with no envelope and no stamp, the blue ink gone brown at the edges.

Pathwell didn't touch the letter. He set his hand beside it, careful of the folded edge. The longing in the paper reached him before his fingers reached it, and he withdrew his hand just enough to keep from picking the thing up.

"Unsent," he said.

"Yes."

"Unanswered."

"By definition. She wrote to the same man for six years and mailed none of them. He married somebody else in year two. Her niece sold the papers with the rest of the estate. I copied everything before I separated the originals."

Pathwell looked at the folded page. "If I use this, it's gone."

"I know."

"The copy keeps the words."

"Not the thing."

Pathwell's hand stayed beside the letter.

"You asked me once why I copy before I buy," Stansbury said.

"I remember."

"This is why."

"That doesn't make destroying it free."

"No." Stansbury nudged the letter toward him. "It makes the cost mine to approve."

He pulled out a chair and sat Pathwell in it.

"Now. You're going to have to be thinking of the same thing she is, at the same time, for this to work. A shared experience works best."

"How am I supposed to manage that?"

"You just have to get lucky."

Pathwell stared at him. "This is really the best chance we've got?"

"Well, unless you were smart enough to put a tracker on her to begin with." Stansbury looked at him for a moment. "Didn't think so. So you sit here, and you think of a moment, a phrase, anything she might be thinking too, and you keep at it until one catches. Normally that's all there is. But because I'm better at this than you—"

He waited. Pathwell sighed.

"You are better at this than me."

Stansbury patted his shoulder. "Good job. When it catches, I'll have the lists ready, and they'll do the rest. They're somewhere in here." He looked around at the city of paper. "Probably."

He went up the half flight, and Pathwell heard him start opening drawers in the room overhead.

Pathwell put two fingers on the fold. For once he made no announcement of what he was about to do. Stansbury had already gone upstairs for the lists; nobody was there to object or admire him. The paper waited under his hand.

The ink warmed under his fingers. The first line began to fade. It didn't burn and it didn't glow; the handwriting simply loosened from the paper, as if the page had stopped being able to remember it.

Pathwell closed his eyes.

The longing came through first, and it wasn't his. A woman's patience worn raw around one particular absence. A porch in summer. The idea of a train arriving, and the knowledge that it probably wouldn't. The impressions were incomplete and private and already fading at the edges, and he worked with the shape of them rather than the details: someone absent, someone specific, a connection that mattered because it had actually existed.

The letter disappeared under his fingers. The blank paper went after it.

Now it needed something real between the two of them.

Neon in wet pavement. Dumplings. Elizabeth on the curb outside the Chinese place, looking at him like he'd personally invented inconvenience.

Nothing. He let it go.

Camp. The tea that tasted like bark arguing with itself. The turnip boy trying and failing to keep his sock above his ankle. Elizabeth had been there with him, but he couldn't find her in the memory.

Nothing.

The archive wagon, and Elizabeth watching her grandmother's cookbook go onto the reserve shelf. The working tightened and slipped. Too much of that one belonged to her and not enough to him.

He tried again.

The Space Between. The cat. Elizabeth in the chair with a hundred lives she'd never lived still pressing on her. A white mug in his hand. *Just in time.* Her face after the first swallow.

*That's terrible.*

*I know.*

Something in the working caught, the way two threads catch when they finally cross at the same point.

Pathwell opened his eyes. "Stansbury!"

Overhead, a drawer stopped. Stansbury's head appeared at the top of the half flight. "What?"

"It caught."

"Really?"

"What do you mean, really?"

Stansbury came down the steps with three scraps of paper in his hand. "I just didn't expect it to work so soon. Or at all, for that matter."

"Why do you keep sounding so surprised?"

"Never mind. What caught?"

"A crappy cup of coffee."

Stansbury looked at him for a long second. "Of course it did."

"We both remember it."

"You hope."

Pathwell looked at the empty place on the table where the letter had been. "No. She's thinking about it. Right now, somewhere, she's drinking something terrible."

"He did say coffee," Stansbury said. "On the road."

"It's a long road."

Stansbury's expression sharpened. "Can she feel this?"

"No."

"Can you make her turn around?"

"No."

"Can you talk to her?"

"No."

"Good."

"Again with the strange enthusiasm."

"I am developing standards."

"Dangerous at your age."

The connection was already starting to loosen. Pathwell held out one hand. "List."

The first one had been written on the back of an envelope in blunt pencil.

MILK
ONIONS
ASPIRIN
STAMPS
SHOE POLISH

"Thrilling," Pathwell said.

"Authentic."

"I can feel the shoe polish."

"Try to survive."

The grocery list held little feeling. Someone had written it to get through a day: milk, onions, stamps, shoe polish. They must have stood in a kitchen with the pencil in hand, deciding what they would forget if they didn't write it down. Pathwell felt the intention move from one errand to the next, a route with no particular importance except to the person who had needed it.

Pathwell put his fingers on it. The pencil marks darkened, and the paper softened, and for several seconds the borrowed sense of going somewhere had nothing to attach to. Then it found the thin thread of the coffee.

Pathwell turned his head. Nothing had moved it. He simply knew which road came next, in the same practical way the writer of the list had known which shop came after milk.

"East," he said.

Stansbury picked up the keys to the work van, and the box. "How far?"

"No idea. Moving."

"Better."

The grocery list faded completely, and Pathwell followed his brother up the stairs.

Elizabeth reached the water tower and checked the receipt. Second left.

The road there was narrower, patched in strips, with a drainage ditch on either side and telephone wires sagging between the poles. She turned.

It was her decision. The road did not reward her with a new view, only another ditch and a sore heel. She checked the receipt again, counted the telephone poles to the next bend, and kept walking.

A mile later the heel of her right shoe started to rub. She stopped beside a mailbox and took the shoe off. The back of her heel was rubbed red but not bleeding.

"Excellent," she told it.

The mailbox declined responsibility.

She put the shoe back on and kept walking.

Stansbury's work van was white, square, and honest about being a work van. It had never tried to look fast, expensive, or particularly interested in the opinions of other vehicles. One rear door was a slightly different white from the rest. The passenger floor held two screwdrivers, a coffee thermos, three zip ties, and a foam shield with a smiling dragon on it. The box of swords rode in the back.

Pathwell sat with the peas against his neck and the second shopping list on one knee.

"How long does this one last?" Stansbury asked.

"Until it doesn't."

"I continue to regret asking you questions."

They passed the first crossroads, and the direction began to weaken. Pathwell waited. Then it was gone.

"Pull over."

Stansbury did.

Pathwell picked up the second list.

SOAP
THREAD
COFFEE
PEACHES
KEROSENE

He looked at Stansbury. "Coffee."

"Do not anthropomorphize the evidence."

"I was making conversation."

"That's worse."

Pathwell activated the list, and the words disappeared one at a time. The echo of somebody's errands settled against the same thread. Road. Turn. Continue. Nothing about Elizabeth's thoughts, or what she wanted. Only where the next step in the sequence pointed.

Pathwell opened his eyes. "Left. Soon."

"There's a water tower up ahead." Stansbury put the van in gear. "That I can use."

Elizabeth heard the engine before she saw the van. She moved farther onto the shoulder. The vehicle came up behind her on the far side of the road and slowed: white, square, one rear door a different shade.

For half a second she considered stepping into the ditch. Then she saw Stansbury behind the wheel.

The van rolled past her and stopped on the far shoulder. The passenger door opened, and Pathwell got out with a bag of peas, mostly thawed, held to the back of his neck. He set it on the roof and had to put a hand against the door before he walked toward her. He looked worse than she did.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey."

Elizabeth looked at the van, then at him. "How did you find me?"

Pathwell glanced at Stansbury, stopped himself, and looked back at her. "A letter that deserved better, two grocery lists, and the worst cup of coffee I've had this century."

Elizabeth stared. "The Space Between."

"Yes."

Her stomach tightened. "Did you put that memory in my head?"

"No."

The answer came too fast. Elizabeth heard it. Pathwell heard it too, apparently, because he stopped before adding anything clever.

"I couldn't. You had to be remembering something we both actually remembered. I kept trying shared things until the working caught one. The lists told us where to look next, for a little while. That's all."

Elizabeth looked at Stansbury through the windshield. He lifted one hand from the wheel.

"I asked the same questions," he said through the open window.

"Repeatedly," Pathwell added.

"Because you repeatedly require supervision."

Elizabeth looked back at Pathwell. He was waiting, looking for the shape of the conversation. She didn't give him one.

"Tell me what you did," she said.

He went quiet.

"Shade said you know." She held his eyes. "I'm not asking you whether it's a good idea to tell me. I'm not asking what you think I can handle. I'm asking what you did."

Pathwell put his hands in his pockets. "Lizzy—"

"No."

He stopped. "Elizabeth," he corrected.

"You don't get to make me the part of this you explain around."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "Okay."

Elizabeth went past him to the van and opened the side door. The metal was cold beneath her palm. Stansbury looked as though he wanted to say something, but she climbed in before he could decide what.

Pathwell came around, collected the peas from the roof and climbed into the passenger seat. "Camp," he said.

Elizabeth looked at him. "No."

Stansbury kept the van in park.

Pathwell turned. "No?"

"Not later. Not at Camp. Now."

Nobody moved. Outside the van, the road continued doing what roads did, offering somewhere else to go. Elizabeth was tired of letting it do the work of ending an argument.

Pathwell rubbed both hands over his face. "I pruned ahead."

Elizabeth watched him. "That isn't an answer."

"No." He looked down at the bag of peas in his lap. "It's the answer I use when I don't want to say the other one."

Stansbury said nothing. Elizabeth waited.

Pathwell moved the peas from one knee to the other. "Someone asked me for help."

"Shade?"

"No. Before Shade."

"What did they ask you to do?"

Pathwell's mouth tightened. "Help."

Elizabeth stared at him. "You are doing it again."

"Yes."

That stopped her for half a second.

Pathwell looked out through the windshield. "They had a problem. A real one. They came to me because I knew things they didn't, and because I had a reputation for getting unpleasant things finished."

"Did they ask you to prune?"

"No."

The van seemed smaller. Elizabeth looked at Stansbury. He was looking straight ahead.

"Did they know you were going to?"

"They knew I was going to handle it."

"No." Pathwell's thumb pressed a dent into the plastic bag. "They did not ask me to prune. I did not ask whether they wanted me to."

"Why?"

"Because I thought I knew the cleanest way through it."

"And the prune was yours."

Pathwell looked at her. "Yes."

"Your future."

"Mine."

Elizabeth leaned back against the metal side of the van.

"So what did pruning do?"

Pathwell took a breath. "Nothing by itself."

Stansbury looked at him then, and Pathwell nodded once, as if agreeing to keep the explanation honest.

"I built a working around the result I wanted. The working could accept future possibility as payment. I decided that was the quickest way to settle the problem, and I paid with mine."

"Quickest."

"And cleanest, I thought."

Elizabeth almost laughed. She didn't. "What went wrong?"

Pathwell's attention dropped to his hands. "The payment released. The working didn't settle cleanly."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

"You don't know, or you don't want to tell me?"

Pathwell looked up. "I don't know. I know what malformed pruning looks like. I know what unclaimed possibility looks like afterward. I know there was more of it than there should have been. I know I failed to contain all of it."

"And then Shade."

Pathwell's expression changed, less defense in it and more of something Elizabeth didn't trust herself to name. "And then Shade," he said.

"You made him."

Pathwell didn't answer quickly. "My choice produced the failure he came from," he said. "That much is mine."

"But you don't know how the failure became a person."

"No."

"Does anyone?"

"Not that I've found."

"Could you do it again?"

"I wouldn't know how."

"Would you try?"

Pathwell closed his eyes for a moment. "No."

Elizabeth watched him open them again.

"He has a thing in his hand that keeps pointing him back at you."

"Yes."

"The blobs keep confusing the two of you."

"Apparently."

"And you still don't know exactly what he is."

Pathwell started toward an answer, and stopped. "No."

Elizabeth looked down at her hands. Shade at the diner: *For him to stop deciding what I am before I get to.*

She looked back at Pathwell. "He thinks you'll decide for him."

Pathwell shifted in the passenger seat. "I heard him."

"That's not the same as listening."

"No."

It was so quiet she almost missed his no. She let it stand. A moment ago he would have reached for a second answer to make the first more flattering.

Then she said, "And paying for it yourself made you feel like the method was yours to choose."

Pathwell looked at her, and didn't answer. Elizabeth waited. Stansbury did too.

Finally Pathwell said, "At the time, I would have called that responsibility."

"What do you call it now?"

His hand tightened around the peas. "I don't know yet."

Elizabeth shook her head. "No. You know enough. Whatever happened after that became Shade. But you still chose it."

Pathwell looked down once. Then back at her.

"Yes."

Elizabeth breathed out.

Stansbury loosened one hand from the wheel. "Where now?"

Pathwell began turning toward Stansbury. Elizabeth watched him catch himself halfway through, the little motion so familiar by now that she knew what question was coming before he said it.

Elizabeth said, "Camp."

Both brothers looked at her. She'd expected Pathwell to answer first. He didn't.

"Why Camp?" Stansbury asked.

Elizabeth looked out the side window at the dark fields. "Because Mama Baga doesn't belong to either of them. Neither does Camp. Mama Baga didn't ask me anything I didn't want to tell her."

Stansbury nodded once. "Camp, then."

Pathwell looked at Elizabeth. "There isn't a reliable threshold from here."

She waited.

He caught himself. "The nearest one I know," he said more carefully, "is at the museum. The graffiti wall. If it's still carrying enough history, it can anchor the door."

Stansbury started the van.

Elizabeth looked at him. "You know the place?"

"Unfortunately."

The van pulled back onto the road, and Elizabeth settled against the side panel.

"Museum," she said.

Stansbury turned east. Pathwell didn't correct the route.
