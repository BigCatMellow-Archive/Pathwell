Chapter Nine

The radio glowed blue through the dark.

Elizabeth sat in the back because she'd chosen the back. The front seat was empty and available and probably more comfortable. She stayed where she was, watching Shade's eyes meet hers now and then in the mirror without asking her to move.

The road ran through fields and small towns that seemed to have closed hours ago. A gas station with one light still on, as though someone inside had decided it wasn't worth the trouble to turn off the last one. A church sign announcing last week's fish fry. A motel vacancy sign missing the V. Elizabeth liked that somebody had left the sign up anyway. A sign could only do so much for a place.

Shade drove with both hands on the wheel when he could. Every few miles his right hand loosened and lifted slightly toward the side window, as though somebody just outside the car had requested his attention. He closed it again, sometimes on the wheel and once under his thigh. Elizabeth waited until she'd watched it happen three times.

"That him?" Elizabeth asked.

Shade looked at her in the mirror. "What?"

"Whatever your hand keeps doing."

His eyes went back to the road. "Yes."

"And right now?"

His right hand flexed once on the wheel. "Behind us."

Elizabeth looked through the rear window. Nothing but dark road.

"Is he following us?"

"No idea."

"Do you know what he's thinking?"

Shade glanced at her again. "No."

"Okay."

That nearly got a smile out of him. "You ask efficient questions."

"I've had an inefficient night."

He nodded as if that made sense.

A song came on the radio, an old one from when she was a kid, and Elizabeth knew the words without remembering when she'd learned them. The melody had kept its place in her head all these years without ever asking whether she still wanted it there. She sang them in her head until her humming gave her away. Shade was tapping the wheel along with the beat, singing under his breath, and when he lost the words in the second verse he heard her and stopped. He didn't say anything about it.

After another mile he said, "You don't have to sit back there. Front seat's still empty."

"So is the seat I'm in. Mostly."

"Fair."

He checked the mirrors at every turn and tapped the wheel twice before making it. She caught herself waiting for the taps. The face was Pathwell's; the habits were beginning to belong to someone she had met tonight.

The diner sat on the far side of a gravel lot under a yellow sign that said DINER 24/7. Shade pulled in and turned off the engine.

"Coffee," he said.

"You really meant that."

"I usually do."

Warm windows. Three cars. A waitress in a blue-and-white uniform behind the counter. Elizabeth could stay in the sedan. She could walk down the road. She could go inside and sit somewhere with a door that, so far as she knew, stayed a door. Each possibility appeared equally ordinary, which felt novel enough to consider.

Shade was already halfway to the door. He didn't look back to see which one she chose.

She got out.

The bells above the diner's door gave a weary little jangle when she went in. They sounded as if they'd already greeted everyone they intended to greet that evening and were fulfilling an obligation.

"Sit anywhere."

Shade had taken a booth by the window. Elizabeth took the seat across from him, close enough to the aisle that standing up would need no negotiation.

The waitress arrived with menus under her arm and a pot of coffee in her hand. She balanced both while pulling a pencil from behind one ear, performing three small tasks at once without making any of them look clever.

"Coffee?"

Shade turned his cup upright. Elizabeth hesitated, then turned hers over too.

The coffee arrived black and steaming. Elizabeth tasted it. Burnt grounds, an old pot and water that had spent a long time traveling through a machine nobody loved enough to clean. She set down the cup, then looked at it again.

It was terrible, and it was the Space Between's coffee all over again. Late nights spent studying. Something in Elizabeth's shoulders came down anyway.

Shade noticed. "Bad?"

"Impressively."

"Good."

"That's not the normal response."

"You looked less tired after you drank it."

Elizabeth stared at him.

Shade lifted both hands slightly. "Observation. Not memory."

She believed him, mostly because he'd bothered to make the distinction.

The waitress came back with a pencil behind one ear. "What can I get you?"

Shade ordered eggs, toast, and the coffee he already had. Elizabeth opened the menu and ordered cheesy grits with extra pepper, bacon, and two eggs sunny side up. The waitress wrote it down and left.

Elizabeth folded the menu shut. "So."

Shade looked out the window. "So."

"You don't know me."

"I met you on the road twenty-five minutes ago."

"Keep remembering that."

"I'll make a note."

Pathwell would have smiled at the joke and waited for someone to admire it. Shade went on holding his cup. It took her another second to realize he wasn't interested in whether she'd laughed.

"What do you remember?"

Shade turned the coffee cup between his hands.

"Fragments."

"Try some nouns."

Shade looked down at the coffee. "A room in Prague I have never entered. A woman named Celia who hated pears. Three ways to cheat at a card game I have never played. The smell of a stable before rain. A staircase with a loose fourth step."

Elizabeth waited. "Useful things?"

"Sometimes."

"A life?"

Shade's hand stopped on the cup. "No."

He looked toward the window again. "There are too many pieces and not enough between them. I don't remember being him. We used to—"

He stopped. His jaw tightened.

"He used to," he corrected.

He used to. Elizabeth looked at the fork in his hand, the plate waiting under it, the coffee cooling beside it. There was a whole person at this table whose memories kept trying to introduce themselves as somebody else's.

"Do you want to remember being him?" Elizabeth asked.

Shade's eyes came back to hers. "No."

The waitress brought their food, the plates landing with a tap and a clank. After an evening of asking questions nobody wanted to answer, Elizabeth appreciated a plate that could simply be put down in front of her.

She went for the bacon first. It was burnt at one end and nearly right at the other, and she chewed, and chewed, and closed her eyes for a second. It was exactly how she'd have made it.

Shade salted his eggs before he tasted them. Pathwell would have done that. Probably.

"The blobs," Elizabeth said.

Shade looked up. "What about them?"

"One came through my apartment door while he was standing in my living room. Another one swallowed Pathwell in a parking lot."

Shade's fork stopped. "Swallowed him?"

"Yes."

"And he got out?"

"I cut him out."

Shade looked at the foam dagger where it lay beside her in the booth, then back at her. "Good."

Elizabeth frowned. "That's all?"

"Would you prefer applause?"

"No."

"Then good."

He went back to his eggs. Elizabeth let him have two bites.

"You drove toward him because your hand told you to."

Shade's jaw tightened. "I drove because it wouldn't stop."

His right hand opened against the tabletop, palm turning toward the window and the road behind them. He closed it into a fist.

"No," he said quietly.

It was for the pull itself. He kept the fist closed until it passed.

"You can resist it," she said.

"Yes."

"Does it hurt?"

"Eventually."

"Then why not go back?"

Shade looked at her as if the answer were obvious. "Because I don't want to."

Elizabeth looked down at her grits. An hour ago she'd steered a Cadillac into a ditch because she was tired of being told what she was allowed to know. She couldn't decide whether she understood Shade's answer or was only pleased to hear it.

"Why did you come toward Pathwell in the first place?"

"I told you. The pull."

"Nothing about me?"

Shade's expression changed, closer to irritation that the question was necessary than to sympathy. "I did not know you existed."

Elizabeth sat very still.

"Pathwell brought me along because he thought the thing at my apartment was after me."

Shade put down his fork. "Why?"

"It came through my door."

"With him inside."

"Yes, with him inside." Elizabeth looked at him. "You think it was after Pathwell?"

"I know what keeps pulling me toward him." Shade glanced at his closed hand. "I don't know what brought that thing to your door."

She replayed the parking lot. The second blob had gone straight for Pathwell. At the school he'd insisted, Her door; Stansbury had replied, Was it? Pathwell had told her that her apartment was marked, and she'd been busy trying to decide whether she believed in marks at all.

Pathwell saying marked, and pulling her into the hallway because he'd already decided the danger belonged to her.

"What if the first one wasn't after me either?" she asked.

Shade took another drink of bad coffee. "Then you were never the point."

Elizabeth looked at him.

She leaned back against the booth. "So what was?"

"I don't know."

"You?"

"Maybe."

"Pathwell?"

"Maybe."

"Both?"

"That would explain why the cleanup keeps making bad decisions."

"Cleanup?"

"That's Stansbury's word."

"Of course Stansbury has the sensible noun." Then it caught up with her. "You know Stansbury."

"I remember him." Shade corrected himself at once. "Fragments of him. Before me. Not now."

"Does he know you?"

"He met me on the road twenty-five minutes ago."

Elizabeth pointed her fork at him. "Don't start doing that."

Shade smiled, but only briefly, with one side of his mouth. For the first time Elizabeth stopped trying to determine which part of the expression he'd inherited.

Elizabeth put the fork down. "What did Pathwell do?"

"I don't know all of it."

"What do you know?"

"That I started with pieces of him already inside me."

"Because of a prune."

"Probably."

"Probably?"

Shade looked down at the table. "I remember the edge of one. The shape of work. His certainty. A problem he had decided belonged to him. Then the pieces stop lining up."

"What was he certain about?"

"I don't know."

"What did he choose?"

Shade looked up. "That's his answer."

"Convenient."

"Accurate." His voice had gone flatter. "The part I remember clearly is the feeling before it."

"What feeling?"

"Certainty."

Elizabeth stopped.

"Not that he was right," Shade said, to the table. "That he had reached the point where being uncertain no longer mattered to him."

The coffee machine clicked behind the counter. The waitress laughed at something a customer said. Elizabeth sat there for a moment with all the things she could ask and none of the reasons to trust their answers.

Elizabeth thought about Pathwell in her apartment saying marked. Pathwell at Camp, arriving with books nobody had asked him to buy. All of it could look generous from one angle.

"Why are you telling me?"

Shade's fingers tightened around the cup. "Because he'll listen to you longer than he'll listen to me."

Elizabeth let out a short laugh. "That's an extremely low bar."

"Still a bar."

"So I'm leverage."

Shade didn't insult her by pretending otherwise. "Partly."

"And the other part?"

He looked toward the road. "I'm tired of carrying pieces of an answer that belong to a question I never asked."

Elizabeth looked at him for a long moment. "You want something from him."

"Yes."

"What?"

"For him to stop deciding what I am before I get to."

Elizabeth looked down at her own hands.

"And what am I supposed to ask?"

Shade met her eyes. "Don't ask me for his confession."

"Then what?"

"Ask him what he chose."

Elizabeth waited.

Shade looked at the window, at his own dim reflection laid over the parking-lot lights. "He knows what he did. Ask him."

Elizabeth sat with that.

The waitress came by with the pot. "Warm you up?"

"No, thank you," Elizabeth said.

Shade held his cup out, and the waitress filled it.

Elizabeth looked at him. "I'm going back."

Shade nodded. "All right."

"I'm not doing it for you."

"I know."

"You don't get to know that yet."

Shade considered the correction. "Fair."

Elizabeth slid out of the booth. "I assume you're paying for this."

Shade already had his wallet out. "I was going to pay for mine."

"I don't have any money. All I have to my name right now is my diary and a foam dagger."

Shade looked at her plate, then at the dagger she'd laid beside her in the booth, and produced his wallet. He counted out enough for both meals and the tip, checking the total twice with the care of a man who had no intention of owing the diner anything after he left.

Elizabeth took the last triangle of toast off his plate. Shade let her.

Elizabeth asked the waitress the way to the bar back toward the fields, the one with a fish over the register.

"That'd be the one out on the county road." The waitress gave her the road number and three turns, then repeated the second one because Elizabeth's face apparently asked her to. She wrote them on the back of the receipt in blocky capitals, underlined the second left after the water tower twice, and handed it over.

Shade watched, and didn't offer to drive her.

Elizabeth tucked the foam dagger into the back of her waistband and went out.

Outside, it was still full night, and the cold got into her sleeves. She stood under the yellow sign with the receipt in her hand. She didn't know where Pathwell was. She did know where the night had started.

Behind the glass, Shade sat alone with his coffee. His right hand opened on the table, and he closed it again.

Elizabeth started walking toward the road. She had a question now, and this time she intended to make him answer it.
