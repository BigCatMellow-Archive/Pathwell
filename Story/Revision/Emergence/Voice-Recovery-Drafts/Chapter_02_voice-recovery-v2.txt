Chapter Two

"Will you slow down!?"

Elizabeth struggled to keep up as the man charged down the stairs, taking them two at a time with the confidence of someone who had never considered falling down them.

"What about all my stuff?"

"Replaceable."

"My stuff?"

"Yes."

They burst through the lobby and out onto the street, where the cold night air struck Elizabeth hard enough to steal what little breath she'd managed to keep.

She stopped on the sidewalk, doubled over, and tried to get it back.

The man continued several steps before noticing he'd lost her.

Looking up, Elizabeth could see her apartment window. The light was still on.

She'd been there for three weeks. Three weeks. Twenty-one days of unpacked boxes, takeout dinners, and trying to work out what came next. She'd barely started putting things away, never mind making the place feel like somewhere she lived.

Her feet almost turned back on their own.

The only thing she'd properly unpacked was Nana's mixing spoon. She'd left it on the kitchen counter, intending to find a drawer for it eventually. It was an ordinary wooden spoon, worn smooth in the middle and darkened along the handle, and she'd carried it across the city wrapped in a dish towel because she hadn't trusted the movers with it.

Now it was sitting in an apartment without a front door.

And there was that thing. Whatever that thing was.

The man came back toward her.

"You okay?"

"No," she snapped.

"Fair."

She straightened, pulling her coat more tightly around herself. Her hands were shaking, although whether from the cold or from everything that had just happened was anybody's guess.

"Where are you taking me?"

"There's a lovely Chinese spot just around the corner here."

She stared at him.

"I have a meeting at nine tomorrow."

"I swear it's really just around the corner."

"No, I have a meeting. At nine. In the morning."

"Right."

"My landlord is going to kill me. How am I going to explain the door? I have to get my dry cleaning before the meeting. Where am I going to get a new door? I don't have my wallet. Do you think someone's going to steal my stuff? Should I call the cops?"

The questions came faster than she could consider the answers. Each one seemed to bring two more along with it.

He nodded politely through the whole thing, his gaze drifting up toward the streetlight above them. He had the patient expression of someone listening to a particularly long explanation of a problem he had no intention of solving.

Elizabeth ran out of breath before she ran out of questions.

"This is all a dream," she said. "Tell me it's all a dream."

He pinched her arm.

She slapped his hand away.

"Why would you do that?"

"It's not a dream. Just a really weird Tuesday for you."

"It's Thursday."

"Which makes it all the weirder."

He turned and started walking again.

Elizabeth remained on the sidewalk, rubbing the spot on her arm and considering, not for the first time that evening, whether the man was an idiot.

She looked back toward her apartment.

A shadow filled the window.

It was there for only a moment, dark against the light she'd left burning.

She didn't stay to see if it moved.

By the time she caught up with him, he was turning the corner.

She followed, expecting to see the coffee shop she passed every morning on her way to work. It was a little place with expensive coffee and a woman behind the counter who had begun remembering her order, which Elizabeth had taken as one of the first encouraging signs that she might eventually belong in the neighborhood.

Except there was no coffee shop.

There was a laundromat.

An old one, with metal shutters pulled across the windows and a sign whose better days had clearly been behind it for some time.

Beyond that was a park.

A whole park, with trees and benches and a path disappearing between them.

Elizabeth stopped.

There had not been a park here yesterday. She was quite certain of it. Parks were difficult things to overlook, particularly when they occupied space previously devoted to several perfectly respectable buildings.

She looked back around the corner.

The street behind her was still there.

She looked forward again.

Three weeks she'd lived in this neighborhood, and she no longer recognized a single thing.

The man seemed entirely satisfied with the arrangement.

"So then, what'll it be? You more of a lo mein girl? I'm all about the dumplings myself. The dumplings are perfect here."

Elizabeth looked at him.

"You just destroyed my home, and now you're hungry?"

"I wouldn't say hungry. More peckish."

He considered the distinction for a moment, apparently finding it an important one.

"And for the record, I saved you. Most people are thankful when someone does that."

"Thankful!?"

"Well, eventually."

They arrived at a little Chinese shop open to the street, its windows crowded with photographs of food whose colors had faded into several variations of orange. A red neon dragon flickered above the counter, occasionally losing its head before finding it again.

The place smelled of frying oil, soy sauce, and the particular warmth of a kitchen that had been working long after most sensible people had gone home.

"Two orders of fried dumplings, please."

"I'm not hungry," Elizabeth said.

"Oh, these aren't for you."

He turned toward her, as though remembering some important point of etiquette.

"And I don't share."

"Then why am I here?"

"Company?"

He gave her a small, hopeful look, then returned his attention to the counter before she could object.

Elizabeth considered leaving.

She could turn around, walk back to her apartment, call somebody, or find a police officer and explain that a man had broken into her home, fought a monstrous collection of rotting jelly in her living room, and then brought her to a Chinese restaurant in a neighborhood that no longer appeared to be her neighborhood.

She couldn't imagine that conversation ending well.

Instead, she took a seat at one of the plastic tables along the curb.

The chair protested as she dropped into it, its thin legs bending slightly beneath her before deciding to cooperate. She folded her arms on the table and rested her head against them.

The cold worked its way through her coat.

She put her hands into her pockets, rubbing her fingers together for warmth. There were a few coins in there, a receipt, and something folded tightly enough to have survived several trips through the laundry.

A piece of paper brushed against her hand.

The ticket.

Elizabeth sat up.

Her dry-cleaning ticket.

She pulled it out, smoothing the yellow paper against the table.

She could still get her clothes.

The cleaners would have everything ready in the morning. She could pick them up on the way to the meeting. It would be tight, but she'd managed worse mornings than that.

She could still make it.

For the first time since she'd woken up, there was an actual problem with an actual solution.

She emptied the rest of her pockets onto the table, digging through the coins for enough money to cover the pickup. The bright yellow ticket sat on top of the little pile.

Then a truck roared past.

It came close enough to rattle the table and lift the edge of the ticket. The paper skittered across the plastic surface, slipped over the side, and caught a gust from the truck as it passed.

Elizabeth reached for it.

Her fingers missed by inches.

The ticket danced once along the curb, caught the gutter, and disappeared.

She stood so quickly the chair nearly went over behind her.

"No, no, no—"

She looked down the street.

Nothing.

She checked beneath the table, along the curb, even in the narrow gap between the chair legs, as though the ticket might have changed its mind and returned.

It hadn't.

"Okay," she said.

She stood with her hands open in front of her.

"Okay, I can just… I can…"

She could explain it to the cleaners. There would be a record. They would have her name. She could call in the morning, or—

Her breath caught.

The smell came back.

Wet. Rot.

It had followed her, clinging to the inside of her coat, waiting there beneath the cold air and the smell of frying oil.

She pressed her hands flat against the table.

She opened her mouth, but nothing came out.

It was a dry-cleaning ticket.

A small square of yellow paper. She knew that. She knew perfectly well how ridiculous it was to be standing on a sidewalk, surrounded by traffic and strangers, feeling as though she'd just lost something irreplaceable.

But there were the boxes. Three weeks of boxes she couldn't bring herself to unpack. Bare walls. Her grandmother's spoon on a kitchen counter she might never see again.

A life she'd packed into garbage bags and moved across the city because staying had felt worse than leaving, only to discover that leaving didn't feel like much of anything at all.

And now she couldn't even manage to keep hold of a piece of paper.

She wasn't sure when the tears started.

She only knew she couldn't stop them.

Elizabeth sat back down, buried her face in her hands, and let herself cry.

The city carried on around her.

Cars passed. Somewhere down the block a siren complained its way through traffic. A group of people walked by laughing about something one of them had said, their voices drifting away before she could make out the joke.

A taxi splashed through a puddle.

The neon dragon continued its struggle to remain entirely illuminated.

Nobody seemed particularly concerned that her life had come apart at a plastic table outside a Chinese restaurant.

Then the chair opposite her scraped against the pavement.

The man sat down with a tray of dumplings and the satisfied air of someone whose evening was finally beginning to improve.

He set Nana's cookbook and her diary on the table beside him.

He was just reaching for the food when he noticed Elizabeth.

He looked at her.

Looked at the dumplings.

Then back at her.

He sighed.

"Oh, fine, you can have a dumpling."

Elizabeth raised her head.

He had already selected one, though from the care with which he made his choice, she suspected it wasn't an easy sacrifice.

He placed it on her side of the tray.

"There."

She stared at it.

He sat back, apparently satisfied that the matter had been handled, and began eating.

Elizabeth wiped her face with the heel of her hand.

The dumpling sat between them, steaming faintly in the cold night air. Its edges were uneven, one side a little darker than the other, and a small pool of grease was collecting beneath it.

She watched the steam rise.

She had no idea what she was supposed to do with it.

The man inhaled his second dumpling, then a third, chewing with the enthusiasm of someone who'd been thinking about these things all evening.

He gave her elbow a little nudge.

"You gonna eat that?"

"No."

"Suit yourself."

He reached over with his fork and claimed the abandoned dumpling.

"I'm tellin' ya, Lizzy, you don't know what you're missing here."

"I've had dumplings before."

"Ah, but not these dumplings."

He said it with such complete sincerity that Elizabeth almost found herself wondering what could possibly distinguish them from any other dumpling.

Almost.

She wiped the last of the tears from her cheeks and looked across the table.

"Who the hell are you?"

He paused with another dumpling halfway to his mouth.

"Pathwell."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"First name?"

"Pathwell."

"Last name?"

"Don't need one. Saves everybody time."

He resumed eating.

Elizabeth waited for him to offer something more.

He didn't.

"So, where to next?" he asked.

She blinked.

"Excuse me?"

"The night is young, Lizzy! Where do you want to go?"

"Home."

Pathwell chuckled softly.

It was a small sound, barely more than a breath, but it landed badly.

Elizabeth straightened in her chair.

"You wrecked my house."

He looked up.

"There's pieces of some… thing all over the place. You won't let me go home. I have a meeting I cannot miss in the morning, nothing to wear to it, and I don't even know where we are anymore!"

She gestured toward the street.

"There's a whole park around the corner that wasn't there yesterday!"

"Lovely park, isn't it?"

"No!"

He lowered his fork.

"All you can think to do is get some crappy food and ask me what's next?"

Pathwell glanced down at the tray.

For the first time that evening, he appeared genuinely offended.

"First off, I wouldn't go back there tonight. But that's just me."

He picked up another dumpling.

"Secondly, these things are perfect."

Elizabeth stared at him.

"Perfectly crappy," he continued. "They aren't trying to be anything they're not. You have no idea how hard it is to find that kind of quality."

He took a bite, closed his eyes for a moment, and nodded to himself.

There was no arguing with that sort of conviction.

Elizabeth looked away.

On the other side of the street, a man was trying to persuade a reluctant dog to continue walking. The dog had settled firmly on the pavement and appeared to be winning.

For a moment she envied it.

Pathwell finished chewing and brushed the crumbs from his hands.

"That being said…"

He stood, gathering Nana's cookbook and her diary beneath his arm.

"It's up to you what you want to do next. The choice is yours, Lizzy."

He looked at her as if he'd just offered something generous.

Then he turned and walked away.

Elizabeth watched him go.

He didn't look back.

She sat beneath the flickering neon dragon, its red light washing over the empty chair and the little table where he'd left her.

The tray was still there.

One last dumpling sat on her side.

He'd left it for her.

She looked at it for a moment, then toward the street where he'd disappeared.

He'd told her she could choose.

She could go home, or at least try to. Find a police officer. Find a hotel. Find someone who could explain why the streets had moved and what had happened inside her apartment.

She could make her own decisions.

It was just that none of them seemed especially good.

The dragon flickered overhead.

And then she heard her again.

Lizzy.

The name came with a warmth she recognized before she understood it.

Butter and sugar. The heat of an oven. The smell of fresh rain drifting through an open kitchen window.

Her Nana, standing beside her.

Elizabeth's eyes moved to the empty chair.

Then the tray.

Then the street beyond it.

She looked back at the table, slowly at first, then all at once.

The cookbook.

Her diary.

She was on her feet before she'd finished the thought.

He still has it.
