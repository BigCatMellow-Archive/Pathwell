This is Shade's belief about his prospects, not proof of an established supernatural barrier. His brief participation in Camp life in Chapters 12–13, including sharing food, making coffee, playing cards and saying he wants to stay, can coexist with envy and resentment rather than resolving them.

**Author clarification:** “He knows his life is temporary, he knows about the blobs.” Shade understands that the cleanup creatures are a serious threat to his continued existence. That knowledge is why ordinary Camp life intensifies his bitterness: other people can imagine a continuing future here, whereas he cannot count on having one. His brief pleasure in meals, coffee, cards and a welcome does not eliminate his knowledge of what is pursuing him.

**Continuity guardrails:** The current Chapter 9 already has Shade discussing the creatures and the cleanup, while Chapter 15 depicts him making an informed choice in the creature's presence. ## Rung Q32 — A witnessed confrontation with Pathwell

**Author clarification:** Shade wants to confront Pathwell, make Pathwell face the consequences of his actions, and **have other people witness that confrontation and reckoning**. This is a concrete purpose distinct from the earlier, potentially exaggerated revenge impulse. Shade is not a mastermind with a long operational plan; his anger has a direction even if his methods are improvised.

**Reading:** Private admission alone may not satisfy Shade. He wants Pathwell's choices and their human cost to be seen by others instead of being treated as an unseen technical error. Do not assume he wants a specific punishment, the crowd to reject Pathwell, or a formal public trial; the author's stated desire is for others to *see it happen*. His original desire to make Pathwell acknowledge responsibility remains compatible with this public dimension.

**Continuity:** Chapter 14 already places Shade's refusal of reintegration and Pathwell's decision to proceed in front of Elizabeth, Stansbury, and Mama Baga. ## Rung Q38 — Shade's accusation: the hope Pathwell leaves behind

**Author's answer:** “That he hurts people without ever thinking of the consequences, he is too caught up with himself, and that he gives people hope and then moves on,”

**Interpretation:** Although the interviewer asked what Shade wants Camp to understand about **Shade**, the author described **Pathwell's pattern as Shade sees it**. Respect this correction instead of treating the pronouns as a confession that Shade knowingly makes these same choices. Shade's public charge is that Pathwell becomes absorbed in his own interventions, overlooks their costs to other people, raises hopes, and then departs while others remain with what he set in motion. This sharpens the already established issue of Pathwell taking over others' problems. The new **“gives people hope and then moves on”** dimension may be especially important to Elizabeth but should not be scripted as a specific abandonment scene without evidence or permission; the prior hypothetical “I'll be right back” scene was not approved. Shade may share the same weaknesses (Q36–37) without this making him an evil counterpart.

**Knowledge safeguard:** Shade has fragmentary inherited memories and separate experiences, not full current access to Pathwell's history. Before writing a specific public accusation or giving named examples, determine which instances Shade can actually know about. This is a view he wants Camp to witness, not an established omniscient verdict about everything Pathwell has ever done.
