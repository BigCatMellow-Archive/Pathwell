# Rules

## Status

**Writing reference: a general guideline for Sunday Morning stories in any setting.** This page owns every must and must-not for a Sunday Morning story: the tone guardrails, the tie to a larger story (when there is one), canon discipline and the worldbuilding rules that still apply. It ends with the setting palette, a guide to building a menu of places, festivals and habits from your own setting. It adds no facts about any world. Where a collection uses a setting's places, festivals, dishes or language features, the setting's owner pages stay authoritative, with their own status labels.

The [Framework](Sources/Framework.md) sets tone and stakes; this page applies it and records what the first collection learned about keeping inside it. See the [order of authority](README.md#order-of-authority).

## Tone guardrails

Every collection is Sunday Morning first (a [standing ruling](Decisions.md#standing-rulings)). Check every pass against the Framework's core promise, "You do not need to brace yourself", before checking it against the voice guide. The guide's "that was unexpectedly sad" is welcome only inside that promise.

- **One sad moment, said once.** Don't tell the same loss two or three ways. (In the first collection, one story told the same estrangement three times; it was cut to one.)
- **Then give the reader somewhere soft to land:** warmth, absurdity or food right after.
- **No public shaming.** When a character's mistake is revealed, lead with what they were doing right. (In the first collection, a builder whose swapped stone caused the trouble was first shown holding the roof up.)
- **Hardship is background, in one line.** No repeated "bad year" paragraphs from story to story. "Still fell elsewhere" gets one line, ideally carried by an object.
- **Stakes stay human-scale.** One tree, one stall, one boat. Characters don't list the region-wide damage. (In the first collection, a line about "every stand of forest" was cut.)
- **Every story has a meal, and a moment where nothing dramatic happens** (Framework §12 and §21).
- **Endings land warm.** A showman goes back to being a showman; a lonely morning ends with a dog to walk.

## Connecting to a larger story

*Optional. This section applies only if the collection belongs to a larger setting or saga. Stories that stand alone skip it.*

*"Connected, not driven" was set 2026-09-27 at the author's direction (a [standing ruling](Decisions.md#standing-rulings)). The earlier rule kept the saga as background only.*

When a collection sits inside a saga, every Sunday Morning story can be a **node in the larger web**: the saga's cause-and-effect chain of current events, schemes and ripple chains. The story is told from the ground, where the people involved can't see the web. This suits sagas whose plot moves through causal handoffs, where the saga's protagonist and ordinary people like them change the larger game without understanding it at first.

At story scale the pressure from the web is always a **nail**: the smallest unit of pressure from the larger web that lands in one community. A letter, a rumor, a delayed cart, a tightened loan, an offer to buy. A nail goes unnoticed because it looks like ordinary local trouble. If the setting's notes on the saga's schemes are themselves evidence rather than canon, treat them that way here too.

### What every story must carry

| Element | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| Current event | At least one of the saga's current events shapes the story's situation, not just its dialogue. |
| Nail | One small, concrete pressure from the larger web lands in the community. |
| Independent reasons | Every step has an ordinary reason a participant would give. The scheme's steps must survive ordinary scrutiny. |
| Attribution | Who is behind the nail (the saga's antagonist, hidden powers, ordinary life, or `UNKNOWN`), recorded **out of story** and provisional. Not everything is the antagonist; keeping that distinction is essential. |
| Local outcome | The nail falls or doesn't because locals act for local reasons. The world exists when the protagonists are absent. |
| Outward effect | The second- and third-order effect beyond the story, including how the antagonist or hidden powers could use it. Major events have second- and third-order effects. |

### What keeps it Sunday Morning

- **Stakes on the page stay local.** The larger web raises what the outcome *means*, not what the characters risk.
- **Nobody in the story sees the pattern.** Characters may repeat a rumor that explains things badly. No one exposes the hidden powers or the antagonist.
- **The antagonist never appears on the page as themselves,** and hidden powers never act as a named body. The saga's lesser figures, the people who carry the scheme's steps, may appear or be mentioned as ordinary people.
- **No on-page harm from the scheme.** Hardship can be context (a displaced family, a lost contract), never the story.
- **Saga readers get the irony; Sunday readers don't need it.** Deleting the thread should leave a working story. Keeping it should make the story matter more.
- **Nothing here fixes the saga's open questions,** such as the antagonist's identity, grievance or plan. Those stay open in the setting's owner pages.

Each story page owns its own Larger-world thread. The cross-story view (calendar, ripple chain, where the nail still fell) is on [Collection](Collection.md#the-web).

## Canon discipline

- Every story page is **Provisional**. Characters, new towns, businesses, customs and plot details invented for a story are not setting canon.
- The setting's owner pages remain authoritative. The festivals, town details and recipes a story uses carry their own status labels there, and some may themselves be provisional.
- Stories must not settle the setting's open questions, including open biography of the saga's main characters.
- New small places are fine. Name them with the setting's own naming logic and mark them **Provisional**.
- In the first collection, no story detail was promoted to canon.

### Promotion rule

A detail invented for a story (a town, a dish, a custom, a character) does **not** become setting canon by appearing in a story or a draft. If it should graduate, promote it into its owner page in the setting (for example the page for its region's towns, or for festivals and seasonal life) with an explicit status, and link back.

## The saga's main characters

*Applies if the collection belongs to a saga.*

The saga's protagonist can headline a Sunday Morning story, especially one whose arc already moves toward small situations. Keep their canonical constraints:

- Keep what the setting says they are *not* (not the cleverest, not chosen, not the rightful anything, if that's their canon).
- Keep where their influence comes from in canon, rather than handing them speeches or strategy the saga wouldn't.
- Their canonical weaknesses work well as gentle comedy and quiet heart.
- Anything the setting leaves open about them (exact age, biography, family, starting point) stays open. A story should not settle it in passing. If a draft needs their past, stop and ask the author.

Most Sunday Morning stories should **not** star them. The world exists when the protagonists are absent, and a town may solve its own problem before the hero arrives.

## Setting rules that still apply

The Sunday Morning mode relaxes stakes, not worldbuilding discipline. Keep the setting's own worldbuilding discipline. The rules that mattered most in the first collection were:

- **Regions and places are ecosystems, not caricatures.** A fishing town has woodworkers who aren't fishers; a forge town has farmers.
- **Stereotypes are character evidence.** A regional insult can be said to someone; the story should prove it wrong, or at least complicate it.
- **Institutions have constituencies.** A guild still has a stickler, a reformer and someone who just wants lunch.
- **Keep magic or technology as scarce as the setting says.** Most of these stories need none.
- **History is messy.** An old document, feud or festival split has a plausible, human origin.
- **Language habits belong to the speaker, not the region.** Show counterexamples. A habit never implies a people is smarter, colder or more spiritual.

## Checklist addendum

Run the Framework's [checklist](Sources/Framework.md#20-the-sunday-morning-story-checklist) first, then:

- [ ] *If the collection belongs to a saga:* does the story carry a current event, a nail, an out-of-story attribution, a local outcome and an outward effect?
- [ ] *If the collection belongs to a saga:* would the story still work with the thread deleted, and matter more with it kept?
- [ ] *If the collection belongs to a saga:* does no character see the pattern, and does the antagonist stay off the page?
- [ ] Is the setting one community, not a region?
- [ ] Is there a festival, season or material limit acting as the clock?
- [ ] Does at least one language or food habit recur as a running element?
- [ ] Does any stereotype appear only as a character's belief, and get complicated?
- [ ] Are new names, towns and customs marked **Provisional**, and registered in the collection's [Registry](Registry.md#names)?
- [ ] Does the story avoid settling any of the setting's open questions?
- [ ] Would a reader ever need to brace? (See the [tone guardrails](#tone-guardrails).)

## Setting palette

A menu, not a rule set. This section is about how to build one for your setting.

If the setting is built around a large-scale engine (a saga's antagonist, hidden powers, a main conflict), Sunday Morning stories show that engine from the ground, in the setting's texture layer: the saga's protagonist making large systems specific again, a world that exists without the protagonists, "what was happening yesterday", and small, contained social ecosystems such as border towns. A setting without a saga still has that texture layer; build from it the same way.

### Framework ingredients the setting already supplies

Fill this in once per setting. The right-hand column says what to look for; replace it with the setting's own places, pages and details.

| Framework ingredient | What to look for in your setting |
| --- | --- |
| Contained community (§4) | small, bounded places: border towns, pass towns, cove towns, one city neighborhood, a junction or crossroads |
| Recurring locations (§4) | inns, guild halls, public scales, mills, canal gates, shrines, market courts, boat sheds; whatever the setting's architecture offers |
| Manageable deadline (§18, §22) | the seasonal calendar: festivals, harvests, openings and closings of routes or seasons |
| Running jokes and habits (§8, §23) | grammatical or speech habits in the setting's languages; sayings and regional banter |
| Meals, errands, ordinary life (§12) | the setting's food, recipes, and how dishes change as people move |
| Ensemble roles (§5) | guild members, clerks, arbiters, innkeepers, pilgrims, wardens, caravan hands; the minor roles the world needs filled |
| Gentle absurdity treated sincerely (§8) | old treaties, obsolete precedents, places with two names, bureaucratic friction |
| Earnest emotional core (§9) | the setting's themes (for example interdependence, belonging at borders, tradition that changes, ordinary decency) |

### Places

Border towns, and other mixed communities, make good defaults: contained, mixed, and already arguing about their own identity.

For each candidate place, list its natural material the way you'd fill a table: who lives there (two crews who don't quite get along, such as forge crews and farmers), what they argue about (smoke, water law, conservation versus contracts), the seasonal events that fall there, and its food. For a city, pick one neighborhood, one stall, one guild office; never the whole city.

### Festivals as clocks

A festival gives a story a date, a reason for the cast to converge (Framework §10, Act III) and a built-in soft landing. Pair each festival in the setting's calendar with the story material it naturally carries: judgments and remembrance, first launches and inspections, enforced stillness beside enforced argument, public accounting and competition, incompatible people sharing one space, end-of-season settling of boats and debts.

A rule worth borrowing for any calendar: people argue about how a festival **used to be** celebrated.

### Language habits as running elements

Draw these from the setting's languages and speech patterns. They work because they are character habits, not jokes about a people. Good kinds of habit: a way of marking whether work is finished or only promised; marking how you know something (seen yourself, heard from someone trusted, or rumor); a "we" that shows who is being counted in; exact quotation; indirect refusal and face-saving speech.

In the first collection, every story used one such habit as its running element. Check the [Registry](Registry.md#devices-already-used) before reusing one.

### Stakes calibration

"Human-scale" here usually means one of:

- a local institution's honor (a public scale, a guild judgment, a regatta bell);
- a festival going ahead, or going ahead properly;
- a family stall, permit, workshop or boat;
- a feud, lien or precedent everyone has quietly outgrown;
- someone's place in a community (the outsider, the apprentice, the in-law).

Material limits are the best complication generator: mud, late carts, cold, short timber, fuel, a flooded cellar. They create inconvenience without catastrophe.
