## Telling

**Source:** The author's Pathwell notes in this repo, brought in on his call ([D10](Decisions.md#standing-rulings)): [`Writing-Principles.md`](../../Writing-Principles.md), [`Quick-Diagnostic.md`](../Archive/Wiki-2026-06/Quick-Diagnostic.md), [`forbidden_patterns.md`](../Story_Files/forbidden_patterns.md), [`pathwell_prose_voice.md`](../Story_Files/pathwell_prose_voice.md) and [`INS-0001-unpaid-plot-debts-must-be-paid-on-page.md`](../insights/INS-0001-unpaid-plot-debts-must-be-paid-on-page.md).

Pathwell is a literary urban fantasy novel, written for a longer, darker book, so each principle is marked **carries over**, **adapted** or **in tension**.

### 1. Write it like it's the sequel (carries over)

"The sequel assumes you were there." Characters don't explain their world merely for the reader. They talk from lived familiarity, and a thing is explained only when the character, relationship or situation would naturally bring it up.

In any setting, its customs, trades, habits of speech, money and paperwork are ordinary to the people who live with them. The first drafts of the first collection broke this: a narrator stopped to explain how one place's people counted a job as finished, and how another's grammar marked who "we" included. Later passes moved those explanations into what characters do and say. The model is a character whose way of sorting evidence shows up in how she talks long before anyone could name it.

**Tension with the Framework, resolved: withhold lore, never clarity** ([W2](Decisions.md#working-decisions)). The Framework's attention test (§13) asks for "minimal dependence on hidden lore" and "understandable geography"; the sequel principle asks us to withhold. The reader may not know why a household hangs a certain plant over its door, or what some sect of observers believes. They must always know who wants what, where everyone is standing, and what just happened. The voice guide agrees: "The reader should sometimes lack information. That is different from lacking clarity."

### 2. Therefore, but; never "and then" (carries over)

Scenes connect through consequence, complication, decision or relationship pressure. "And then" is the warning sign. In Sunday Morning, social pressure counts as much as plot. A neighbor's anger at the hero is a *but*; a debt chalked up on a slate is a *therefore*.

### 3. Every scene changes something, or makes a later change matter (adapted)

A scene must cause something, change a relationship, knowledge or expectation, make a later choice believable, or give recovery after a big event.

**Adapted:** Framework §12 says quiet scenes "are not filler if they deepen attachment to the world", and Pathwell's principle 11 agrees ("breathe when the story needs possession"). **Enjoying the characters is a job a scene can do.** In the first collection, a night-long vigil scene changed almost nothing in the plot and was one of the best scenes in the book.

**Deletion test:** "If this scene is removed, ______ becomes weaker." If nobody can fill the blank, not even with "the reader's pleasure in these people", cut or merge.

### 4. Show the iceberg's tip; let the world exceed the scene (carries over)

Know more than the reader needs. Depth comes from characters treating their world as real, not from mystery decoration. "One unexplained detail can help. Ten can become noise. Zero can be correct." Pathwell's Quick Diagnostic asks for one unexplained detail per chapter, but its later Writing Principles overrule that: **there is no quota.** A town where nobody remembers why it has its name, and nobody minds, is enough.

### 5. The body may know before the mind (adapted)

A signature Pathwell move (the sugar-cookie scene), but one route into feeling, not a compulsory beat. People also suppress, mislabel, or react through habit and expertise. A builder notices beams before faces; a craftsman reads a crack the way he'd read his own tools. Use the response that fits the person.

### 6. Characters want things, often more than one (carries over)

Every important person in a scene has some pressure shaping them: a preference, a fear, an obligation, face to protect, wanting to be useful. A clerk needs the file closed *and* can't afford to lose it. A trade leader protects his members' rates *and* needs what the other side is selling. Wants can be social, not only practical.

### 7. Pressure the character's self-story (carries over)

Don't give every character one neat "Lie". Ask what story this person tells about themselves, what keeps that story working, and what this situation makes hard to ignore. A retired expert is "retired". A newcomer is "only a guest here".

### 8. Don't explain the metaphor before performing it (carries over)

If an image, ritual or recurrence already carries the meaning, don't translate it. Two halves of one torn thing, found and put together, don't need a sentence about unity.

### 9. Incomplete answers need a human reason (carries over)

Evasion builds worlds only when it comes from a person: they don't know, they refuse, they're protecting face or someone else, they misunderstand, or they want something else from the exchange. An old cook who answers every question about her practice with questions about food is doing it because the practice was never in words, not to make a mystery.

### 10. Place is functionally specific (carries over)

A place matters through what people can do, notice, know or lose there: procedure, expertise, material limits, routine, who belongs, who controls access. A tea stall outside a hearing room, a weighing yard, a mill loft: each works because of what happens in it.

### 11. Subtext must be recoverable (carries over)

Know what is said, what is meant, what can be inferred, what each person knows and wants, and what social action the line performs. Sometimes direct speech is strongest.

### 12. Promise, progress, payoff (carries over)

The opening promises an *experience*, not only plot facts. The middle changes or deepens what the reader is waiting for. The ending fulfills, transforms or deliberately leaves open (in the first collection, the author chose to leave one story's central relationship open). The story pages' promise ledgers already do this.

### 13. Theme through action, not speeches (carries over)

Author-side questions ("Whose decision is this?", "What makes something irreplaceable?") are planning tools. They should rarely become dialogue.

### 14. Earn the big moments; memory before loss (carries over, and very Sunday Morning)

"What has the reader actually experienced that makes this matter?" If a big moment fails, inspect what came before it rather than making it louder.

"Before we destroy something important, let it be ordinary for a while." In Sunday Morning the losses are small (a race, a bell, a family's old stall), so the "having" can be small too. Show the boat's crew at work before the race. Let a dish be tasted three ways before anyone defines it.

### 15. Pay your debts on the page (carries over)

From Pathwell's INS-0001: if a scene creates an emotional or causal debt, let the reader feel its cost before moving on. One beat is enough: an acknowledgment, an aftermath, a small cost felt in the body. **Adapted:** in Sunday Morning the payment is usually warm, not heavy. The winnings go to buy everyone supper.

### 16. Humor is social action (carries over)

Humor creates or changes affiliation, permission, status, intimacy, avoidance, embarrassment, rivalry and repair. "Comedy needs a real serious side, not a mandatory waiting period." The situation must be real enough for the wrong response to be funny. This matches the Framework's "absurdity treated sincerely" (§8).

### 17. Read it aloud (carries over)

Listen for cadence, character voice, comic timing, awkward exposition, repeated sentence shapes, and dialogue no human mouth would carry. "A sentence sounding good is not proof that it belongs."

## Character discovery before explanation

**Working craft guidance (2026-10-13, from the Pathwell diner exploration; [W8–W9](Decisions.md#working-decisions)).** Use when a revelation, a relationship, or a choice isn't convincing yet; this is not a requirement to add interior monologue to every scene.

1. **Let the discovery land before assigning a response.** Distinguish what happened, what the viewpoint character knows, their immediate reaction (which may be confused, contradictory or not yet named), and what they *eventually choose*. Don't jump from a devastating fact to the next efficient plot destination just because the outline knows where the character goes.
2. **Uncertainty is a finding, not a blank to fill with theory.** “I don't know yet” can mean the character hasn't earned that choice or feeling. Preserve the open question instead of inventing a tidy motivation. A short-lived wish to undo an experience need not be their settled desire. Check the scene's time scale: first seconds are not the same as decisions made an hour later.
3. **Do not psychoanalyze ordinary behavior.** Someone can be lively around a friend without thinking about why; someone can disagree without secretly relitigating their entire agency. Distinguish the *character's lived feeling* from the *author's explanation* of it. Self-knowledge is not automatic.
4. **Distinguish welcome, opportunity and access when those differ.** A person may know they are wanted somewhere yet worry they cannot physically get back. Similarly, one person can sincerely care and still fail to show up. Test the precise source of fear or injury instead of substituting the closest theme.
5. **Make dialogue an action by its speaker.** For a persuasive or manipulative line, ask what the speaker needs, what they actually know, what they can plausibly infer, why this listener might respond, and what practical route the exchange opens. A truthful warning can still be self-serving; it does not become the story's moral verdict.
6. **A scenario used to test character isn't yet a scene.** Future meetings, promises broken, or alternate decisions can reveal a habit or flaw, but mark them *diagnostic only* until selected and supported by the causal chain. Do not rewrite present events or settle open world mechanics to accommodate an attractive hypothetical.

**How to discover rather than dictate:** Ask one consequential question at a time, grounded in what has already happened on the page. If the author rejects the offered alternatives, update the model of the character rather than rephrase the same forced choice. Challenge contradictions only when the actual story produces one, and keep author uncertainty labeled as uncertainty. The corresponding stop-and-check procedure belongs in [Pipeline: emotional and knowledge handoff](Pipeline.md#emotional-and-knowledge-handoff-in-think).

These are portable questions, not a transfer of Pathwell's emotional register. A Sunday Morning story still follows its own [tone guardrails](Rules.md#tone-guardrails).

## Voice

**Sources.** The author's [voice guide](Sources/Voice-Guide.md), verbatim, is the authority on how sentences sound. Notes from two of his sample chapters (a Pathwell and an Elizabeth story, shared 2026-09-27 and not stored here) are secondary evidence and give way wherever the two disagree ([W1](Decisions.md#working-decisions)).

**Use the guide as a sensibility, not a checklist** ([D6](Decisions.md#standing-rulings)). Each story takes only what it already wants from it. A checklist of beats per story (a sad beat, a choice, a dropped joke, a callback) makes a collection predictable ([History](History.md#what-went-wrong-and-where-the-lesson-lives-now)).

**Rhythm.** The first voice notes, read from the samples alone, overweighted fragments and one-line paragraphs. The guide is explicit:

- **Ordinary movement:** mostly clean, medium-length sentences.
- **Fragments:** for danger and comic timing, not the default.
- **Reflection and emotion:** longer sentences that carry accumulation and memory.
- **Avoid** a manuscript made entirely of rapid fragments, and every scene ending on a joke.

**Sample-chapter habits that still hold,** because the guide agrees with them: starting in motion; dialogue carrying scenes; humor from character contrast and mundane worries; feeling through smell and taste; grief carried by objects; callbacks; a turn on the last line.

**Narration with an opinion, reconciled** ([W3](Decisions.md#working-decisions)). Pathwell's [`forbidden_patterns.md`](../Story_Files/forbidden_patterns.md) warns that the author's narration can go invisible ("half the wit lives in the narration's asides", of Pratchett). The guide warns against "self-consciously clever narration". Both hold: **narration may have attitude, a point of view that notices and judges, but it shouldn't compete with the characters for laughs.** Earlier passes on the first collection cut narrator jokes to about one per story. That was a fair correction for AI-drafted prose, not a rule that narration has no voice. When the author writes over these drafts, the narration's attitude is his. **The narrator has its own voice,** and its register says where that voice speaks and where it steps aside ([writing against sameness](#writing-against-sameness)). In the Pathwell revision a note reading the author's quiet sample narration as the target was wrong: his own watch-list names invisible narration as his weakness. For Pathwell, the register is [pathwell_narrator_register.md](../Story_Files/pathwell_narrator_register.md).

### Write how people talk

The author's own strength, in his words: people told him he "wrote how people talked", in narration as much as in dialogue ([D14](Decisions.md#standing-rulings)). His example of the failure: "He meant to walk straight to the mill. He did not manage it." Nobody says "he did not manage it", so it sits on the page. Said the way a person would: "He didn't get very far."

- **The test.** Would somebody telling this story out loud, to a friend, say the sentence this way? If it sounds like writing, rewrite it (Elmore Leonard: "If it sounds like writing, I rewrite it.").
- **The narrator borrows the character's words.** Close third person soaks up the idiom of whoever it's following: what a retired farmer would call a thing, how a stonemason would size up a crack. This is free indirect style, and critic Hugh Kenner named its word-level form the "Uncle Charles principle", after Joyce. It gives the narration a person's voice without a speech.
- **Contractions by default,** in narration and dialogue. Full forms ("did not", "I am not") only when someone is insisting, or as a character's formal register, such as an official's voice in a public courtyard. The [checker](tools/sunday_morning_check.py) reports uncontracted forms in narration.
- **Say it the everyday way.** "A lot of" over "a great deal of" (unless it's that character's phrase); "turned up" over "appeared"; "found out" over "discovered". Drop throat-clearing like "found that he", "in the particular way that", "for once". Keep the precise word when the character would use it.
- **Not folksy, not modern.** No phonetic dialect and no stacked idioms (Leonard: dialect sparingly). No modern slang that breaks the world. The goal is a person talking, not a performance of one.
- **Different people, different talk.** Borrowing the viewpoint character's words keeps the stories from sounding like one narrator: a story about a valley farmer should sound like his valley, and a story about a fisherman should sound like his cove.

**Sources:** [Uncle Charles Principle (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncle_Charles_Principle); [Story Street Writers on the Uncle Charles principle](https://storystreetwriters.com/writing-craft/mind-the-gap-how-the-uncle-charles-principle-and-its-adaptations-can-close-the-gap-between-narrator-and-reader-in-different-narrative-points-of-view/); [Elmore Leonard's 10 Rules of Writing](https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/08/21/elmore-leonard-10-rules-of-writing/); [Kathy Steinemann on contractions](https://kathysteinemann.com/Musings/contractions/).

### What the earliest first draft reveals about the author's telling

**Status: observed craft, added 2026-10-26.** The author identified a newly supplied `pathwell - work` manuscript as his *earliest first draft*, distinct from `Pathwell Working.docx` and the later author benchmark. [Line Notes §11](Line-Notes.md#11-what-the-authors-earliest-first-draft-adds-to-the-voice-evidence-2026-10-26) contains ten verbatim excerpts, their functions and their risks; [the source](../Archive/Manuscript/Author_First_Draft_pathwell-work_plaintext_2026-10-26.txt) is in the historical archive. The observations below are **editorial inference from his prose**, not extra author rulings or a new template. The imported [Voice Guide](Sources/Voice-Guide.md) remains verbatim and higher in authority; the [Framework](Sources/Framework.md) still decides what makes a story Sunday Morning.

**The correction:** "grounded, conversational and slightly irreverent" is true but incomplete when interpreted as short, clean deadpan. The first draft repeatedly shows an **expressive oral storyteller**: the narrator has a mind that associates things; objects borrow human feelings; the joke is often hidden inside a description; an ordinary record or habit can become briefly marvelous; and a longer run of prose is sometimes the way affection gets onto the page. The strongest later revisions sharpen these instincts and make people/causes clearer. The weaker revisions replace them with neutral reporting.

#### The useful instincts — what to bring into Sunday Morning stories

1. **Let the narrator enjoy noticing things.** A worn bar seat has supported patrons too drunk to support themselves; the bookstore contains not just books but the ordinary papers of countless lives. In a Sunday story, an inn, village library, school office, boat or shop can reveal its people through the signs of being used, repaired and loved. Sometimes the narratorial aside *is part of the reward for being there*. The narrator may have a quiet opinion; it needn't be disembodied stage direction.

2. **Allow the ordinary world to act figuratively.** The author's door "burst open with panic"; a box "seasoned the floor with books"; a vinyl seat "sighed". These are **nonliteral choices**, not errors requiring correction. An unexpected familiar verb can express the moment more precisely than a literal verb. Judge the physical event separately from the image: clear staging first, then the expressive effect. See [line-editing: nonliteral language](Line-Editing-Protocol.md#literal-correctness-is-not-the-test-for-figurative-language).

3. **Let humor express a character's priorities, not just their cleverness.** The food can be "perfectly crappy" because Pathwell truly values it; a cat can be a blood god whose wages are mice; someone worries about a front door when the event is much bigger than a door. For Sunday Morning, use small incompatible priorities — a festival official concerned about the bunting, a mechanic concerned about the borrowed spanner — as a way of showing worldview. **Keep the sincere concern beneath the joke.** Don't make every character a different comedian reading the same writer's one-liners.

4. **Give the story a speaking rhythm.** An observation may double back ("or at least it wasn't where it was"), trail into a human connective ("but there he was"), or gather details before landing. Commas, conjunctions and selective repetition can make the story *sound told*, not simply reported. A brief fragment then matters because most surrounding prose doesn't stop every five words. **Don't mechanically correct every comma splice or lengthen every fragment; don't mechanically preserve either.** Read it aloud for comprehension, emphasis and pleasure.

5. **Trust a sensory crossing when it carries experience.** "Sunshine directed right at her stomach" renders warmth through an impossible but intelligible combination. Smell can bring taste or remembered company. Sunday Morning does not need Pathwell's magic system to use this: a smell from a kitchen or the texture of a repaired bench can shift the scene emotionally before anyone explains why. Protect the *experience*, not necessarily the old wording; the later sugar-cookie passage is stronger in its particular scene.

6. **Let a place accumulate human lives.** The first draft's great bookstore catalogue of wills, diaries, letters, recipes, unfinished songs and other remnants is long and imperfect, yet its *accumulation* tells us what the place means. For a Sunday story, sometimes three telling details are enough; sometimes a measured roll of many is what makes a town, workshop or tradition feel inhabited. **The question is whether accumulation creates a feeling a summary cannot**, not "can the plot continue without this list?" The author's evident affection for useful, imperfect, shared things is more transferable than the literal catalogue.

7. **Protect room for digression and delight.** An odd bit of local practice, somebody's food preference, a ridiculous bureaucratic distinction, or a familiar chair may matter because it makes us want to spend another page with these people. It does not need a later revelation to justify its existence. The framework explicitly values enjoying the ensemble and its little world. But the detail still needs a function: *pleasure, attachment, contrast, atmosphere, rhythm and character* count alongside causality.

**How these instincts transfer without importing Pathwell:** use lower-pressure local stakes, shared routines, small institutions, visible kindness or disagreement, and the gentle consequences of people wanting different things. The extraordinary can be replaced with any slight wrinkle; the narrator's playful grammar and eye for a lived-in world are not dependent on fantasy, danger, memory magic, or a particular character. The tone promise is still **"You do not need to brace yourself."**

#### What the strongest revision keeps — and what it cuts

- **Keep / sharpen:** a surprising verb that does specific work; a funny wrong-detail reaction rooted in character; a sensory memory before explanation; a place marked by its people; a brief narrator self-correction; a run of details that earns attachment; dialogue that genuinely sounds spoken.
- **Cut / repair:** repeated explanations after the picture already landed; three metaphors competing with one physical event; a joke continued past its best beat; a catalogue substituting for a scene's movement; camera/knowledge errors; circular conversations; unclear action; draft typos. Removing these isn't removing the author's style.
- **Do not replace the loss with a quota.** The first collection's correction of **AI-drafted** narrator jokes to roughly one per story was local repair, **not an author-voice rule**. Likewise "prose primarily clear and invisible" names the resting register, **not a ban on bursts of visible pleasure**. D6 still stands: this is sensibility, not a checklist or a required trick in every scene.
- **Expand the cutting test.** Ask whether deletion changes **plot, character, attachment, atmosphere, rhythm, emotional texture, or sheer enjoyment of the telling**. If the action survives but the place becomes sterile, something *did* change. Equally, an amusing sentence doesn't earn endless length merely because it's amusing.

#### A practical restoration check for drafts and AI collaborators

When a Sunday story is clear but feels generic, try this **diagnostic, not a fill-in-the-blanks recipe**:

1. **Read the passage aloud** like you were telling an interesting incident to someone. Where does it sound like a report rather than a person who enjoys telling it?
2. **Find its most specific human detail.** Could it reveal somebody's habit, history, competence, annoyance or affection instead of adding more generic scenery?
3. **Look for one lost expressive choice:** a verb, aside, sensory connection, spoken connective or deliberate rhythm that was cut because it was "awkward" or "technically impossible." Reconsider its *effect* in context.
4. **Check character-specific priorities.** Who would genuinely respond to the wrong problem? If the same line could be said by anyone, take the joke out or give it to the one person for whom it's sincere.
5. **Check density:** is the most interesting sentence being drowned by three more? Preserve the best one and cut the explanations. Is the scene too thin because everything that isn't strictly causal has been removed? Give it room.
6. **Ask the original deletion question properly:** what would the reader lose by not spending these few seconds in this place with these people?

**For new prose:** An AI should not invent a fixed set of "author-like" quips or imitate original typos to approximate personality. Use the author's actual sentences as a **comparison for instincts**, offer small author-owned alternatives for metaphors and narrator judgments, and let the author decide the final rhythm. The best Sunday Morning story may be quiet, raucous, plain, lyrical or none of those uniformly; what should remain constant is **a felt human storyteller, even when that storyteller chooses to stay quiet**.

## Writing against sameness

**The biggest lesson of the Pathwell revision (2026-09-30).** A single sentence rarely gives AI prose away. What does is *sameness*: a small set of polished moves used with the same success everywhere. The same joke construction twice in a chapter, every character sharing one dry deadpan, a clever conceit in every paragraph, and a neat closing line on every scene. The Pathwell revision brought its checker numbers (contractions, "Not X. / Y." fragments, commentary) close to the author's own chapters, and the chapter still read as generated, because the sameness was untouched ([D17](Decisions.md#standing-rulings)). The voice guide already asks for everything below ("everyone sharing the same sense of humor", "Do not turn every observation into a clever observation", "Not every line should be quotable"). This section turns it into steps. What research reports about AI fiction is collected, for awareness, in [AI tells](Sources/AI-Tells.md) ([D18](Decisions.md#standing-rulings)).

These are guides for judgment, not rules. Any of them can be broken on purpose. What they catch is a move made by habit.

**Before writing a scene**

- **Map the narrator's register.** Mark the stretches where the narrator steps aside (action, sensation and magic, the line after a punchline, any character whose register is precise) and where it's present (arrivals, transitions, aftermath, one-line character observations, what the viewpoint character hasn't named yet). The narrator's attitude goes where the map puts it, and usually small: "anyway", "probably", the wrong detail noticed, an absence noted. A setting can own its narrator's register (Pathwell's is [pathwell_narrator_register.md](../Story_Files/pathwell_narrator_register.md)).
- **Know whose joke it is.** Give each character a source for their humour: their worldview and defences, what they notice, how they dodge ([Craft 16](#16-humor-is-social-action-carries-over); the voice guide's "Pathwell-Style Comic Character"). Before a joke goes in, ask whether someone else in the scene could have said it. If they could, it probably isn't that character's joke yet. A voice note that gives the whole cast one register invites one shared voice; keep the register for the book, and the humour for each person.
- **Let the situation be funny first** (voice guide). If a scene is funny, the characters are usually sincere in it. If only the phrasing is funny, the scene is being decorated.

**While writing**

- **Let people think like people.** Worries, lists, wrong guesses and half-finished thoughts, not a neat diagnosis of their own psychology. A line where a character names her own motive precisely is emotional material: flag it for the author.
- **Usually no more than one polished closing line per scene:** an aphorism, a neat reversal, a two-part antithesis, a "for the first time" turn. Most paragraphs can end on an action, a line of dialogue or a plain fact.
- **Some texture is just texture.** Not every object needs to be a symbol, a callback or a setup ("Do not force every event to symbolize something").
- **Vary the move, not the words.** Synonym swaps don't help. If a construction has been used nearby, the next beat needs a different kind of move: an action, a line of dialogue, or silence.
- **New emotional lines and new jokes stay plain and get flagged** ([who writes what](#ai-and-the-author-who-writes-what)).
- **Never make the prose worse on purpose.** No typos, filler or random fragments. The aim is the story the guide describes, not prose that fools a detector.

**After writing**, inside [after every pass](Pipeline.md#after-every-pass):

- **Two benchmarks, not one number.** Compare the thinking, the senses and how distinct the voices are with the author's own writing. Compare the narrator with its register. The checker's counts are a floor, not the test.
- **Track joke shapes** in the collection's Registry, as names and openings are tracked ([Registry: devices](Registry.md#devices-already-used)); count the ones a pattern can catch as [watch patterns](Registry.md#watch-patterns-optional).
- **The fresh check reads for sameness.** Beyond drift and repetition, it asks:
  1. Is any joke construction, closing line or gesture repeated within the story, or from its neighbours?
  2. Does each joke belong to the character who says it?
  3. Is the narrator speaking where its register says to step aside, or quiet where it should be present?
  4. Do people think like people?
  5. How many polished closing lines does each scene have?
  6. Does anything on the [AI tells](Sources/AI-Tells.md) list cluster here?
- **A detector score isn't a check** ([D17](Decisions.md#standing-rulings)).
- **Then a reader reads it cold** ([reader protocol](Reader-Protocol.md), D20). The rules above catch faults in the text. The reader catches the places where a person reading would stop.

## Lessons from the author's line notes

These rules come from his line notes on Pathwell's Chapters 1 and 2 (2026-10-01), and rules 9 to 14 from his own markup of Chapter 1 (2026-10-23), where he made the cuts and rewrites himself. He asked for them to be recorded "so that we can… make sure we keep the same quality". Each one links to the worked examples in [Line notes](Line-Notes.md): the note, the change and the reasoning ([D19](Decisions.md#standing-rulings)). Check a draft against them before it goes to him.

1. **Walk the scene physically** ([examples](Line-Notes.md#1-walk-the-scene-physically)). Know where everyone is, what they're holding, what they can hear and what they left with. Count the events: "again" has to mean a second time. A worry must be true to the situation (she has no wallet; she didn't lose her transportation). A physical cause needs physical distance (the table is at the curb, so a passing truck can blow the ticket away).
2. **Every piece of knowledge needs a source on the page, and no one asks about what they just saw** ([examples](Line-Notes.md#2-every-piece-of-knowledge-needs-a-source-and-no-one-asks-what-they-just-saw)). If a character changes their mind, show what changed it (he smells the blob). If a wrong belief drives the plot, make it visible ("You really don't know?").
3. **Plant it, call it the same thing every time, and make it matter before it's lost** ([examples](Line-Notes.md#3-plant-it-name-it-the-same-way-and-make-it-matter-before-its-lost)). One name for one thing ("dry cleaning" in the outburst, "dry-cleaning ticket" in her pocket). Make the stake felt before it's taken away. Whatever sets off a feeling must be something the reader can recognize.
4. **If cutting it changes nothing, cut it, and don't replace it** ([examples](Line-Notes.md#4-if-cutting-it-changes-nothing-cut-it-and-dont-replace-it)). This is his test. Naming grief outright makes it smaller; let objects and small losses carry it. Cut filler gestures. **Warmth has to pass the same test:** a soft landing should come from the people the story follows and change something (pay something off, or change how we see someone), not be a kindness from someone offstage.
5. **Write how people talk, and let the line carry what they feel** ([examples](Line-Notes.md#5-write-how-people-talk-and-let-the-line-carry-what-they-feel)). After tears comes "Who the hell are you?", not "What's your name?". If no version of a line passes the talk test, cut it. Punctuation is voice too: a panicked answer can end in a question mark.
6. **Check what each describing word suggests** ([examples](Line-Notes.md#6-check-what-each-word-carries)). "Crisp" pages sound new. Watch for the same phrase twice on one page. Don't trade one stock phrase for another.
7. **Give two people a beat by having them answer each other** ([examples](Line-Notes.md#7-give-two-people-a-beat-and-let-them-answer-each-other)). An exchange that shows something about one of them does more than a stock gesture. An accusation is the speaker's reading of events; let the other character's reply correct it, not the narrator. Let a character take the other's word and turn it around ("crappy" → "Perfectly crappy").
8. **Rhythm on the page** ([examples](Line-Notes.md#8-rhythm-on-the-page)). Give a beat that needs a moment its own paragraph. Two quips in a row with the same shape is one too many.
9. **No reminders** ([examples](Line-Notes.md#9-what-his-own-rewrites-show), row 1). Don't re-tell the reader who is holding what or what they just saw. If a line only double-checks, cut it.
10. **Stay in the viewpoint character's senses** (row 2). Others' perceptions and feelings come through what she can see and hear. A line describing what another character smells, feels or decides inside is a viewpoint slip.
11. **Flow, with fragments for one beat** (rows 3 to 5). Join clauses with commas and "and", and let short paragraphs merge into one movement; comma splices are his voice, not errors to fix. Save a fragment for a pause a voice would make (an order, a held breath, an afterthought). Carrying a word forward into the next clause ("stillness, a stillness…") is his rhythm; don't trim it.
12. **Everyday words, and a narrator who talks** (rows 6 and 7). Plain words someone would say aloud ("plastic", "dripped", "splattered"), a pronoun where the name is already clear, and spoken connectives in narration ("But there he was", "And there…").
13. **Spoken lines over narrated reactions; save the signature word** (rows 8 and 9). Where a draft gives a look or a pause, give him something to say. Keep a word that pays off at the end (Pathwell's "Perfect.") out of casual use before it.
14. **His brackets are requests** (row 10). A bracketed note about blocking or logic means he wants the staging solved: write the plainest fix and list it for him.

**When pushing back on a note** ([examples](Line-Notes.md#where-a-note-was-adjusted-or-held-and-why)):

- Close third person can name only what the viewpoint character notices, so a reveal is protected by what she *doesn't* register. Put the reader's clue where she can't see it.
- Before changing an object, check the promise ledger for everything that depends on it.
- Keep the opening hook fast. Put the stakes just after it, not inside it.
- Read all the notes together before applying any of them; two notes can collide.
- His own rewrites get the same check. Raise a cost (an offstage action, a line that explains what the scene showed, a phrase repeated nearby) and let him choose ([examples](Line-Notes.md#questions-raised-about-his-own-rewrites-2026-10-23)). Don't apply his markup before giving feedback when he asks for feedback.
- Fit a new idea to what the story already has before adding new lore.

## AI and the author: who writes what

The guide sets the division of labor ("AI Collaboration Guidance"), and it applies to every pass:

- **AI leads on** continuity, structure, motivation, pacing, action clarity, repeated beats and thematic consistency. The strongest AI contributions in the first collection were structural: plans, ledgers, continuity and the uniqueness matrix.
- **AI is cautious with** jokes, emotional language, philosophical statements, character-defining dialogue, metaphors, narrator commentary and final sentence rhythm. Lines in these categories are scaffolding for the author and should be flagged for his review.
- **New text in those categories stays plain and flagged.** When a fix needs a new emotional line or joke, put in the plainest version that does the job and list it for the author with an alternative or two. In the Pathwell revision the reviser's own new lines were among those a detector flagged hardest.
- **Push back on the author's notes when they aren't right** (Pathwell revision, R26). Check each note against the story, the canon and his other rulings before applying it; when it has a cost, say so plainly with the reason and an alternative, and let him decide. Applying a note silently isn't deference; it's skipping the check.
- **A voice problem routes to DO if it's a line, and to the author if it's taste** ([routing](Pipeline.md#stage-3--do-draft)).
- **The drafts are scaffolds for the author to write over,** not a substitute voice. Taste, humor and voice are his call, which is why L4 requires his reading ([Pipeline](Pipeline.md#stage-4--judge-review-independently)).

## The author's watch-list

From Pathwell's [`forbidden_patterns.md`](../Story_Files/forbidden_patterns.md), which records habits flagged across that project, not generic advice. AI drafting shares several of them.

| Habit | Catch it | Status here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Exposition disguised as dialogue** | Is one character telling another something they'd both already know, for the reader's benefit? | Watch. The main risk in world-heavy stories. |
| **The docent:** a character explaining how the world works to someone who just experienced it | Any passage that narrates the rules | Watch. Keep each explanation to one or two lines. |
| **Filtering** ("she saw", "he felt", "she heard", "noticed", "watched") | Search for perception verbs, cut the filter, render the thing | The checker counts these per story. |
| **Body-part cataloging** | Three body parts in one sentence: cut to one, make it specific | Watch |
| **Metronome narration:** sing-song, same-shape sentences between lively dialogue | Read the narration aloud separately | Watch; see the rhythm rules above |
| **Narration without an opinion** | Is narration only stage direction? | In tension; reconciled under Voice |
| **Telling what the scene just showed** | After an image does the work, delete the sentence naming the feeling | The most common remaining fault. Prefer cuts. |
| **Adding volume when uncertain** | If a passage feels off, cut before you add | Applies to revision passes too |
| **Pulling on resolved threads** | Build on locked decisions; don't re-derive them | Check [Decisions](Decisions.md) first |
| **Confirm new elements before building on them** | Don't treat an unconfirmed detail as established | Matches the [promotion rule](Rules.md#promotion-rule) |

**When editing rather than diagnosing:** [Line-editing and revision verification](Line-Editing-Protocol.md) is the operational protocol. It gives concrete acceptance and regression checks at the word, sentence, exchange, paragraph, scene and chapter levels. The examples above remain the rationale; word-search counts never require automatic replacements.

## The one-line test

From Pathwell, before keeping any paragraph:

> *Did I explain something the reader could feel? Did a thing move on its own that a person should have chosen? Could this be shorter?*

And the guide's revision rule: clarify first, strengthen second, embellish last. Prefer what sounds like the story over what sounds like good writing.

## Sunday Morning scene diagnostic

Pathwell's five-question [Quick Diagnostic](../Archive/Wiki-2026-06/Quick-Diagnostic.md) (now archived; its last question was overruled by Writing Principles 18), adapted. Run it on every scene of a draft.

1. **What is moving the viewpoint character in this scene?** A want, fear, obligation, upset, or conflicting impulse can drive it. “Nothing” or “to understand what's happening” is a prompt to inspect the scene, not automatic proof the character is passive. After a shock, allow a specific reaction before expecting a settled plan ([character discovery](#character-discovery-before-explanation)).
2. **Therefore, but, or and then?** Fix "and then".
3. **What changed from top to bottom?** A relationship, knowledge, a plan, or the reader's attachment. If nothing did, cut or merge.
4. **Did anyone explain what the story already showed?** Cut it.
5. **Would a reader brace here?** If so, add warmth, absurdity or a meal nearby ([tone guardrails](Rules.md#tone-guardrails)).
