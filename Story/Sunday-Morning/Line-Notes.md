# The author's line notes: worked examples

## Status

**Evidence, open. Added to as the author gives line notes.** This page records each line note the author has given, what changed on the page, and why. It started with the Pathwell revision: his notes on Chapters 1 and 2, on 2026-10-01 (Pathwell R25, R27 and R28).

The rules drawn from these notes are in [Craft: lessons from the author's line notes](Craft.md#lessons-from-the-authors-line-notes). This page is the evidence for them. When a rule seems arbitrary, the example here shows the problem it fixes. When he gives new notes, add them here, and add a rule to Craft only if the lesson is new.

The examples are from Pathwell, but each lesson applies to any Sunday Morning story.

Each entry gives:

- **Note:** what he said, in his words where they're short.
- **Change:** the text before and after.
- **Logic:** why the change is right. This is the part to carry into the next chapter.

## 1. Walk the scene physically

A reader pictures the room, so the room has to work: where everyone is, what they're holding, what they can hear, what they brought with them.

| Note | Change | Logic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "the first crash was the boxes falling over otherwise pathwell is just sitting there reading a book while the blob is slamming against the door" (Ch1) | "It was the crash that woke her." now means a stack of her boxes going over: "One stack had gone over, and her winter sweaters were all over the rug." The blob's first thud comes later. | If the noise that woke her had been the blob, a man calmly reading her diary through it would make no sense. The fix keeps the opening line and changes what it refers to. |
| "the first thud happens but the story says 'it came again'" (Ch1) | "It came again. A thud against the front door." becomes "A thud hit the front door." | "Again" tells the reader they missed something, so they go back and look for it. Count the events. |
| "how am I going to get to the meeting… she didnt lose her transportation? Maybe she doesnt have her wallet or keys?" (Ch2) | "How am I going to even get to the meeting?" becomes "I don't have my wallet." | Panic can jump around, but each worry still has to be true. She left with only shoes and a coat, so the wallet worry is true, and it costs her something. |
| "How close are they to the street where the truck can cause the wind to pick up?" (Ch2) | "a plastic table sat on the street" becomes "one of the plastic tables set out at the curb", and "a truck roared past close enough to rattle the table". | A physical cause needs physical distance. If the truck is close enough to rattle the table, its wind can take the ticket. |
| "How did Elizabeth lose track of Pathwell so easily?" (Ch4) | Before, the path narrowed, she dropped behind, and "she could only hear the cart somewhere ahead of her". After, she stops to shake something out of her office shoe; "Pathwell didn't look back"; the path splits around a tree; "She picked a side. Her books were on that cart." | A loaded handcart on a root-crossed path is slow, and she had a hand on it. Losing him needs a stop of her own (the shoe), a reason he doesn't wait (he never looks back), and a fork. Every check had missed this, which is why there is now a [reader protocol](Reader-Protocol.md) (D20). |
| "I noticed in the bar scene that stansbury smiled into his beer before he got it" (Ch6) | "Stansbury smiled into his beer." became "Stansbury smiled down at the bar." (the drinks arrive a few lines later) | A prop can't be used before it's in the scene. The reader and the fresh check both missed this one, so the author asked for a causality check: the [reader protocol's causality pass](Reader-Protocol.md#the-causality-pass) (D22) walks every beat for what must already be true and what it changes. |

## 2. Every piece of knowledge needs a source, and no one asks what they just saw

| Note | Change | Logic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Wheres your cookbook… i feel like he should smell the blob to know its there" (Ch1) | Added: "He lifted his head and sniffed. Under the cheese and the cake there was something else, faint and wrong. Low tide, a long way from any sea. / His face changed." | He goes from casual to urgent, so the reader has to see what changed his mind. A smell also leaves room for her to get the full stench later. |
| "Does she need to ask about the diary? … when she walked out on him with it." (Ch1) | "Why are you holding my diary?" is cut. | She watched him read it, so the question is the writer checking in, not her. |
| "Does he need to ask something like 'you dont know?' he thinks that the blob is there for her" (Ch1) | Added: "Then he looked at her more carefully. / 'You really don't know?' / 'Know what?' / 'Huh.'" | A character's wrong belief that drives the plot has to be visible on the page. Otherwise the reader can't see the misread, and the later reveal has nothing to overturn. |

## 3. Plant it, name it the same way, and make it matter before it's lost

| Note | Change | Logic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "make it clear what the ticket is for, before hand?" (Ch2) | "laundry ticket" becomes "dry-cleaning ticket" (also in Ch3). Her worry earlier in the chapter already says "I have to get my dry cleaning before the meeting." | The setup was already there, but two names for one thing hid the link. Call a thing by the same name every time, and the reader makes the connection on their own. |
| "Do we need to make the meeting more important, like she wakes up in chapter 1 and is like 'okay, parties over, i got a big meeting in the morning'" (Ch2, applied in Ch1) | Ch1's party paragraph: "The guests had left hours ago, and she'd gone straight to bed. She had a meeting at nine, her first big one since she'd started." | A loss only hurts if the reader felt the stake before it went. It goes after the opening rather than in it, so the first lines stay fast (see the push-back section). |
| "Maybe somehting like 'She heard her again, Lizzy'" (Ch2) | "She heard her again." comes before "Lizzy." | Whatever sets off a feeling has to be readable. The bare "Lizzy." relied on the reader guessing it was Nana's word. "Her" is unclear for one line, then the butter and sugar make it clear. That short delay is fine. A wrong guess that lasts would not be. |

## 4. If cutting it changes nothing, cut it, and don't replace it

His test, from Ch1: "it doesnt help the story and doesnt change anyhting if removed."

| Note | Change | Logic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "get rid of the 'since the funeral'" (Ch1) | "A voice she hadn't heard since the funeral." became "A voice that couldn't be here." | Naming the grief makes it smaller. The impossible voice already tells the reader she has lost someone. |
| "Drop 'a funeral she couldnt cry at' just cut the line, it doesnt need to be replaced" (Ch2) | Cut. Her tears now come from the boxes, the move and the lost ticket. | People break down over small things, and the lost ticket is the small thing here. Ch3 ("Nana. The funeral.") carries the grief later, so nothing is lost. |
| "Maybe cut the 'He drummed his fingers on the edge of the table, looking around aimlessly.'" (Ch2) | Cut. | The gesture is filler: it doesn't show us anything about him that the scene doesn't already. |
| "I dont think the strangers kindness passes the rule about if you remove it, does it change anything" (Ch2 Sunday-tone trial) | Cut the napkins sent out by "whoever was working the counter". The same test then cut the shopkeeper's glass of water from the Ch3 trial. | Warmth has to pass the cut test too. A kindness from someone offstage changes nothing, and here it competed with the one that does: Pathwell's grudging "Oh, fine, you can have a dumpling." Additions that survived the test: "He left the last dumpling on her side of the tray." (it pays off "I don't share" and changes how we see him as he walks off with her books); "Elizabeth was halfway around it before she knew she'd moved." (it changes her: she acts before anyone tells her to). |

## 5. Write how people talk, and let the line carry what they feel

| Note | Change | Logic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Should maybe be 'Who the hell are you?' or something that speaks to her frustration" (Ch2) | "What's your name?" becomes "Who the hell are you?" | She's just cried, and anger is what comes next. A neutral question wastes the moment. |
| "Just poorly worded, theres no way that passes the Twain rule of writing how people talk" (Ch2) | Cut "You went through my boxes. I feel like that's information I should have." It wasn't reworded. | If no version of a line sounds like speech, the scene probably doesn't need it. Here "Who the hell are you?" already says it. |
| "Just make it 'fair'?" (Ch2) | "That's fair." becomes "Fair." | Pathwell's register is clipped. The shorter line also sounds less interested, which is the point. |
| "The line should end in a ?" (Ch1) | "My Nana always made the best sugar cookies?" | When someone answers a question while panicking, they aren't sure of their own answer. The question mark is her voice. |

## 6. Check what each word carries

| Note | Change | Logic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "describing the pages as crisp makes them sound new" (Ch1) | "crisp and stained" becomes "worn and stained". | Describing words have to fit the object's history. Nana's cookbook is old and well used. |
| "using 'in years' too many times" (Ch1) | One "in years" is left. | A phrase used twice on one page sounds like a habit. |
| "what if its like 'a voice that couldnt be real'?" (Ch1) | "A voice that couldn't be here." | Pushed back on: "couldn't be real" is a stock phrase. "Couldn't be here" is plainer and more specific, since she knows exactly whose voice it is and why it's impossible. |

## 7. Give two people a beat, and let them answer each other

| Note | Change | Logic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "need more of a beat between them" (Ch2, after "Oh, these aren't for you… and I don't share.") | Added "Then why am I here?" / "Company?" (the reviser's line, flagged) | A beat between two people works best as an exchange that reveals one of them. A stock gesture like "She stared at him." does less. Here his answer shows he hasn't thought about her at all, which is his blind spot for the rest of the book. |
| "Maybe add that 'you wont let me go home'" (Ch2) | Her outburst gains "You won't let me go home." | This is her version of events, and his reply ("I wouldn't go back there tonight. But that's just me.") corrects it without forbidding her anything. Let the characters argue it out instead of the narrator settling it. |
| "Need to mention they are not great but 'perfectly crappy' and 'they arent trying to be anything theyre not'" (Ch2) | "Secondly," he said through a half-chewed mouthful, "these things are perfect. Perfectly crappy. They aren't trying to be anything they're not. You have no idea how hard it is to find that kind of quality." | He picks up her word "crappy" and turns it around, and "perfect" is his word (as in "Perfect." in Ch1). The line carries some theme, coming from a man missing part of himself, but it stays light because it's about dumplings. Don't repeat it elsewhere in the book. |
| "Maybe make it '"Secondly," through his half chewed dumpling'" (Ch2) | As above. The earlier "through half-chewed food" is cut. | Put the physical gag where the comic timing is, and use it once. |

## 8. Rhythm on the page

| Note | Change | Logic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "line break at 'she hurried'?" (Ch2) | The paragraph splits into three: him walking off; "She looked back one more time at her apartment window. A shadow filled the space."; "She hurried to catch him at the corner." | The shadow needs a moment to itself before she moves. The fix also removed "looked around… looking back". |
| (two quips in one exchange) | "That's it. Easy to remember." became "That's it." once "Don't need one. Saves everybody time." went in. | Two answers in a row with the same shape (short answer, then a reason) make a pattern. Keep one ([writing against sameness](Craft.md#writing-against-sameness)). |

## Where a note was adjusted or held, and why

These are the cases where [push-back](Craft.md#ai-and-the-author-who-writes-what) changed what went in. In each case the author agreed.

| Note | What happened | Logic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Change 'gathering up his things' to 'gathering up her things'?" (Ch2) | **Kept "his things".** | The chapter is in her close point of view, so the narration can only name what she notices. If it says "her things", she has seen the books leave, and "He still has it." is no longer a discovery. "His things" is also how he sees it: to him they're just what he's carrying. The reader already got the clue where she couldn't: "dropped her books on the table beside the tray", while she was crying. |
| "maybe it needs to be something else other than a wooden spoon?" (Ch2) | **Kept the spoon.** | It was his own July choice ("The only thing she unpacked"), and it pays off three times later (Ch12 and Ch15: FIND NANA'S SPOON; Ch17: "Because it was a spoon."). Before changing an object, check the promise ledger for everything that depends on it. |
| The meeting, planted "she wakes up… i got a big meeting" (Ch2) | **It went in the paragraph after the opening, not at the moment she wakes.** | The first lines (the crash, a man in her boxes) are the hook. Put stakes right after the hook, not inside it. |
| "half-chewed" asked for on two lines (Ch2) | **It went on one line.** | Two notes can collide. Read them together before applying either. |
| "the exploding blob is what marks the house" (Ch1) | **It became a prop for a bluff:** "He nodded at the ooze drying on her walls." He made up "marked", and the ooze helps sell it. | Fit a new idea to what the story already has before adding new lore (Pathwell R23). The author chose this: "lets keep it a bluff". |
| "a voice that couldnt be real" (Ch1) | **"couldn't be here"** | His idea, with the stock phrase swapped out. |
