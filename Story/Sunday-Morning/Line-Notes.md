# The author's line notes: worked examples

## Status

**Evidence, open. Added to as the author gives line notes.** This page records each line note the author has given, what changed on the page, and why. It started with the Pathwell revision: his notes on Chapters 1 and 2, on 2026-10-01 (Pathwell R25, R27 and R28). On 2026-10-23 he marked up Chapter 1 himself (Pathwell R59/P78): his own cuts and rewrites, not notes to the reviser. What those rewrites show about how he writes is [section 9](#9-what-his-own-rewrites-show); where the reviser questioned a rewrite is at the end of [the push-back section](#where-a-note-was-adjusted-or-held-and-why).

The rules drawn from these notes are in [Craft: lessons from the author's line notes](Craft.md#lessons-from-the-authors-line-notes). This page is the evidence for them. When a rule seems arbitrary, the example here shows the problem it fixes. When he gives new notes, add them here, and add a rule to Craft only if the lesson is new.

The examples are from Pathwell, but each lesson applies to any Sunday Morning story.

Each entry gives:

- **Note:** what he said, in his words where they're short.
- **Change:** the text before and after.
- **Logic:** why the change is right. This is the part to carry into the next chapter.

## 1. Walk the scene physically

A reader pictures the room, so the room has to work: where everyone is, what they're holding, what they can hear, what they brought with them.

| Note | Change | Logic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "the first crash was the boxes falling over otherwise pathwell is just sitting there reading a book while the blob is slamming against the door" (Ch1) | "It was the crash that woke her." now means a stack of her boxes going over: "One stack had gone over, and her winter sweaters were all over the rug." The blob's first thud comes later. | If the noise that woke her had been the blob, a man calmly reading her diary through it would make no sense. The fix keeps the opening line and changes what it refers to. |
| "the first thud happens but the story says 'it came again'" (Ch1) | "It came again. A thud against the front door." becomes "A thud hit the front door." | "Again" tells the reader they missed something, so they go back and look for it. Count the events. |
| "how am I going to get to the meeting… she didnt lose her transportation? Maybe she doesnt have her wallet or keys?" (Ch2) | "How am I going to even get to the meeting?" becomes "I don't have my wallet." | Panic can jump around, but each worry still has to be true. She left with only shoes and a coat, so the wallet worry is true, and it costs her something. |
| "How close are they to the street where the truck can cause the wind to pick up?" (Ch2) | "a plastic table sat on the street" becomes "one of the plastic tables set out at the curb", and "a truck roared past close enough to rattle the table". | A physical cause needs physical distance. If the truck is close enough to rattle the table, its wind can take the ticket. |
| "How did Elizabeth lose track of Pathwell so easily?" (Ch4) | Before, the path narrowed, she dropped behind, and "she could only hear the cart somewhere ahead of her". | "I noticed in the bar scene that stansbury smiled into his beer before he got it" (Ch6) | "Stansbury smiled into his beer." became "Stansbury smiled down at the bar." (the drinks arrive a few lines later) | A prop can't be used before it's in the scene. ## 9. What his own rewrites show

From his own markup of Chapter 1 (2026-10-23): strikeouts and additions in his hand, not notes to the reviser. Each row is a pattern that ran through several of his changes. Write toward these; don't "correct" them back.

| Pattern | Before → after (his) | Logic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. No reminders.** Don't re-tell the reader who is holding what, or what they just saw. | "He went straight back to the boxes, the diary still under his arm." → "He went straight back to the boxes." (his margin: "we get it, he still has the diary"). Also cut: "Elizabeth leaned close enough to see the ink still there beneath it."; "She waited. Hushed, pressed against the door."; "Someone might have fallen in the hall." | Each cut line was the narrator double-checking something the reader already had. The scene moves faster without the check. |
| **2. Stay in her senses.** Other characters' perceptions and feelings come through what she can see and hear, not through narration of what they sense. | Cut: "He lifted his head and sniffed. Under the cheese and the cake there was something else, faint and wrong. Low tide, a long way from any sea." Now: "Something wet and dark sagged into the gap, bringing with it a stench of low tide." Also cut: "He stopped. For a second he wasn't in a hurry at all." | The sniff paragraph was inside Pathwell's nose in a chapter told from Elizabeth. Giving her the smell at the door keeps the viewpoint and puts the danger on her. |
| **3. Flow, not fragments.** Join clauses with commas and "and"; merge short paragraphs into one movement. Comma splices are his voice, not errors. | "She knew him, a little. He'd been at the party, talking to everybody." → "She knew him, a little, he'd been at the party." / "It had been a good party. Better than she'd expected…" → "It was a good party, better than she'd expected." / "She went to the door and turned the lock and opened it against the chain." The door, the smell, the gap and the slam became one paragraph. | People telling a story don't stop every five words. Stacked one-line paragraphs make every moment the same size; his joined sentences let the action run. This is the opposite of the "one cadence for every register" weakness the revision keeps finding. |
| **4. Fragments are for one beat.** | "Stillness." / "Don't. Think." / "Taking their light with it." | He uses a fragment where a voice would pause: a held breath, an order, an afterthought. Because the paragraphs around them run long, the fragments land. |
| **5. Repetition as rhythm.** Carry a word forward into the next clause. | "There was only her racing heart and stillness, a stillness interrupted by a dull rumbling." → "Stillness. There was only her racing heart and stillness. A stillness interrupted by a dull rumbling, rumbling that grew into shaking, a shaking that rocked the walls around her." | A storyteller's cadence: each clause picks up the last word of the one before and pushes it further. He expanded this where an editor would trim. Leave it. |
| **6. Everyday words over writerly ones.** | "Paper cups" → "Plastic cups"; "the stench of wet-rot permeating the room" → "splattered across the room"; "dropped onto the carpet" → "dripped"; "now said only ELIZ" → "now only read ELIZ"; "Nana's blue marker" / "Nana's pen" → "her blue marker" / "the pen". | Plain words that someone would say aloud. He also cut a repeated name where a pronoun was clear. |
| **7. The narrator talks like someone telling it.** | "He sat on the floor with a box open beside him" → "But there he was, sat on the floor, with a box open beside him"; "There in the middle of it all" → "And there in the middle of it all". | Spoken connectives ("But there he was", "And there…") and colloquial grammar ("sat on the floor") make the narration a person talking, which is what "writes how people talk" means in narration. |
| **8. Spoken lines over narrated reactions.** | Added: "You should probably get behind something."; "The old one, the handwritten one with notes. I know there's one around here somewhere."; "You really have no idea?" He looked her over, searching for a reaction.; her outburst "No, look at what you did, my apartment is wrecked, how am I going to even clean… whatever this even is?" | He gives characters something to say where a draft gave them a look or a pause. Her outburst is the first time she pushes back in her own words. |
| **9. Save signature words for the payoff.** | The early "Perfect." → "Good… that's good."; the chapter's last "Perfect." stays. | A word that will carry weight at the end is spent if it's used casually first. |
| **10. Bracket what isn't solved yet.** | His margin notes: "(need some sort of blocking here to demonstrate his confusion)"; "(how can she see inside the book?)". | He marks staging gaps and logic questions instead of forcing a line. Solving them is the reviser's job (staging is the reviser's lead): write the plainest fix and list it for him. |

## 10. Correction to a literalist editorial objection (2026-10-26)

The 2026-10-23 table above contains an editor's question about **"the stench of wet-rot splattered across the room"**: "A smell doesn't splatter." That is an accurate statement about literal physics but **not** sufficient editorial evidence against the line. A transferred action may convey sudden, violent arrival, and the author deliberately substituted this verb for "permeating." It is neither a factual claim about a smell nor automatically a prose defect.

The remaining legitimate tests are reader comprehension, point of view, physical blocking, unwanted ambiguity and image density **in the passage**. Until one of those fails, the default is **KEEP**, with artistic choice belonging to the author. This corrects the earlier editor's *reasoning*, not the historical entry or the author's wording. General editing test: [Line-Editing Protocol — figurative language](Line-Editing-Protocol.md#literal-correctness-is-not-the-test-for-figurative-language). ### What this corrects in earlier editing advice

- The original isn't chiefly **minimalist deadpan**. It alternates practical physical storytelling with an exuberant, associative narrator; a measured, funnier/sadder observation sometimes expands rather than compresses.
- "If cutting a sentence changes nothing, cut it" has a blind spot when **nothing** means only plot or causal information. A sentence may change pleasure, warmth, rhythm, place, character or the reader's desire to stay. Those effects count.
- "Let the situation be funny first" **does not ban** funny narration. It means the narratorial joke should belong to this moment, not read as a generic line imposed on it.
- "The narrator shouldn't compete with the characters" is a question of **timing and density**, not a fixed allowance such as one narratorial joke per story.
- The correction for **excess personification** is to examine *clustering and comprehension*; treating every nonliteral verb as an error erases the voice.
- "Polish" may rightfully remove repetitions, typos, redundant physical beats and excessive catalogue length; it may not silently swap unique metaphor for safe but lesser prose.

## 12. Blocking, action beats and the risk of overcorrection (2026-10-26)

**Prompt / scope.** The author asked whether his early tendency to describe characters' movements and gestures ("blocking") was genuinely a defect, or whether editing advice had made his prose too literal and spare. He offered this **illustrative sentence**, not a verified quotation from the manuscript:

> "Your house has now been marked. Magically speaking," he said, nodding his head to the side.

**Author clarification (later 2026-10-26):** The intended little sideways/indecisive nod was **not a gesture toward the room or toward the ooze**. It was a physical qualification of Pathwell's words, something like **"so to speak"** or a half-concession attached to "Magically speaking." This is the author's meaning, not an editor's interpretation of direction.

**Source-verified current Chapter 1** (`Story/Chapters/Chapter_01.txt`, after the author's P78 markup):

> He nodded at the ooze drying on her walls.  
>
> "Your house has now been marked. Magically speaking. So we should probably be anywhere but here."

The current version instead **points out visible residue** to make his invented "marked" claim look supported by evidence (R27). That is a coherent alternate characterization, but **it is not the original gesture's intended meaning**. The earlier editor praised the rewrite for specifying the nod's target, **without checking whether it had a target at all**. That evaluation was wrong. The author's version performs an uncertain, conversational hedge, which can be funnier and more characteristic than a deliberate demonstration of evidence.

**Evidence that earlier blocking sometimes was a real problem:** In the [author's earliest Chapter 1 draft](../Archive/Manuscript/Author_First_Draft_pathwell-work_plaintext_2026-10-26.txt), the stranger keeps shuffling/searching boxes and Elizabeth tries variations of "hey", "Hey", "HEy" and "HEY" before his response. Multiple similar gestures, box searches and reassertions of surprise are doing overlapping jobs. Cutting or tightening these can improve pacing and physical comprehension without erasing the vivid **"WIth all the courage a baseball bat could proivde"** (original spelling retained), which conveys her fear through a prop. That is a genuine repetition/density issue **alongside** a genuinely good physical image; neither cancels the other.

**Earlier example of a defensible cut:** The author's own note, preserved in [§4](#4-if-cutting-it-changes-nothing-cut-it-and-dont-replace-it), suggested cutting `"He drummed his fingers on the edge of the table, looking around aimlessly."` The movement conveyed nothing that wasn't already there in the local scene. This is different from a purposeful physical qualification of Pathwell's words. Do not revive cut filler because gestures as a category have been defended.

**Editorial distinction:** 
- **Legitimate repair:** simplify repeated movements; clarify action that hides who did what; locate a gesture's object; fix blocking that contradicts geography, timing, or POV; stop a joke or action sequence after the best beat.
- **Overcorrection:** strip character-specific gestures because their **dialogue remains technically intelligible**, prefer disembodied voices to the author's oral/physical storytelling, or require every gesture to contribute new plot information.
- **Effect of either choice:** Evaluate what a line contributes to **intent, power, character, comic timing, vulnerability, social atmosphere, embodied experience, and the reader's pleasure** as well as cause and clarity.
