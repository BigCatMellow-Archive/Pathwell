Chapter Eight

The car found the ditch before she found the brakes.

The rear end went first, swinging wide, and the front followed it like a question resolving into an answer she hadn't wanted. The telephone pole was just there, the way telephone poles are — stationary and committed. The car wrapped itself around it with a sound like all her dishes breaking at once and then was very still.

The dust settled.

Sound came back wrong: the engine ticking first, then glass settling somewhere near her shoe, then Stansbury coughing from inside the car. The seatbelt was still across her in her mind, though she was in the dirt and one side of her skirt was wet. She tried to put the pieces in order — road, pole, Stansbury, Pathwell — and the order would not hold.

Elizabeth was sitting in the ditch, six feet from the car, without a clear account of how she'd gotten there. The side mirror was in the road somewhere.

Pathwell came around the hood of the car, moving with the careful precision of someone checking a hypothesis and trying very hard not to look drunk. He crouched in front of her. "You okay there, Elizabeth?"

"I think so." She put her hand to the back of her head. Nothing wet. "What happened?"

"You crashed the car." He helped her to her feet, held on while her knees sorted themselves out. "I may have contributed to the conditions."

The pole was through the front of the car. Stansbury was still in the passenger seat.

"Are you okay?" she said.

Pathwell turned his attention from the car, almost surprised by the question. "Oh, me? Yes." He did a slow rotation — arms out, showing untouched clothes, coat undamaged. "See? Fine. You really should be more careful, dear."

"Elizabeth!" Stansbury's voice, from inside the car. Then again, louder: "Elizabeth!"

He was braced in the passenger seat, bleeding from the nose, watching her through the cracked windshield. Then Pathwell. Then Elizabeth and back to Pathwell, longer this time.

Elizabeth turned her head.

For half a second she reached toward him anyway. Same height. Same coat. Relief got there before sense did.

The man standing next to her was wearing Pathwell's coat wrong.

Same ribboned jacket. Same long hands. The smile arrived half a second late.

Her chest pulled back before the rest of her had a name for it. She knew it the way you know a smell before you place it: not the knowledge itself but the fact of the knowledge, landing ahead of the name.

She took a step back.

He glanced up. The expression on his face was the one Pathwell wore when something had gone sideways earlier than expected. "Honestly, I had hoped it wouldn't come to this." A pause, something resigned settling in. "Well. At least not this soon."

"Who are you?"

"You can just call me Mr. Shade." He said it without looking up from the papers.

"You're wearing his coat wrong."

He glanced down at himself. "Yes. It keeps doing that."

He finished sorting through the papers, took one sheet in hand, and said something under his breath that didn't carry.

Her hand found his sleeve instead of the ground. Wrong fabric, wrong angle, but her balance had already chosen.

The air moved.

Not wind — something with direction and intention, a wall of it that hit her sideways and put her on the ground. She got a knee under herself. A second gust came in behind the first, hotter and lower, and she went flat. Through the dust and the noise Pathwell was tucked behind the car, his mouth moving in words that didn't reach her.

"Stay," Shade said, and the third gust said it too.

She was almost upright when his hand found the back of her coat and lifted.

"We have somewhere to be," he said. "And I do intend to be on time."

He walked her across the road like that — her feet finding the ground and losing it in turns. Her eye found the side mirror in the road. It was catching the light.

He opened the back door of a car parked on the far shoulder. She didn't scream. He didn't look back to see if she would. He closed the door. Inside: cold, and the particular quiet of a car that had been driving alone for a long time. He settled himself in the driver's seat, checked his mirrors, and stuck his arm straight out the open window before pulling onto the road in a sharp turn.

The wreck shrank behind them.

Pathwell stayed in the ditch until the car was gone. Then he stayed there awhile longer anyway.


Stansbury climbed out through the window frame, the door having made up its mind against opening, and stood in the road gripping his side.

"What," he said, "was that."

"That," Pathwell said, "was me."

Stansbury looked at the horizon where the car had been. "No wonder he was such a jerk." He put a hand on Pathwell's shoulder. "Come on."

"She's — "

"I know where he'll take her." He nudged him gently away from the horizon. "Bar's the other way."
