Chapter Six

The bar was neater than it had any right to be.

Not clean — clean was a different aspiration — but cared for, the way things get when people have been coming to them for a long time. The plastic on the stools was cracked and worn smooth. The television in the top corner played a baseball game with the sound off. The dartboard had been hit so many times it was more hole than board, and someone had repaired the worst section with a different color of cork.

A mounted fish watched the room from above the register with the exhausted expression of something that had heard all of this before.

The bartender had roughly the same face.

"Appletini for her," Pathwell said.

Elizabeth turned.

"No."

Pathwell stopped halfway toward the dartboard.

"No?"

"I don't want an appletini."

"You haven't tried it."

"That has not prevented me from forming an opinion."

Stansbury sat down at the bar.

"She's learning."

Pathwell looked from one to the other.

"Fine. What do you want?"

Elizabeth looked at the bottles behind the bartender.

She had no idea.

The bartender waited without interest.

"Ginger ale."

Pathwell stared at her.

"We came to a bar."

"You came to a bar. I happen to be in it."

Stansbury smiled into his beer.

Pathwell pointed at him.

"This is spreading."

He moved toward the dartboard.

The bartender set a glass of ginger ale in front of Elizabeth and a bottle of domestic beer in front of Stansbury.

"What does he drink?" the bartender asked, nodding toward Pathwell.

Stansbury watched his brother throw a dart without looking at the board.

It landed two inches from the center.

"Whatever requires the most explanation."

The bartender nodded as though this were enough information to work with.

Elizabeth rested an elbow on the bar.

The wood was worn smooth in exactly that spot — not damaged, just changed by a thousand people making the same small decision.

Pathwell threw another dart.

Bullseye.

He immediately looked disappointed with it.

"He's going to pretend that was luck," Stansbury said.

"I was not."

"You were about to."

Pathwell pulled the darts free.

"You two have become unpleasantly collaborative."

Elizabeth drank her ginger ale.

For several minutes, nothing happened.

That turned out to be good.

A man at the pool table missed an easy shot and blamed the cue. Someone near the door laughed too loudly at something Elizabeth had not heard. The bartender opened a jar of pickled eggs, reconsidered the smell, and closed it again.

Stansbury ordered fried fish.

Pathwell objected to the batter on philosophical grounds.

Nobody asked Elizabeth what she was doing with her life.

Nobody needed anything from her.

Nobody tried to explain magic.

She found herself watching the brothers instead.

They knew how to occupy the same room without paying attention to each other, which was apparently different from ignoring each other. Stansbury knew which dart Pathwell would reach for before he reached for it. Pathwell moved Stansbury's beer six inches away from the edge of the bar without looking. Neither acknowledged either act.

It was the kind of familiarity built out of repetition rather than affection and therefore probably more durable.

Stansbury ate one piece of fish.

Pathwell appeared beside him immediately.

"How is it?"

"Good."

"Really?"

"No. I ordered it because I enjoy disappointment."

Pathwell took one from the basket.

Stansbury slapped his hand with a napkin.

"Order your own."

"We're family."

"That has never entitled you to my fish."

"What, then, is family for?"

"Usually this argument."

Elizabeth laughed.

Pathwell looked at her.

"You see what I endure."

"I have been with you for one night and I already think he deserves the fish."

"Betrayal."

The bartender put a second basket in front of Pathwell without being asked.

Pathwell looked at him.

"You understand me."

"You ordered this last time."

Pathwell considered that.

"Less romantic, but acceptable."

Elizabeth looked at Stansbury.

"How long ago was last time?"

"Don't ask questions that encourage him."

"Seventeen years," Pathwell said.

The bartender shrugged.

"You complained about the batter then too."

Elizabeth looked at Pathwell.

"Consistency."

"Stagnation," Stansbury said.

"Branding."

The room warmed around them.

Not magically. Just people arriving, coats coming off, the door opening and shutting, glasses collecting water rings on the bar.

Elizabeth realized, slowly, that she was having a decent time.

That felt irresponsible.

She let it happen anyway.


Stansbury waited until Pathwell went to retrieve his darts before speaking quietly.

"You chose to come with him from Camp?"

Elizabeth looked at him.

"Yes."

"He didn't still have something of yours?"

"No."

"Nobody was chasing you?"

"No."

Stansbury nodded.

"All right."

Elizabeth waited for the rest.

There wasn't any.

"That's it?"

"What else did you want?"

"Most people seem to have an opinion about what I should do next."

"I have several."

"And?"

"You didn't ask for them."

Elizabeth looked at him for a second.

"That is a surprisingly rare skill tonight."

Stansbury took a drink.

"Don't confuse restraint with virtue. I've known him too long. Sometimes I just know which argument isn't worth having."

Pathwell called from the dartboard.

"I can hear you."

"That was deliberate."

Pathwell threw another dart.

It missed the board completely.

Everyone looked at it where it stuck in the wood paneling.

Pathwell stared.

Stansbury stared.

Elizabeth looked at Pathwell's hand.

He flexed his fingers once.

"That," Stansbury said, "was not deliberate."

"The wall had been arrogant all evening."

Pathwell went to retrieve the dart.

Elizabeth watched him cross the room.

He looked fine.

Which, she had recently learned, was apparently a posture.


Pathwell disappeared toward the back hall a few minutes later.

Elizabeth assumed bathroom until several minutes became longer than a bathroom should require unless something had gone badly.

Stansbury did not look toward the hall.

He did stop eating.

"Does he do that?" Elizabeth asked.

"Do what?"

"Go quiet and disappear."

"He disappears constantly. Quiet is newer."

Stansbury set down his fork.

He still did not go after him.

Elizabeth noticed that too.


The back hall was narrow, lit by one yellow bulb.

A dead pay phone hung on the wall beside a mirror spotted dark around the edges where the silver had failed.

Pathwell stopped in front of it.

For a moment he saw exactly what he expected.

His own face.

Older than it looked. Tired around the eyes. Hair refusing several decades of advice.

Then the tension returned.

It had been with him in small ways for longer than he cared to count. A pull without destination. A thread caught somewhere behind the ribs and drawn taut at irregular intervals. Usually easy to dismiss as pruning damage, exhaustion, age, or one of the many consequences he had collected and declined to inventory properly.

This time it tightened hard enough to make him stop.

Pathwell turned his shoulders slightly left.

The tension eased.

He turned right.

It sharpened again.

He frowned and turned back.

Loose.

Right again.

Taut.

Not a direction he trusted. Barely a direction at all. More the difference between pulling against a snag and moving with it.

In the mirror, his reflection looked ordinary.

Then, for one heartbeat, it didn't.

Nothing changed that he could name.

Same coat. Same face. Same eyes.

But the arrangement was wrong in the smallest possible way, like hearing your own voice on a recording and recognizing every word without recognizing the man who said them.

Pathwell leaned closer.

His reflection leaned closer.

Perfectly.

Of course it did.

He felt ridiculous.

The tension tightened again when he angled toward the far wall.

Pathwell stopped testing it.

The apartment door.

The blob already there before the cookbook.

The way it had made sense only after Elizabeth broke the working.

The long intermittent tension he had filed under damage because damage was easier than mystery.

His own face in the mirror suddenly felt less like evidence than accusation.

"No," he said quietly.

The mirror offered no opinion.

The pull faded.

For several seconds Pathwell stood alone beneath the yellow bulb.

Then he straightened his coat and went back to the bar.


Elizabeth knew something had happened because Pathwell came back without talking.

He sat beside Stansbury.

He moved his untouched drink half an inch to the left.

Then back.

His right hand went to his sleeve and stopped there.

Stansbury looked at it.

"What?"

Pathwell looked toward Elizabeth.

Then toward the television.

"Later."

Stansbury's face changed very little.

Elizabeth had started noticing that little could be enough.

"You said you came here because something didn't fit," she said.

Pathwell looked at her.

"Yes."

"Does something fit now?"

He considered lying.

She saw that too.

"Something points in an unpleasant direction," he said.

"Which direction?"

Pathwell looked down at his hand.

"Mine."

Stansbury became completely still.

Elizabeth felt the bar continue around them.

Pool balls struck. The bartender laughed at someone near the register. The mounted fish continued withholding judgment.

"What does that mean?" she asked.

"I don't know yet."

The answer sounded unnatural coming from Pathwell.

That made her believe it.

Stansbury pushed his basket of fish away.

"Tell me."

Pathwell looked at Elizabeth again.

Then back at Stansbury.

"Not here."

Elizabeth heard the distinction.

Not now.

Not here.

Not necessarily not her.

She decided to let him have that much.

For the moment.

Pathwell picked up a dart.

He did not throw it.

He turned it between his fingers instead.

The fun had not vanished.

It had simply become something they were all pretending they could get back to.

They couldn't.


Stansbury paid the tab.

One beer.

One ginger ale.

Two baskets of fish.

A bowl of peanuts nobody remembered ordering.

Pathwell looked at the receipt.

"You tipped too much."

"You tip too little."

"I tip historically."

"That doesn't mean anything."

"It has meant something in several currencies."

The bartender took the receipt away before the argument could become archival.

Outside, evening had settled over the parking lot.

The western sky was red beneath a low bank of cloud.

Elizabeth stopped at the bottom of the steps.

"Red sky at night," she said.

Stansbury came out behind her.

"Sailors' delight," Elizabeth continued. "My mom used to say that. Means tomorrow's supposed to be good."

Pathwell stood in the doorway.

"We still have to get there first."

He looked toward the Cadillac.

Then to the far edge of the lot.

That same stillness returned.

Shorter this time.

Sharper.

Stansbury saw it.

He took the keys from his pocket and held them out to Elizabeth.

She looked at them.

"Why me?"

"Because I want the passenger seat."

"That is not an explanation."

"It's the useful version."

Pathwell looked at him.

Stansbury looked back.

Elizabeth understood enough.

He wanted to watch his brother.

Possibly question him.

Possibly stop him from opening a door in a moving car.

All seemed reasonable.

"You know how to drive?" Stansbury asked.

Elizabeth took the keys.

"Yes."

"Good."

Pathwell pointed toward the Cadillac.

"For the record, I am an excellent driver."

"No one asked," Elizabeth said.

Stansbury smiled.

Pathwell looked betrayed.

Elizabeth closed her hand around the keys.

Behind them, the screen door slapped shut.

For one second the night was quiet enough that she could hear insects in the grass beyond the lot.

Then the smell reached them.

Low tide.

Wet rot.
