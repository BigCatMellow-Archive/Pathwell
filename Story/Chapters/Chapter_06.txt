Chapter Six

The bar was neater than it had any right to be. Not clean — clean was a different aspiration — but cared for, the way things get when people have been coming to them for a long time. The plastic on the barstools was cracked and worn smooth. The TV in the top corner played a game with the sound off. It had probably been that way for years. The dartboard had been hit so many times it was more hole than board, and someone had repaired the worst of it with a different color of cork.

The bartender had the practiced patience of someone who had heard everything.

"Appletini for her," Pathwell said, before Elizabeth could open her mouth.

She looked at him.

"You'll like it," he said, and moved off toward the dartboard.

Stansbury ordered something domestic for both of them, slid a bottle toward where Pathwell had been standing, and carried his own to a stool at the far end of the bar. He sat with his back mostly to the room.

Elizabeth stared at the drink in front of her.

She rested her elbow on the bar. The wood was worn smooth in exactly the spot where a thousand people had rested their elbow — not worn away, just different from the wood on either side. Polished by the same decision made consistently.

"You okay there?" the bartender asked.

"I don't know," she said. The bartender nodded like this was a reasonable answer and went back to wiping the counter.


The appletini was fine. She drank it slowly. Pathwell was throwing darts he'd found somewhere, with the calm focus of someone who had done this in a hundred bars before this one. He threw without looking at the board. Most of them landed.

He was not cold so much as careful with warmth.

She glanced toward him. He was already pulling another dart from the board and wasn't looking back.

"He's going to pretend he doesn't know what he's doing," Stansbury said. He'd moved without her noticing, was now on the stool beside her. "He always does. He's been doing it since before your grandmother was born."

"Is that supposed to be reassuring?"

"No." Stansbury turned his bottle in his hands. "I want to say something to you and I need you to hear it rather than react to it."

She turned to look at him.

"Go home," he said. "I mean it. Take a cab, call someone, walk — however you want to do it. Go home tonight."

"My apartment is — "

"I know about the apartment. I'm not talking about the apartment." He turned the bottle in his hands until the label faced away from him. "You went through the door. You gave something up you didn't have to. That's more than most. You can stop here."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I've seen what happens to people Pathwell takes an interest in." He set the bottle down. "The cost lands somewhere. Usually not where he was looking."

She turned the appletini glass in her hands, the stem slick from condensation. The ice had mostly gone. The drink had mostly happened before she'd noticed she was having it.

Pathwell appeared at her other shoulder. "Stans, why is my drink wearing fruit?"

"Old times," Stansbury said.

"You didn't correct him," Elizabeth said.

Pathwell looked at her.

"When he said what he said." She turned the appletini glass in her hands. "You didn't correct him."

Pathwell picked up his bottle from the bar and considered it. "No," he said, after a moment. "I suppose I didn't." He took a drink. "Another round?"

She didn't say yes. She didn't leave either. She sat with the empty glass.


Pathwell disappeared for a while. Darts, she assumed, or the back somewhere.

The back hall had the shape of back halls in bars that have been the same floor plan for thirty years: narrow, one overhead bulb, a pay phone that hadn't worked since the century turned. There was a mirror at the end of it. Nothing significant about the mirror.

Pathwell stood in front of it.

His face. The right face, in the right mirror, at the right angle. And then — not wrong exactly — *off.* His own face in the silver, looking back at him from a degree or two from where he'd put it. The way a recording sounds like you but assembled wrong, the breath landing between the words instead of under them.

Something moved in him that was not him. Not pain — he'd felt enough of that to recognize it on arrival. His hands wanted to reach toward the glass. He gripped the pay phone instead.

The face in the mirror held a second past where a reflection should stop. He was aware of his own breathing, of the overhead bulb, of the particular quality of silence in a back hall that had been quiet in exactly this way for thirty years. He was aware of all of these things in the manner you become aware of your own heartbeat — not wrong, just suddenly audible.

The mirror was just a mirror again.

He let go of the pay phone slowly. The dent his thumb had made in the plastic was something he would not recall making.

He stood there. The beer. The pay phone. The thin crack in the mirror's corner that had not been there a second ago.

He filed these where he filed things that had happened in hallways.

He fixed his coat and went back out.

He ordered a drink he didn't finish. Left it sweating on the bar, moved it half an inch to the left, moved it back. His hand found the edge of his own sleeve and checked it, once, for something that wasn't there.

Stansbury ordered the fish he'd been thinking about since the parking lot and ate them at the bar with the serious focus of someone paying off a debt to themselves.

The bar got louder as the evening came in. More of the half-full parking lot migrated inside. Elizabeth stopped trying to follow the conversations around her and let it become noise.

A jar of pickled eggs sat at the far end of the counter. Nobody had touched it in a long time. It had achieved the quality of furniture. For a while, it was the safest thing in the room to look at.

When Pathwell came back he was quieter.

He came back, he sat down, he nodded when Stansbury said something about the road. Something had gone out of the room he took up.

She didn't say anything.

Stansbury caught it anyway. He caught most things, unfortunately, and then ordered another round.

The next hour loosened both of them. Not happily. Pathwell drank like a man trying to get ahead of a thought. Stansbury drank like a man who had been waiting years for the thought to catch up.

Elizabeth counted without deciding to. Two bottles apiece, then a third for Stansbury, a fourth she lost track of for Pathwell. It wasn't judgment. It was arithmetic — the kind you do when you've noticed, quietly, that you're the only one at the table who'll still be able to find the door later.

"You never do this halfway," Stansbury said.

"I do many things halfway."

"Name one."

"Apologies."

"Exactly."

Pathwell lifted his glass. "That was not the rebuttal you think it was."

They went on like that. Elizabeth sat between them and slightly behind the conversation, which turned out to be a place designed for disappearing.


"Red sky at night," Elizabeth said.

She'd stepped outside for air and found the parking lot painted red.

Pathwell and Stansbury came out behind her.

"Sailors' delight," she said. "My mom used to tell me that. It means tomorrow's going to be good."

"We still have to get there first," Pathwell said.

Stansbury produced the keys from his pocket, looked at them for a moment, and held them out to Elizabeth.

She looked at the keys. Then at him.

"You've had one appletini," Stansbury said. "And you hated it."

"I didn't hate it."

"You hated what it represented." He dropped the keys into her hand. "Close enough."

Pathwell looked at the keys, then at Stansbury. "I am perfectly capable of driving."

"You are perfectly capable of convincing yourself of that," Stansbury said.

"Those are different."

"Not to a telephone pole."

She took the keys.
