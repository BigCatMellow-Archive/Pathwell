Chapter Sixteen

The archive roof came down and Camp Cunnan kept moving.

That was the first thing Elizabeth understood about aftermath.

Nothing waited for meaning.

The roof collapsed inward in a cough of sparks and black canvas. Three people shouted at once. Somebody dragged the nearest water barrel farther from the heat. Somebody else kicked burning paper away from the wheel of the next wagon. The bucket line shifted ten feet to the left because the place where it had been useful was no longer the place where water could do anything.

Mama Baga stood in the middle of it with soot across one cheek and pointed at problems one at a time.

"That axle first."

Two people moved.

"Keep the children behind the blue wagon."

Someone answered.

"Stop putting water on the front. It's gone. Save the sidewall."

The line changed again.

Nobody asked whether the archive could still be saved.

It could not.

That fact did not make the other work disappear.

Elizabeth was sitting in the dirt because at some point after the roof collapsed her legs had stopped participating.

Milo sat six feet away with his right trouser leg cut open to the knee and both hands clenched around the edge of a blanket.

His right foot was bare.

His left sock had fallen down again.

The healer pressed along his ankle.

"This?"

"No."

"This?"

"Ow."

"Useful."

"For who?"

"Me."

Milo considered that deeply unfair.

The archivist sat behind him with one hand on his shoulder.

Not holding him down.

Just there.

Elizabeth watched the healer test the leg again.

Milo hissed.

Alive.

The word kept arriving as if somebody had to keep delivering it.

Alive.

The diary was gone.

The cookbook was gone.

Shade was gone.

Milo was alive.

Her mind could hold all four facts but not yet arrange them into anything useful.

"Elizabeth."

She looked up.

The Camp healer had finished with Milo and was crouching in front of her now.

"Shoulder."

Elizabeth looked down.

Her left arm hung badly inside the remains of the sling.

"Still attached."

"A low standard."

"It has been a morning."

The healer looked toward the place where the archive had been.

"Apparently. Can I check it?"

Elizabeth almost said yes automatically.

Stopped.

The pause hurt less than the shoulder did.

"Yes."

The healer moved carefully.

Her fingers found the joint.

Elizabeth swore.

Milo looked impressed.

The archivist said, "Don't learn that one."

"I already knew it."

"Then forget it."

The healer ignored them.

"Still in."

Elizabeth breathed out.

"Good."

"More swelling. You strained everything I told you not to strain."

"There was a shelf."

"Shelves are famous for respecting medical instructions."

"This one was especially difficult."

The healer retied the sling tighter and packed a cold wet cloth around the shoulder.

"You use this arm again today and I will tie you to the wagon."

Elizabeth looked at Mama Baga.

"Is that allowed?"

Mama Baga did not turn around.

"No."

The healer sighed.

"Fine. I will ask you very firmly not to."

"That seems less effective."

"It is what we have."

Elizabeth nodded.

"I'll try."

The healer looked at her.

Elizabeth corrected herself.

"I won't use it unless somebody is under furniture again."

"Better."

Not approval.

A workable agreement.

The healer moved on.


Stansbury was next.

He sat on an overturned bucket with his right hand submerged in a basin of clean water gone pink around the wrist.

His face had settled into the expression of a man who considered pain a clerical inconvenience.

The healer knelt beside him.

"Out."

Stansbury lifted the hand from the basin.

The burn had changed since Elizabeth last saw it.

Red across the back of the hand and wrist. Blisters rising. One strip near the thumb already pale in a way Elizabeth did not like.

Stansbury looked at it as though someone had damaged a tool he owned.

"Can you move the fingers?" the healer asked.

He did.

Slowly.

"Sensation?"

"Yes."

"All of them?"

He touched thumb to each fingertip.

"Yes."

The healer nodded.

"Good. This is going to take a while."

"Define a while."

"Longer than you want."

"Everything does."

She began cleaning the edges.

Stansbury's jaw tightened.

He did not pull away.

Pathwell watched from twenty feet away.

Someone had moved him onto a blanket after the blob left. His coat was open. The gauze at his temple had been replaced. His hands lay palm-up beside him, empty.

For once, empty did not look temporary.

The healer had already checked him.

Ribs bruised. Chest impact. Exhaustion. Pruning shock. No immediate crisis that outranked the people currently being treated.

So he waited.

Elizabeth noticed how strange that looked on him.

Pathwell noticed her noticing.

He looked away first.


The fire stopped being dangerous before it stopped being fire.

By late morning the archive was a black frame, warped wheels, wet ash, and sections of wall that still breathed smoke when the wind changed.

Camp people moved through the perimeter with long hooks, pulling apart anything that might hold heat.

The archivist stood beside the saved records with a piece of cardboard balanced on his knee.

There was no archive desk anymore.

So the cardboard became one.

The blue road notebook.

The cloth-wrapped family letters.

One flat box Stansbury had pulled from the side compartment.

Another box somebody had found beneath the wagon after the wall collapsed, soaked through but unopened.

A metal cash tin containing accession slips.

Three ledger pages blackened around the edges.

That was not all that had survived.

It was enough to begin counting.

The archivist wrote each item down on a sheet clipped to the cardboard.

Elizabeth watched him reach the bottom of the page.

He stared at the list.

Then at the ruin.

"How many?" Mama Baga asked.

"Saved?"

"Start there."

He read the list.

Nobody said the number was good.

Mama Baga waited.

"Lost?"

The archivist looked at the ruin again.

"I don't know yet."

"Then write that."

He did.

UNKNOWN.

The word occupied its own line.

Mama Baga nodded.

"Next."

The archivist swallowed.

"The road copies were in the rear cabinet. Most are gone. We have the blue notebook and the current north-route copy. The healer found it folded in Milo's trouser pocket."

Milo looked up from the blanket around his legs.

"I got it."

The archivist looked at him.

"You did."

Milo sat a little straighter.

The archivist continued.

"Cookbook reserve section is gone. Intake is gone. Weather shelf, mostly gone. Family correspondence, some saved. Medical records—"

His voice stopped.

Mama Baga waited.

"Gone," he said.

The healer, across the clearing, closed her eyes once.

Then opened them and continued wrapping Stansbury's hand.

Elizabeth put her right hand against her coat pocket.

Nothing there.

The motion happened before she could stop it.

Checking.

Like a tongue finding the place a tooth used to be.

Her hand stayed there.

The diary had not been hers when it burned.

That did not stop her body from expecting its weight.

The cookbook had not been hers either.

That did not stop her from knowing exactly where on Nana's cover the flour stain had been.

Loss apparently did not care much about paperwork.


Milo limped over after the healer finished splinting his ankle.

Not broken, she thought.

Badly bruised. Maybe sprained. Stay off it.

Milo had interpreted this to mean limp dramatically with assistance.

The archivist had given him a broom handle as a temporary crutch.

He stopped beside Elizabeth.

"Was that your book?"

Elizabeth looked at the ruin.

"Which one?"

"The one by the door."

"It belonged to Camp."

Milo frowned.

"You wrote it."

"Yes."

"So it was yours."

"Both can be true."

He considered this.

"That's annoying."

"Very."

Milo leaned on the broom handle.

"The other one?"

"My grandmother's cookbook. Also Camp's."

"You could have grabbed them."

The sentence had no accusation in it.

That made it easier and harder.

"Yes."

"But you got me."

"Yes."

Milo looked toward the ash.

Then down at his bare foot.

"My boot burned too."

Elizabeth looked at him.

He nodded solemnly.

"It was a good boot."

"I'm sorry."

"The other one is still good."

"That's usually how pairs become less useful."

Milo thought about that.

"I could give it to somebody with one foot."

Elizabeth laughed.

Her shoulder punished her immediately.

Milo looked pleased with himself.

Then his face changed.

His eyes went toward the empty patch of ground beyond the failed brass frame.

"Shade's dead?"

The question arrived without warning because children were apparently permitted to do that.

Elizabeth looked at the same patch.

"Yes."

Milo tightened both hands around the broom handle.

"Did the blob eat him?"

"Yes."

"Was he a blob?"

"No."

"Was he magic?"

Elizabeth looked down at her right hand.

"Partly. I don't know."

Milo waited.

"He was Shade," she said.

That was the only answer she trusted.

Milo nodded.

"He cheated at Hearts."

"He was losing."

"That's why he cheated."

Elizabeth looked at him.

"Did you actually catch him cheating?"

Milo looked offended.

"No."

"Strong case."

He shifted the broom handle under his arm.

"He still owes me a hand."

Elizabeth's throat closed so quickly it hurt.

Milo looked back toward the empty dirt.

"That counts as dying, right?"

Mama Baga had come up behind them without Elizabeth noticing.

"Yes," she said.

Milo turned.

Mama Baga looked at Elizabeth.

"His name was Shade?"

Elizabeth nodded.

"Shade."

Mama Baga looked toward the empty ground.

Then toward the remains of the card table, where several wet playing cards had been gathered into a stack.

"Then Shade died here this morning."

No prayer or ruling followed. Mama Baga said his name once because someone had died and names were one of the few things the fire had not managed to take.

The archivist looked up from his cardboard desk.

He found a clean corner on the loss sheet.

He wrote:

SHADE — DIED AT CAMP.

Then, after a moment:

GREEN JACKET, CAMP ISSUE — LOST WITH HIM.

Milo leaned over to read it.

"He liked the pockets."

The archivist added:

LIKED POCKETS.

Mama Baga looked at him.

"That is not an archive category."

"Archive burned. Categories are temporarily flexible."

Mama Baga considered this.

"Fair."

Elizabeth stared at the line until the letters blurred.

That was as much funeral as Camp had time for.

It was enough to make the grief worse.


Pathwell approached only after he had been told he could stand.

He walked slowly.

Not performatively.

His ribs would not permit performance.

He stopped several feet from Elizabeth.

He did not sit beside her.

He did not say Lizzy.

"Elizabeth."

She looked at him.

He had ash on one knee and dried blood beneath the clean gauze.

He looked exhausted enough that under different circumstances she might have wanted to help him.

Those circumstances were not available.

"I am sorry," he said.

Elizabeth waited.

Pathwell did not add because.

Did not say he had been trying to fix it.

Did not mention how much future he had lost.

That helped less than he probably hoped.

"For which part?" she asked.

He looked toward the archive.

"The museum. Your shoulder. The working. Shade. The archive."

"Those aren't one thing."

"No."

"Don't make them one thing because one apology is easier."

Pathwell's mouth closed.

The old reflex to answer quickly reached his face and stopped there.

"All right," he said.

Elizabeth looked at him.

"I don't know if I forgive you."

"I'm not asking you to."

"Good."

The word came harder than she intended.

She let it.

Pathwell nodded.

He stayed where he was.

"What do you need?" he asked.

Elizabeth almost laughed.

Not because it was funny.

Because after everything, the question sounded suspicious coming from him.

"Right now?"

"Right now."

She looked toward Milo, who had returned to the archivist's cardboard ledger and was attempting to contribute spelling corrections.

Then toward the empty place where Shade had been.

Then toward Pathwell.

"I need you not to make me responsible for whether you become a better person."

Pathwell went still.

Elizabeth continued before he could answer.

"I am not going to tell you what lesson to learn so you can learn it at me. I am not going to make your guilt useful. I am not going to say losing Shade was worth something because maybe you change after it."

Pathwell looked down.

"All right."

"And if you say 'I know,' I may hit you with Milo's broom."

A beat.

"I was going to say all right again."

"Good choice."

"Rare morning for those."

Elizabeth stared at him.

The joke died immediately.

"Sorry," he said.

"You don't get to use being funny to decide when this becomes easier."

Pathwell nodded.

"All right."

This time nothing followed.

Elizabeth looked away first.

That ended the conversation for now. Nothing more.


Camp dealt with Pathwell after lunch.

Not before.

That seemed to bother him more than anger would have.

By then the dangerous heat was gone. Milo had been fed. Stansbury's hand was wrapped from knuckles to halfway up the forearm. Elizabeth's shoulder had been iced, rebound, and threatened with further medical bureaucracy. The saved records had been moved beneath a dry awning and separated into wet, scorched, and stable piles.

Only then did Mama Baga put a chair in the middle of the clearing.

She did not tell Pathwell to sit in it.

She sat in it herself.

Pathwell stood across from her.

Stansbury sat nearby with his burned hand elevated on a folded blanket.

The archivist held the cardboard inventory.

The healer remained because three of the people involved were injured and she apparently trusted nobody to remain medically irrelevant for more than ten minutes.

Elizabeth stayed at the edge of the group.

Mama Baga looked at Pathwell.

"What do you owe?"

Pathwell looked toward the archive.

"Everything we lost."

"Wrong."

He looked back at her.

Mama Baga pointed to the archivist.

"Can he replace everything we lost?"

"No."

"Can you?"

"No."

"Then stop naming impossible payment as if saying a large enough number is accountability."

Pathwell said nothing.

Mama Baga nodded toward the cardboard inventory.

"Again. What do you owe?"

Pathwell took longer.

"Work. Restitution where restitution is possible. Replacement of practical materials. Repair of what can be repaired."

"Defined by whom?"

He hesitated.

Elizabeth watched the hesitation.

"Not me," Pathwell said.

Mama Baga leaned back.

"Better."

The archivist lifted the inventory.

"Blank archival paper. Storage boxes. Waxed cloth. Two replacement cabinets. Drying racks. Copying supplies. Route copies reconstructed from surviving originals and external copies where they exist."

Pathwell nodded.

The archivist continued.

"You will not touch charged originals without permission."

"Agreed."

"You will not prune to buy any of it."

Pathwell's eyes flicked up.

Mama Baga said nothing.

The silence did the work.

"Agreed," Pathwell said.

"You will not decide that something is close enough to replace what burned."

Pathwell looked toward the blue road notebook.

"Agreed."

"You will ask before reorganizing anything."

Stansbury made a small sound that might have been pain and might have been satisfaction.

Pathwell looked at him.

"You're enjoying this."

"Immensely."

Mama Baga looked at Stansbury.

"You are also injured because of him. Don't become decorative."

Stansbury's satisfaction vanished.

"Fair."

The archivist continued.

"Museum obligations remain separate."

"Yes," Pathwell said.

"Camp does not absorb those because you caused something worse afterward."

"Yes."

Mama Baga looked at Elizabeth.

"Anything you want named?"

There it was.

Want.

Not owed to the group because she was injured.

Not required testimony.

Elizabeth considered it.

"My shoulder is not a Camp expense."

Pathwell looked at her.

"It should be mine."

"That's not what I said."

He stopped.

Elizabeth looked at Mama Baga.

"I don't want compensation decided today."

Mama Baga nodded.

"Then it isn't."

Pathwell opened his mouth.

Closed it.

Good.

Mama Baga looked back at him.

"First rule of restitution: the person who broke the thing does not get to create the schedule because waiting makes him uncomfortable."

Pathwell absorbed that.

"Understood."

"Second rule: no pruning shortcuts."

"Understood."

"Third rule: when somebody tells you stop, develop a new hobby."

Stansbury said, "Cards."

Milo, from across the clearing, shouted, "He's probably terrible!"

Nobody laughed for a second.

Then the old woman in the knitted cap did.

Quietly.

The sound moved through the clearing without asking permission.

Elizabeth did not laugh.

She did not mind that someone else did.


By evening Stansbury's burn had stopped looking wet and started looking permanent.

The healer changed the dressing beneath a lantern near Mama Baga's wagon.

Pathwell sat on the step below him, holding the clean bandages because that was the job he had been given.

Nothing more.

Stansbury flexed his fingers while the healer examined the back of his hand.

"Movement's good," she said.

"Excellent."

"You will scar."

Stansbury looked at the burn.

"Probably."

"Definitely. Across the hand. Maybe the wrist. It may tighten when it heals. We can manage that with stretching."

Pathwell's attention sharpened.

Elizabeth saw it from where she sat by the central fire.

So did Stansbury.

"No," Stansbury said.

Pathwell blinked.

"I didn't say anything."

"You inhaled like an advertisement."

The healer looked between them.

Pathwell held the bandage roll very still.

"There are ways to reduce what remains after the skin closes."

Stansbury looked at his brother.

"Ordinary ways?"

Pathwell did not answer quickly enough.

"No."

"Would they require somebody's record?"

"Possibly. Not necessarily."

"Pruning?"

"No."

Stansbury considered the hand.

The burn had happened because he chose to enter the archive.

The archive had burned because Pathwell chose not to stop.

Both things were true.

Stansbury looked at the healer.

"Will the hand work?"

"If you do what I tell you, likely very well."

"Then treat the burn."

He looked at Pathwell.

"Let it be."

The words were quiet.

No challenge in them.

No punishment either.

A boundary.

Pathwell's fingers tightened once around the bandage roll.

Then loosened.

"All right."

He did not explain what he could have done.

Did not offer a better version.

Did not reach for his coat.

The healer held out her hand.

Pathwell gave her the bandage roll.

That was all.

Elizabeth watched him stop.

It was not enough to repair anything.

It was still different.


Night made the missing archive larger.

During the day there had always been someone crossing in front of the ruin, carrying something, counting something, wetting ash, moving a wheel, checking heat.

After dark the space stayed empty.

No wagon wall.

No door.

No yellow archive light.

Just a rectangle of darker ground and the smell of wet charcoal.

Elizabeth sat near it with a blanket around her shoulders.

Her left arm was back in the sling.

Her right hand kept finding the empty coat pocket.

Checking.

Nothing.

She let it happen this time.

The motion was part of the loss.

Not proof she had chosen wrong.

Milo had gone to sleep in another wagon with his ankle elevated and one surviving boot placed beside the cot for reasons nobody had challenged.

Stansbury was asleep or pretending to be, his wrapped hand resting on his chest.

Pathwell was carrying wet archive boxes from one tarp to another because the archivist had told him where they belonged.

He did not rearrange them.

That, too, was visible.

Mama Baga sat down beside Elizabeth.

Neither of them spoke for a while.

The Camp fiddle started somewhere behind the wagons.

Not a song Elizabeth knew.

Not a funeral tune as far as she could tell.

Just somebody playing softly because the day had been loud.

"He liked terrible coffee," Elizabeth said.

Mama Baga looked toward the fire.

"Shade?"

"Yes."

"He made terrible coffee."

"Aggressively."

"I tasted it."

Elizabeth looked at her.

Mama Baga's face remained solemn.

"I considered asking him to leave on that basis alone."

Elizabeth laughed before she could stop herself.

The laugh broke in the middle.

Then she was crying.

Not elegantly.

No gradual cinematic tear.

Her face folded and her right hand covered her mouth and suddenly her body had decided it had been patient long enough.

Mama Baga did not touch her.

She stayed beside her.

Elizabeth cried for Nana.

For the cookbook.

For the diary and every stupid grocery list inside it.

For the museum letters.

For Shade's green jacket and excessive pockets.

For a man whose history had only just begun and who had still managed to owe Milo a card game.

For the fact that choosing Milo had been right and had still cost her things she wanted back.

For the fact that none of those losses balanced each other.

When the crying slowed, Mama Baga handed her a clean cloth.

Elizabeth wiped her face.

"Was he a person?" she asked.

Mama Baga looked at the dark archive space.

"He said no when somebody tried to decide what happened to him."

Elizabeth waited.

"He chose to stay here. He played cards. He made bad coffee. He died."

Mama Baga looked at her.

"Person is the word I have for that."

Elizabeth nodded.

That answer hurt too.

It was supposed to.

Across the clearing Pathwell finished carrying a box to the tarp.

The archivist pointed to another.

Pathwell went to get it.

He did not look toward Elizabeth for approval.

Good.

Tomorrow there would be more lists.

The museum.

The archive.

Supplies.

Repairs.

People deciding which pieces of damage could be addressed and which could only be named.

Nothing would make Shade return.

Nothing would make the diary original again.

Nothing would make Nana's cookbook something other than ash.

The work would not become forgiveness just because it was necessary.

Elizabeth understood that before anyone tried to say otherwise.

She sat beside the empty archive until the fiddle stopped.

Then she went to sleep because morning was going to require things from all of them, and for once Pathwell would not be the person deciding what those things were.
