Chapter Twelve

Camp Cunnan was the same as it had been — the ring of wagons, the smaller fires, the ropes tied in knots that knew what they were doing. It was dark still, an hour before dawn, and most of the camp was quiet.

Mama Baga came down the steps of her wagon before they reached it. Elizabeth's arm. Shade. Nothing for a long moment.

"Bring her in," she said.

Inside the wagon it was close and warm and full of people deciding not to say things. The slight healer moved between Shade and the cot.

"No," Shade said.

Every spoon stopped in its cup.

He held up both hands. "Not no as in move. No as in that will set wrong if you do it that way."

"You a healer now?" the slight woman said.

"No." Shade nodded at Elizabeth's shoulder. "I've been broken in most of the available directions."

Mama Baga's bracelets clicked once. A girl went to the archive wagon and came back with a packet of letters tied in blue thread, the pages soft at the folds.

Shade took one sheet, read nothing on it, and pressed it between his palms. His right hand opened once toward the door, empty, as if answering a call no one had made.

He closed it before anyone could ask.

"Hold still," he said.

Elizabeth did not. Not at first.

He waited. He did not coax, did not explain, did not make himself softer than he was. When she nodded, he set the paper against her shoulder and said a word that made the lamps in the wagon lean.

The pain went bright and then thin. Her arm settled back into itself with a sensation like a drawer closing correctly after months of sticking.

Shade stepped back.

The healer touched Elizabeth's shoulder, then made a small offended sound.

"That'll hold," Shade said.

Mama Baga's bracelets clicked again.

Elizabeth sat with her arm in a sling. The diary was still in her bag, its corner pressing against her hip.


Shade was at the tree line when she found him, looking out at the dark. He did not seem haunted so much as accompanied.

"He'll be here by morning," he said, without turning.

"I know."

The fire from the camp reached them in pieces — light and warmth both, arriving and not quite arriving. She stood beside him and watched the same dark he was watching.

"He's going to try to cut me loose." He said it without emphasis. "He thinks that solves the problem."

"Does it?"

"It kills me." A pause. "What's left of me, anyway."

"Left over from what?"

"From him deciding I wasn't going to happen." He said it the way you'd give directions to somewhere you'd rather not go. "He does that. Decides ahead of time, so he doesn't have to watch it happen the slow way. Mostly it stays a decision. This once, it didn't."

He turned to look at her then. "I'm not telling you to stop him. I'm telling you because he won't tell you."

"That's not the same thing as warning me," she said.

He didn't answer.

The fire popped somewhere in the camp. A child was awake before dawn, running between the wagons on an errand that seemed urgent to them. The sound of small feet on dirt and then silence and then again.

She thought about what she wanted to say. The thing she kept arriving at was that she had no right to any of this — not the draw, not his choices, not whatever was happening between him and Pathwell that had been happening since before she'd been born. She also had a very specific right to all of it, because she was standing in it and had been since the night a blob came through her door.

"The draw won't just stop because you want it to," she said.

He went quiet.

"No," he said. "It won't."

He turned his cup in his hands. The gesture was Pathwell's, worn to something different by time and use, the way a path through a field becomes its own thing separate from whoever made it.

"I'm not going to tell him what to do," she said. "But I'm going to be there when he decides."

Shade looked at her.

"He's going to have to stop."

"He won't."

"Then I'll stand there until he has to notice."

He was quiet for a moment. The sky at the horizon had started to change in the particular way it changes before anything is certain — just slightly less dark, just the suggestion of something.

"You know," he said, "you're not what he described."

"What did he describe?"

"Something he was holding onto." He looked at the horizon. "You're not that."

She didn't answer.

He went quiet too, and they stood at the tree line for a while longer with the fire behind them and the dark in front, and the child ran another errand in the camp, and the fiddle started and stopped.
