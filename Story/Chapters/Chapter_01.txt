Chapter One

It was the crash that woke her.

For half a second Elizabeth lay still, trying to place it inside the apartment she had only lived in for three weeks.

Then came another.

Not from outside. From the living room.

She stepped into the hall barefoot and found a man tearing through her boxes.

He had one open at his knees and another overturned beside him, her things spread across the floor in the careless taxonomy of a burglary being conducted by someone with very specific standards. A framed photograph. Two paperbacks. Tax documents. A scarf she had been looking for since the move.

He lifted her diary, considered it for half a second, tucked it under his arm, and reached for the next box.

Elizabeth watched him do this because there are situations the mind rejects on administrative grounds before fear has a chance to get involved.

"Do you mind?"

"Where's your cookbook?" the stranger said without looking up.

Another box hit the floor.

"Excuse me?"

"The old one. Handwritten if possible. Family is better."

"Who are you?"

A wet thud hit the front door.

Both of them looked at it.

The wood bowed inward and settled back into place.

The stranger pointed at it. "Cookbook."

"What was that?"

"A reason to answer quickly."

Another thud. Wet. Heavy.

The deadbolt jumped.

"Kitchen," she said. "Cabinet over the refrigerator. There are three—"

He was gone before she finished.

Elizabeth stayed where she was, staring at the door.

Something moved on the other side. Not footsteps. Too much of it at once. A slow pressure against the frame followed by a sound like a soaked mattress being dragged across tile.

The stranger came back carrying Nana's cookbook.

Elizabeth's stomach dropped for an entirely different reason.

The cover had once been white. Years of flour, butter, fingerprints, and being shoved into drawers had turned it the color of old teeth. Three rubber bands held the spine together. Nana Jones had written the title on the front in blue marker because apparently the fact that it was full of recipes had not been sufficiently clear.

"Give me that."

"In a minute." He flipped through the pages. "Don't think. Favorite recipe."

"Give me the book."

The doorframe cracked.

He looked up at her.

"Favorite recipe."

"I don't know."

"Yes, you do. Don't think."

The answer arrived before she could stop it.

"Sugar cookies. My Nana made sugar cookies."

"Perfect."

His fingers moved through the stained pages quickly, slowing only when the handwriting changed from recipe to note and back again. He found the page by a faded paper tab.

NANA JONES SUGAR COOKIES.

Nana's looping handwriting filled the margins with substitutions, warnings, and one underlined instruction not to overmix because Nana had apparently once suffered a bad batch and chosen to make it everyone else's inherited problem.

The stranger put his palm flat against the page.

The ink warmed gold beneath his hand.

Elizabeth forgot the door.

"What are you doing?"

"Trying to keep the evening from getting worse."

The letters brightened. Not shining on the page. Shining through it. Gold gathered beneath his palm and began spreading toward the edges in fine branching lines.

The front door split down the center.

Black ooze pressed through the crack.

The smell arrived first.

Low tide. Wet rot. Something left too long in a sealed container and opened by mistake.

Elizabeth gagged.

The stranger didn't move his hand.

"Almost."

The gold on the page thickened into something brighter than light ought to be.

Elizabeth grabbed the cookbook.

"Stop."

He caught the other side. "Don't—"

She pulled.

The book came free.

The page did not.

It tore at the binding with a dry ripping sound, the glowing sheet staying beneath his palm while the rest of the cookbook slammed against Elizabeth's chest.

For one impossible second nothing happened.

Then everything went white.

Butter.

Sugar.

Rain through an open kitchen window.

Elizabeth was twelve years old with flour on her shirt and Nana Jones beside her, pushing the mixing bowl back across the counter.

Don't overmix them, baby. You'll toughen them.

Her grandmother's voice was so ordinary that it hurt worse than if it had sounded like a ghost.

Elizabeth's body moved before she did.

She stumbled backward into the wall, the cookbook clutched against her ribs.

The gold light in the stranger's hand buckled.

What had been gathering cleanly across the torn page broke apart. Bright threads snapped loose and scattered over the carpet, under the table, across his sleeve — pieces of warmth with nowhere obvious to go.

His expression changed.

Not fear.

Annoyance arriving one second before fear became necessary.

"That's new," he said.

The door came in.

It collapsed under the weight of something at least six feet across and considerably less committed to having a shape.

The blob pushed through the frame in a slow black fold, taking splintered wood with it. Its surface shifted whenever Elizabeth tried to focus on it. Every edge seemed to move a fraction before her eyes got there.

It had been coming into the apartment.

Then the loose gold on the floor snapped and hissed.

The blob changed direction.

Fast.

It went for the broken working.

The stranger shoved Elizabeth sideways as the thing crossed the room. The cookbook slipped from her grip and skidded under the table.

"Bathroom."

"What is that?"

"Bathroom is more useful."

The blob hit him before she moved.

He went into the wall hard enough to knock a framed print loose. The torn recipe page stayed in his hand, burning gold around the edges.

The blob rolled over the scattered light on the carpet.

Where black touched gold, the gold vanished.

Not dimmed. Gone.

The thing shuddered and pulled more of itself toward the spill.

The stranger looked at it, then at the remaining light trapped in the page.

"Oh, don't be greedy."

He slapped the page against the floor.

The impact sang.

A clear bright note rang through the apartment.

Light struck outward in a flat wave, sharp enough to ripple the blob back from him and soft enough that the framed photograph on the floor only slid three inches.

Elizabeth did not wait for clarification.

She ran for the bathroom.

The smell followed her. She made it to the toilet before her stomach decided the evening had contributed enough.

Behind her came a wet impact, a curse, something heavy hitting the wall, and the stranger saying, "That is entirely unnecessary," to whatever was trying to consume the remains of her living room.

Elizabeth slammed the bathroom door.

She pressed both hands against it and listened.

A thud.

Another.

The sound of furniture moving without anyone's permission.

Then a low, wet rumble that seemed to travel through the floorboards.

Silence.

Elizabeth kept her hands on the door.

Her heart was loud enough to qualify as a second emergency.

Something scraped across the hall outside the apartment.

Then farther away.

Then nothing.

A knock came from the other side of the bathroom door.

Three polite taps.

"You can come out now. It's mostly somewhere else."

Elizabeth opened the door two inches.

The living room looked as though a storm had developed strong opinions about personal property.

The front door was gone. A section of frame hung inward. Dark slime streaked the carpet and walls, already collapsing into dull wet patches that smelled worse than they looked. One lamp had survived by falling behind the couch. Everything else had participated.

The stranger stood in the middle of it all with her cookbook in one hand and her diary tucked under his arm.

The sugar-cookie page was gone.

Not burned. Gone. A ragged edge remained at the spine where it had torn free.

Elizabeth stared at it.

Then at him.

"Why are you holding my diary?"

He glanced down as if he'd forgotten it was there. "This?"

"Yes, this. Mine."

"Right." He adjusted it under his arm and looked toward the missing front door. "We should go."

"Put my things down."

"Absolutely. Somewhere else."

"I'm not going anywhere with you."

"Understandable."

He stepped over the remains of a box and looked into the hallway.

Elizabeth waited.

He looked left. Then right.

For the first time since she had walked into the room, he was still.

Something about that frightened her more than the talking had.

Then his face settled into certainty again.

"Your apartment's marked. Magically speaking."

"Marked by what?"

"Second-tier question."

"There are tiers?"

"There are now. First-tier question is whether you own shoes."

"You broke into my apartment, destroyed my door, tore a page out of my grandmother's cookbook, and now you're making up tiers."

"I didn't destroy the door."

She looked at the empty frame.

He followed her eyes.

"Not personally."

"I'm not leaving until you tell me what is going on."

"And I respect that position." He started toward the door. "Deeply inconvenient, but principled. Shoes?"

"Why could I hear her?"

That stopped him.

She looked at the cookbook in his hand.

"My Nana. I heard her voice."

He looked at the torn place in the spine.

Something in his expression softened, though not enough to become an apology.

"Because it was hers," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"No." He looked into the hallway again. "It's the amount of answer we have time for."

"I already told you I'm not—"

A wet thud sounded from somewhere down the hall.

Not close.

Not far enough.

The stranger's head turned toward it.

His hand tightened around her diary.

"Shoes," he said.

Elizabeth's heart skipped.

Another sound followed. Slow. Heavy. Something testing a door that did not belong to her.

She looked once at the wrecked living room. Her boxes. Her things. The bare patch of wall where the front door had been.

Then she jumped into the shoes by the coat rack and pulled on the first coat she could reach.

"Good," the stranger said.

"This does not mean I'm going with you."

"Of course not."

He took her hand anyway.

Elizabeth looked at him.

He smiled.

"Ready?"

"No."

"Perfect."
