Chapter Five

Pathwell wouldn't open anything near the wagons, so they walked until the trees thinned and the sky went gray. At the edge of a field Pathwell lay down in the grass and slept until well past noon, the way she imagined people slept after giving blood. The Space Between, Elizabeth guessed. She sat with one hand on the diary in her coat pocket and didn't sleep at all.

When he woke up, he rolled his sleeves to the elbow and looked around the field like a man measuring a room.

"Stay close, Lizzy. This next part is going to be fun."

He reached behind him and pulled out a handful of folded pages, stacked together in a mess. He gripped them tight, crumpling them in his hand, and a yellow light danced out in every direction from the edges of the paper. The fingers of his left hand turned in a circle, twisting and spinning. He opened that hand wide and snapped it shut, catching the light. A few more twists of his palm, a few flicks of his fingers, and he drew a circle on the ground around them.

From the circle, a golden line burst into the center, made a small bead there, and roped out in wild directions. The light burned Elizabeth's eyes and the ground beneath it, and the grass steamed. The circle spun around them, gaining speed. A wind kicked up from the ground. Elizabeth's new clothes filled with it, the loose hair behind her bandana danced in the swirling air, and Pathwell's coat took flight behind him and flapped. He tucked his arms into his chest. The wind stopped, and the circle stopped spinning.

"What was that all about?"

But as the words left her mouth, his arms shot out, palms open, fingers splayed. The circle took off again, spinning wildly, air shooting around them, and it was all Elizabeth could do to close her eyes. The wind whipped around her, gaining fury, and pulled the breath out of her. Her eyes watered. Her joints ached. Her bandana flew off her head, and she reached blindly to grab it before it was lost. Her hand was snatched back just as her fingers closed on it. Pathwell hugged her tight, and she shut her eyes again.

The wind let up. When she opened her eyes, the bandana in her hand had been singed in half.

Pathwell let go of her. The pages in his hand were gone. "You okay? Still got all your fingers and toes?"

She held the bandana up to his face. "You mean that would have done this to my fingers?"

"Oh, most certainly," he said.

"What was that?"

"That was a portal."

"To where?"

"Here."

"Where is here!?"

"I don't know," Pathwell said cheerfully. "Let's find out."

Gathering her wits, Elizabeth realized she was in a small room, just big enough for the two of them and some mop buckets. Her elbow bumped a shelf of cleaning supplies, and she lowered her arm. Pathwell found the door in front of them, opened it with blind confidence, and stood in the doorway a moment before leaning his head out and looking around. He closed it again.

"Looks clear."

"What do you mean, 'looks clear'? Where did you take me? Are we not supposed to be here?"

Pathwell shrugged. "Don't know. But it looks clear, and we aren't going to figure out much standing in here."

He opened the door and stepped out, took a few short, quick breaths, sniffing the air, then leaned back in. "Air's fine."

"Fine compared to what?" Elizabeth yelled in a whisper.

He shrugged again. "You never know with these sorts of things."

Through the open door, the hall was empty. White speckled tile, with a blue tile here or a red one there at random, sometimes next to each other and sometimes diagonal. The doors had numbers on them and names below that. One wall was lined with posters, and the other with double-stacked lockers.

"Why are there lockers on the wall?" Pathwell asked.

"What?" Elizabeth poked her head out the door. She saw the lockers, the posters, the names on the doors and the numbers above them, and she shrank back into the closet. In the quietest yell she could manage: "You brought us to a school!?"

"What?"

"A school, Pathwell. You know. A place where children learn things. Surely you've been to school before."

"Oh, that explains the posters." He walked up to one on the wall.

"What are we doing in a school, Pathwell?"

He looked back at her, still hiding in the closet. "Looking for my brother."

"In a school?"

"Apparently."

"What do you mean, apparently?"

"We went where we needed to go. That doesn't mean I know where that is. Sometimes where you want to go and where you need to go aren't the same place."

"That must be why I'm not at home, and I'm currently hiding in a freaking closet in an elementary school."

"How'd you know it was an elementary school?"

"It was a lucky guess," she snapped.

"You can come out of there. There's no one around. It's Saturday." He raised his arms and shrugged.

Elizabeth opened the door, looked slowly around, and stepped out of the closet. As she closed it behind her, a bell rang, loud and long. She tried the door. It had locked behind her. Children came pouring out of the classrooms in a tidal wave of waist-high backpacks.

Elizabeth stared at Pathwell, wide-eyed and angry.

Pathwell stopped one of them by the loop at the top of his backpack. "Small child. What is today?"

"Friday."

Friday. Somewhere back in the city, her nine o'clock meeting had come and gone while she sat in a field.

"You're unbelievable," Elizabeth said.

The pint-sized siege of chaos raged around them, but as all storms do, this one passed too. They had drawn the attention of a few teachers on their way by, but not enough to warrant an inquisition. So Pathwell started walking the halls, peeking through the glass in the classroom doors.

"Which room is he in?"

"I don't know."

"What does he teach?"

"I don't know."

"Does he even work here?"

Pathwell sighed. "You know, I'm going to run out of ways to say 'I don't know' if you keep asking the same question."

He turned back to the classrooms and strolled on down the hall. Elizabeth sighed and followed him, looking suspicious as she tried her best not to be noticed. It wasn't until the third hallway that Pathwell froze.

"What's wrong? Or don't you know that either?"

Pathwell cupped his hands around his eyes and pressed them against the glass of a darkened classroom. "I don't see anyone here," he said, disappointed. He straightened up. "And sarcasm will get you nowhere in this world."

"Can I help you?" a voice said behind them.

It was a stout man with a badge on a lanyard, the kind of person Pathwell seemed to make a point of avoiding.

Pathwell waved him off. "No, no, we're good here. Thank you."

The man paused and looked at them. "Could you please put on your IDs? They're required to be worn at all times in the building."

"Well, you see…" Pathwell patted his pockets and looked around him. "I don't know what happened to them. I could have sworn we had them."

The man moved closer. "Was there someone you were looking for?"

"No, not at this time."

"Then what are you doing here? Who are you?" He reached for his walkie-talkie. "Can someone get me eyes on two guests in the north wing?"

"Oh, there's no need for that." Pathwell raised a hand and began to reach into the back of his coat.

"There you are! Honey, you just wandered off. I told you I was just going to be a minute." Elizabeth walked up from behind Pathwell and looked at the man. "Oh my goodness, I'm sorry, I didn't mean to interrupt. I'm the missus." She stuck out her hand. "It's nice to meet you, Mister…?"

The man smiled politely, slightly confused. "You can just call me Ken. It's a pleasure to meet you. I'm sorry, I didn't get your name."

"Mrs. Pathwell. And this is my husband, Mr. Pathwell." She gestured at him with an elbow to the side. "Don't be rude, honey."

Pathwell jolted as the elbow landed a little more intentionally than he'd expected. He forced out a smile and leaned forward quickly with his hand out.

Ken shook both their hands, wary and confused. "Nice to meet you. Do you have a student here currently?"

The two of them paused, each waiting for the other to answer, when a voice behind them broke the silence.

"They're here to see me, Ken."

A tall man stepped around Elizabeth, a walkie-talkie still in one hand. His shoulders were wide and his hair was slicked jet black, and he was well dressed, in a button-down under a vest. "Sorry, I was running late, Pathwell. Hope I didn't keep you waiting long."

Pathwell locked eyes with him. "Stansbury." He forced a smile. "It's been too long."

Stansbury smiled back with a light chuckle. "Sorry about the confusion, Ken. I'll make sure they check out when they leave." Then, to the two of them: "Maroon car by the loading dock. Give me twenty minutes."


Pathwell was draped over the hood of an old maroon Cadillac, parked under the one tree in the lot, squinting up through its branches at the overcast sky.

"My god. Is this what waiting is like?"

Elizabeth looked up from scratching at the little space beside her fingernail. "It's been twenty minutes, Pathwell. You can't tell me you've never waited for anything before."

Pathwell lifted his chin and looked at her upside down. "If I have, it certainly wasn't this boring."

"So what's he like?"

"Who?"

She nodded toward the school. "Your brother."

"Oh. Right." Pathwell turned over on the hood and propped his head in his hands. "Well…" He paused in thought. "When you've been at it long enough, friends become enemies and enemies become friends, blah blah blah. You get the idea. Stansbury just likes to be a thorn in my side. And everyone's side, whenever he gets the chance." He sat up. "There was this time I was dealing with a particularly annoying Bakhtak—"

"Oh, that was your fault, and you know it. You even told me afterward." Stansbury dropped a cardboard box onto the hood. He looked at Elizabeth. "He lies, you know. Don't listen to him when it comes to old stories. He only partially remembers his own half-truths."

"I do not," Pathwell said.

"See?" Stansbury rolled his eyes. Then his eyes went to Pathwell's right hand, which hadn't quite stopped shaking since the Space Between, and stayed there.

"You pruned."

"Hello to you too."

Stansbury leaned back against the Cadillac. "Mama Baga sent word that something followed you into a city apartment before you started a working."

Pathwell slid off the hood. "That's the short version."

"It's the useful version. Tell me the order."

"It was already at the door before I used anything," Pathwell said. "Then the working broke, and once the thing got through the door, it went for the waste."

Elizabeth raised a hand. "I broke it. I grabbed the cookbook while he was using it."

"So after it arrived, it behaved normally."

"That's what I said."

"No. You said something came through a door and tried to kill you."

"That is a more compelling version."

"It's also a worse description."

Elizabeth looked between them. "Normally?"

Stansbury looked at her rather than at Pathwell. "Things like that clean up loose magical waste. Failed workings. Unsettled messes. If you don't stand between them and what they're after, they're unpleasant rather than murderous."

"That would have been useful information yesterday."

"Yes." Stansbury tapped one finger against the Cadillac's paint. "The part that doesn't fit is why it was already at your door."

"Her door," Pathwell said.

Stansbury looked at him. "Was it?"

Pathwell's expression flattened. Elizabeth almost asked whose door it was, if it wasn't hers. She didn't.

She poked at the box instead. Foam swords lay packed inside, bright handles and blunt gray blades, with smaller pieces underneath: daggers, short batons, two round shields with smiling dragons printed on them.

"What's with the swords?"

"Those are his weapons," Pathwell said, with an extra dismissive tone on weapons.

"That you need," Stansbury corrected. "Out of this week's stock."

"What are we going to do with a bunch of foam swords?" Elizabeth reached into the box, and Stansbury smacked the back of her hand. She snatched it back.

"We are going to do nothing. These aren't toys for you to play with."

Elizabeth rubbed the back of her hand, confused and annoyed. "They're foam."

"Stansbury is very sensitive about his swords, Lizzy. Be nice."

"We used to play with these as kids. What are they going to do for us now?"

"They're magic," Stansbury said. "They don't need to be pointy."

"Oh, come now, Stansbury, the girl didn't know your noodles are actually very dangerous," Pathwell said, poking at the box, and Stansbury snatched it back from him too.

He took one of the swords and stepped out from under the branches. The foam glowed a bright orange, interlocking plates of light wrapping it from the base up. He wound up and struck the trunk. The tree shook, its leaves wobbling, and every drop of the morning's rain came loose from its branches and poured down on Elizabeth and Pathwell. Stansbury stood there dry, with the look of a man who expected them to be impressed.

"What was that for?" Elizabeth said, while Pathwell laughed.

"These can do a lot more than you think." Stansbury pointed the sword at her. "You don't need to know any magic to use one. You hit the thing you mean to hit, and it does what it was made to do. They aren't indestructible. If one cracks, splits or starts heating when it shouldn't, stop using it."

He put the sword back and lifted one of the daggers, short enough to hide under a coat, gray blade, black handle, a loop at the pommel.

"This one cuts separation into things that don't separate easily. Adhesions. Clinging effects. Certain magical bindings."

"People?" Elizabeth asked.

"It's designed not to." He laid it back in the box.

"What do you do here, anyway? Do you teach?"

Stansbury laughed. "Oh, heavens no. I haven't actually taught a class in years."

"Then what do you do all day?"

"This and that," Pathwell said dismissively. "He steals children's energy."

Stansbury's lips pursed and his eyes narrowed. "That feels a bit reductive, even for you." He turned to Elizabeth. "I harness the latent magic energy created by the children's work and redirect it into items. Then I sell those items to interested buyers. I don't steal anything. The kids get their work back, the parents get to put it up on the fridge and then toss it in a box in the attic for a decade. It would never be used otherwise."

"That feels… harmless… I guess?" Elizabeth let the words mumble out, looking to Pathwell. "It doesn't really feel any different from what you did at the bookstore. Selling off potential nobody was going to use."

Pathwell slid the box across the hood toward his brother. "All right, Stansbury, you can stop showing off and put your noodle away."

"Always so crass, Pathwell." Stansbury collected his things, walked around to the driver's side and put the box in the back seat. "Well?" He gestured to the doors.

"Shotgun!" Pathwell yelled, hurrying past Elizabeth and knocking her into the hood at the waist.

"Pathwell!" She gathered herself, walked around to the back of the car, and slammed the door.


Her sleeves were still damp from the tree. They rode in silence through the small town, mostly because the radio only worked when it wanted to, and partly because that annoyed Pathwell.

Stansbury glanced once at Pathwell's right hand before he pulled away. "How thin?"

Pathwell looked out the window. "He said I didn't have much left to give."

"He who?"

"The shopkeeper."

Stansbury's jaw moved once. "And you bought what?"

"Books."

"Of course you did."

"Paper too."

"That fixes it."

You would be forgiven for not stopping in the town as you drove through, and even more forgiven if you didn't notice it while you did. A small crossroads store sat at the only stop sign and served as the town's general store and restaurant, with most of the rest of the town just off the main road. A small bar sat behind the store, sharing its parking lot. It had a pool table, a dartboard and its regulars.

Stansbury parked in the kicked-dust lot.

"What are we doing here?" Pathwell said, sticking just his head out of the window. "You brought us to a bar."

"I brought myself to a bar. You happen to be in my car. I just got off work, and I wouldn't mind a refreshment, Pathwell. Besides, I'm sure the poor girl could use a break after you've been dragging her around." Stansbury turned off the car and stepped out, looking back at Elizabeth in the back seat. "You're welcome to come in. Just bring one of those little foam daggers with you." He smiled.

Pathwell got out of the car through the open window and stretched his legs.

Elizabeth sat in the back, looking at the two of them, then at the poorly glowing neon sign in the bar's window, advertising a beer they no longer sold and she doubted anyone still made. It was late in the afternoon, and the parking lot was half full. It was hard to tell for which side, or whether the cars would just migrate from one to the other as the day went on.

The screen door slapped shut behind Stansbury.

Pathwell had stopped at the foot of the steps. He looked toward the bar, then past it, at nothing she could identify. Then the moment passed, and he came back to the car and noticed her hesitating.

"Hey. You're gonna be fine. Look, if you're worried, just bring one of Stansbury's little swords."

She heard Stansbury's voice in the back of her head. He lies, you know.

Pathwell looked at her, perplexed. "I don't lie."

That settled it. She took one of the foam daggers from the box beside her, the gray one Stansbury had held up at the school, and climbed out, cracking her head on the door frame. She felt around her clothes for somewhere to put it and settled on the back of her waistband, under her coat.

The air smelled of old beer and a sunbaked dumpster. The low sun scattered itself through the single tree that shaded parts of the lot.

The bar's thin screen door sat on a weathered deck, up a few short steps. Elizabeth looked around with one foot on the bottom step. Rows of corn across the road to her left, the scattered shelter of the town to her right. Pathwell was already up the steps, holding the screen door.

Elizabeth followed him inside.
