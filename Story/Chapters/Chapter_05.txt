Chapter Five

Pathwell was draped over the hood of an old maroon Cadillac like he was sunbathing, which was difficult to explain given that it was overcast.

He had his chin tilted toward the sky and his eyes half shut.

"My god," he said, without moving. "Is this what waiting is like?"

"It's been twenty minutes," Elizabeth said.

"Is it always like this?"

"Yes."

He turned his head upside-down to look at her.

"How do you stand it?"

Elizabeth sat on the low concrete wall beside the school loading dock and picked at the strip of skin beside one fingernail.

"Practice."

"Bleak."

"You could try it."

"I have centuries of evidence against patience as a lifestyle."

"That explains a lot."

Pathwell smiled without opening his eyes.

Elizabeth looked at the school.

It was the kind of building that managed to look temporary despite having been there for decades. Beige brick. Flat roof. Portable classrooms in the back that had acquired flower beds and therefore lost any plausible claim to portability. A hand-painted sign beside the loading dock reminded staff not to leave boxes in the fire lane.

The ordinariness of it made last night feel briefly more ridiculous.

Camp Cunnan had been behind a door in a forest that had been behind a bookstore inside a wall.

Pathwell's brother apparently worked at a school.

This, somehow, was where disbelief had drawn a line.

"What does he do here?" Elizabeth asked.

"Stansbury?"

"No, the other brother you haven't mentioned."

Pathwell opened one eye.

"You are becoming sarcastic."

"I have had a difficult night."

"Fair."

The loading-dock door opened.

A man came through backward carrying a cardboard box against his chest.

He was taller than Pathwell by just enough that Elizabeth immediately understood why the subject of which brother was older had annoyed him.

Stansbury set the box on the Cadillac's hood with a thud.

Pathwell flinched despite himself.

Stansbury noticed.

His eyes moved once to Pathwell's right hand.

The tremor from the Space Between had mostly faded, but not entirely.

"You pruned," Stansbury said.

Pathwell sat up.

"Hello to you too."

"How much?"

"Enough."

"That's not an amount."

"Then the answer is perfect."

Stansbury looked at Elizabeth.

"Don't listen to him. About anything. But particularly about measurements and old stories. He only partially remembers his own half-truths."

"I have an excellent memory," Pathwell said.

"You remember what's useful to you. That's different."

Elizabeth stood.

"Elizabeth."

"Stansbury."

He offered his hand.

She shook it.

His grip was ordinary.

She was beginning to appreciate ordinary things on principle.

Stansbury nodded toward Pathwell.

"Mama Baga said something followed you into a city apartment before you started a working."

Pathwell slid off the hood.

"That is the short version."

"It's the useful version."

Stansbury leaned back against the Cadillac.

"Tell me the order."

Pathwell's face changed slightly.

Elizabeth had seen that before too: the moment someone required him to stop performing competence and account for sequence.

"I was inside," Pathwell said. "It was already at the door."

"Before you used anything?"

"Yes."

"Before you broke anything?"

Elizabeth raised a hand.

"I broke it."

Both men looked at her.

"The working," she said. "I grabbed the cookbook while he was using it."

Stansbury nodded once.

"Then?"

"The page stayed active," Pathwell said. "The interruption left waste. Once the thing got through the door, it turned toward that."

"Immediately?"

"Immediately."

Stansbury looked down at the Cadillac's hood.

He tapped one finger against the paint.

"So after it arrived, it behaved normally."

"That's what I said."

"No. You said something came through a door and tried to kill you."

"That is a more compelling version."

"It is also a worse description."

Elizabeth looked between them.

"Normally?"

Stansbury looked at her rather than Pathwell.

"Things like that clean up loose magical waste. Failed workings. Unsettled messes. Usually if you don't stand between them and what they're after, they're unpleasant rather than murderous."

"That would have been useful information yesterday."

"Yes."

Pathwell opened his mouth.

Stansbury held up one finger without looking at him.

Pathwell closed it.

Elizabeth liked Stansbury slightly more.

"The part that doesn't fit," Stansbury continued, "is why it was already at your door."

"Her door," Pathwell said.

Stansbury looked at him.

"Was it?"

Pathwell's expression flattened.

Elizabeth felt the question settle between them.

Not an accusation.

Not an answer either.

"That's why I'm here," Pathwell said.

"Eventually."

"I had a delivery."

"You invented a delivery."

"They needed the paper."

"Those statements can both be true."

Pathwell looked offended by the concept.

Stansbury pushed away from the car.

"I want to see what happens next before I decide what I think happened first."

"Excellent," Pathwell said. "Road trip."

"I didn't say you were driving."

"Then less excellent."

Elizabeth looked at the cardboard box.

"What's in that?"

Pathwell glanced at it.

"Weapons," he said, in the tone of a man embarrassed that the word had been applied to foam.

"Working inventory," Stansbury corrected.

He opened the flaps.

Foam swords lay packed lengthwise inside. Bright handles. Blunt gray blades. The kind sold in toy aisles beside inflatable hammers and plastic shields.

There were smaller pieces underneath—daggers, short batons, two round shields with smiling dragons printed on them.

"Stock," Stansbury said. "I make them in batches. People who know what they're buying buy them."

Pathwell looked at the smiling dragons.

"Some with less dignity than others."

"Dignity is not a safety feature."

Elizabeth reached toward a sword.

Stansbury put two fingers on the box lid.

Not stopping her.

Waiting.

"They look like toys," Elizabeth said.

"They're supposed to."

"Why?"

"Because people are considerably less nervous carrying a foam sword than a real one into a hotel lobby."

"That is disturbingly practical."

"Most useful things are."

Pathwell leaned over the box.

"Stansbury has built an entire professional philosophy around not being arrested."

"It has served me well."

Stansbury lifted one of the swords.

"These are finished tools. Already worked. You don't need to know magic to use one. You hit the thing you mean to hit."

Elizabeth looked at the foam blade.

"And then?"

"Then the tool does what it was built to do."

"Which is?"

"Depends on the tool."

Pathwell pointed at the sword in Stansbury's hand.

"That one humiliates trees."

"It disperses surface water and certain clinging effects."

"Humiliates trees faster."

Stansbury walked to the nearest maple.

He checked once behind it to make sure no one was standing there.

Then he swung.

The foam connected with the trunk.

The tree shuddered.

Every wet leaf released at once.

A brief localized rainfall dropped directly onto Pathwell.

Elizabeth was standing far enough away this time to avoid most of it.

Stansbury remained completely dry.

Pathwell stared at his coat.

"You aimed that."

"Of course I did."

"Petty."

"Precise."

Elizabeth laughed.

Pathwell pointed at her.

"You are encouraging him."

"I think he was like this before me."

"Tragically."

Stansbury returned the sword to the box.

"They aren't indestructible. They need maintenance. Some effects wear down faster than others. If one cracks, splits, or starts heating when it shouldn't, stop using it."

Elizabeth nodded.

Stansbury reached under the swords and lifted one of the foam daggers.

It was short enough to hide under a coat, gray blade, black handle, a loop at the pommel.

He held it out toward Elizabeth.

"Do you want one?"

She looked at it.

"Do I need one?"

"I don't know yet."

That was the first answer anyone had given her all night that improved because it admitted uncertainty.

Elizabeth took the dagger.

It weighed almost nothing.

"What does this one do?"

"Cuts separation into things that don't separate easily. Adhesions. Clinging effects. Certain magical bindings."

"People?"

"It's designed not to."

"Designed?"

"Safety is something you build, not something you assume."

Elizabeth turned the dagger over.

There was no glowing symbol. No ancient writing. Just foam with a seam down one side where it had come out of a mold.

"And I can use it?"

"If you can stab something with a foam dagger, yes."

"I have that skill."

"Most people do."

Pathwell opened the Cadillac's passenger door.

"Wonderful. Everybody armed? Shotgun."

He moved faster than seemed fair and folded himself into the front seat.

Elizabeth remained beside the open rear door.

Stansbury watched Pathwell settle in.

"He does that," he said.

"I've noticed."

"You can make him move."

From the front seat Pathwell said, "No she cannot."

Elizabeth looked at Stansbury.

Stansbury looked at her.

She considered it.

Then got into the back.

"Not worth it."

"Wise," Stansbury said.

"Cowardice," Pathwell said.

"Resource allocation," Elizabeth said.

Stansbury shut her door.

Pathwell turned around in the seat.

"I liked you better before the sarcasm."

"You didn't know me before the sarcasm."

"Exactly."


The back seat smelled like old cars—engine oil, vinyl, and something floral from an air freshener that had stopped trying several years ago.

Elizabeth put her bag beside her and looped the dagger through the outside strap where she could reach it.

Through the window, the school's loading dock was already empty.

A cart rattled somewhere inside the building.

Someone laughed behind a closed door.

Stansbury put both hands on the wheel at ten and two.

Pathwell noticed.

"You still drive like an instructional pamphlet."

"And you're still alive to complain about it."

The Cadillac started on the second turn.

Stansbury glanced at Pathwell's right hand again before pulling away.

"How thin?"

Pathwell looked out the window.

"He said thin."

"He who?"

"The Shopkeeper."

Stansbury's jaw moved once.

"And you bought what?"

"Books."

"Of course you did."

"Paper too."

"That fixes it."

Elizabeth watched Pathwell's reflection in the side window.

He smiled.

It did not reach the rest of his face.

Stansbury did not press.

That was almost as interesting as if he had.

They drove through a town built to be passed through.

A crossroads store. A stop sign. A laundromat with one fluorescent tube flickering behind the glass. In one upstairs window, the blue light of a television.

Then fields.

The radio worked intermittently, which annoyed Pathwell more than silence would have.

Stansbury turned it off.

"That's worse," Pathwell said.

"For you."

Outside town the road opened and flattened.

The sky over the fields was the color of a coin someone had been carrying a long time. The distance between things was easy to measure here. Fence. Tree. Barn. Road.

Elizabeth found that, against expectation, slightly steadying.

A place where you could see what was coming from a long way off.

"So where are we going?" she asked.

"I'm going for a drink," Stansbury said.

Pathwell turned toward him.

"You're what?"

"I just finished work."

"We came to you because something impossible reached a city apartment before it had any reason to be there."

Stansbury glanced at him.

"And it will remain impossible for the length of one drink."

Elizabeth looked out the window so they would not see her smile.

At the edge of a field, sharing a dirt lot with the crossroads store, sat a bar.

It had a pool table visible through one window, a beer sign for a brand that might no longer exist, and three pickup trucks parked at angles that suggested parking had been treated as a personal interpretation.

Stansbury pulled in.

He turned off the engine.

"One drink," he said.

Pathwell looked at the building with deep suspicion.

"You brought us to a bar."

"I brought myself to a bar. You happen to be in my car."

Stansbury got out.

Pathwell opened his door, then paused.

Something in his posture changed.

Small enough Elizabeth might have missed it if Mama Baga had not made her notice the stillness before.

He looked toward the bar.

Then past it.

Then at nothing she could identify.

The moment passed.

He got out.

Stansbury leaned down beside Elizabeth's open window.

"You're welcome to come in. You're also welcome to stay here."

Elizabeth touched the foam dagger at her bag.

"Which do you recommend?"

"Whichever one you choose."

Pathwell looked over the roof of the car.

"Stay close."

Stansbury looked at him.

Pathwell added, "Recommendation."

Elizabeth opened the door.

"I'm coming in."

She clipped the dagger loop inside the back of her waistband.

The foam pressed lightly against her coat.

The screen door slapped shut behind Stansbury.

Pathwell was still beside the Cadillac, looking at the bar with the focused attention of someone deciding whether a feeling belonged to the present or had followed him there from somewhere else.

Elizabeth came around the car.

"You okay?"

Pathwell looked at her.

"Fine."

She waited.

"That is apparently a posture," he added.

Elizabeth almost smiled.

Then he pushed off the car and headed for the steps.

The early evening sun came through the single overhanging tree in pieces. Through the screen door she could hear low voices, the knock of a cue ball, a laugh from somewhere in back.

The bar had been there long enough to stop trying.

Elizabeth followed the brothers inside.
