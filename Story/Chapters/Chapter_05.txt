Chapter Five

Pathwell was draped over the hood of an old maroon Cadillac like he was sunbathing, which was difficult to explain given that it was overcast. He had his chin tilted up at the sky and his eyes half shut.

"My god," he said, without moving, "is this what waiting is like?"

"It's been twenty minutes," Elizabeth said.

"Is it always like this?"

"Yes."

He turned his head upside-down to look at her. "How do you stand it?"

Elizabeth was picking at the edge of her fingernail, that small strip of skin next to the nail where things catch. She didn't answer.


The school had a loading dock along the side, and it was from there that Stansbury eventually appeared, dropping a cardboard box onto the hood of a car with a thud that made Pathwell flinch despite himself.

"Don't listen to him," Stansbury said to Elizabeth, by way of greeting. "About anything. But particularly about old stories. He only partially remembers his own half-truths."

"I have an excellent memory," Pathwell said, sitting up.

"You remember what's useful to you. That's different."

"What's in the box?" Elizabeth asked.

"Weapons," Pathwell said, with a tone that suggested he found the word embarrassing applied to whatever was in the box.

"That you need," Stansbury said.

Elizabeth looked in the box. Foam swords. A dozen of them, packed in lengthwise, the kind sold in toy aisles next to the inflatable hammers. She reached in.

Stansbury's hand came down on hers. Not hard. Just — final. "These are not toys."

"They look like toys."

"They're not."

Pathwell was already sliding off the hood. "Stansbury is very sensitive about his swords, Lizzy. It's best not to engage."

"I'm not sensitive," Stansbury said. "I'm precise." He lifted one of the swords from the box and walked to the nearest tree, said something under his breath, and swung. The foam connected with the trunk and the tree shook — not like something struck it, but like something had passed through it, all the leaves releasing their water at once, a brief localized rainfall that soaked Pathwell and Elizabeth both and left Stansbury standing dry with a look that expected applause.

"What was that for?" Elizabeth said.

"Demonstration."

"Of what?"

"That they work."

Pathwell was wringing out his sleeve. "Alright, Stansbury, you've made your point, put the noodle away."

"Always so crass." Stansbury placed the sword carefully back in the box, collected it, and walked around to the driver's side. "Well?" He gestured at the car doors.

"Shotgun," Pathwell said, and moved faster than seemed fair, shouldering past Elizabeth and folding himself into the front seat.

She stood in the kicked-up dust and looked at the back door.

"He does that," Stansbury said from inside.


The back seat had the smell of old cars — engine and something floral, an air freshener long past its prime. Her bag went on the seat beside her. Through the window the school's loading dock was already empty, the scuff of Stansbury's box on a car hood already just a sound that had happened.

She pressed her hand flat on the worn vinyl.

Pathwell's arm had shaken in the shop. She hadn't asked about it. He hadn't said. The cat had licked its paw and the shopkeeper had gone back to his ledger and that was what she'd been given, which was apparently the way information arrived here — sideways, incomplete, in whatever quantity the person delivering it had decided was enough.

She lifted her hand off the seat.

Stansbury drove with both hands at ten and two. The grip precise and unchanged, the way her father had explained it once in a parking lot when she was fifteen and she had let it go the minute they got back on the road. She looked at her own hands in her lap.

Fine.

They rode in silence through a town built to be passed through. A crossroads store. A stop sign. In one window, the blue flicker of a television. Then the road.

The radio worked intermittently, which annoyed Pathwell more than silence would have, so Stansbury turned it off.

Outside of town the road opened into fields, and the sky over them was the color of a coin someone has been carrying a long time. The fields were flat and the distance between things was easy to measure and exact, which she found, against all expectation, slightly steadying. Something about the scale. A place where you could see what was coming from a long way off.

At the edge of a field, sharing a parking lot with the crossroads store, sat a bar. It had a pool table and a dartboard and its regulars, and a neon sign in the window advertising a beer they probably didn't make anymore. The parking lot was kicked-down dirt, half-full.

Stansbury pulled in and turned off the car. "I just got off work," he said, preemptively. "I wouldn't mind a refreshment."

"You brought us to a bar," Pathwell said.

"I brought myself to a bar. You happen to be in my car."

He got out. Pathwell unfolded himself from the front seat through the open window, which Elizabeth was choosing not to comment on. She sat in the back.

Stansbury leaned down to her window. "You're welcome to come in," he said. "Grab one of those little foam daggers if it makes you feel better."

The screen door slapped shut behind him.


Pathwell was still standing by the car, looking at the bar with the focused attention of someone deciding whether they believe in something. He looked back at her. "You're going to be fine."

"You keep saying that."

"Because it keeps being true." He pushed off the car. "Stay loose. Stay close. And yes, bring the dagger."

She reached through the open window and pulled a foam dagger from the box. It was lighter than it looked. She wasn't sure if that helped. She turned it over in her hands, found a loop in the handle, and tucked it into the back of her waistband.

Pathwell was already at the steps.

The early evening sun came through the single overhanging tree in pieces. Through the screen door she could hear the bar — low voices, the knock of a cue ball, a laugh from somewhere at the back.

She stood outside. The sky above the bar had gone the color of whatever comes before dark. The in-between.

The screen door was heavier than it looked. Behind it was a wood door, and behind that, the dim interior of a bar that had been there long enough to stop trying.

It snapped shut behind her.
