Chapter Three

She found Pathwell staring into a streetlight.

Again.

He stood beneath it with Nana's cookbook tucked under one arm and her diary under the other, looking up into the yellow glow as though the bulb had said something interesting and was refusing to repeat itself.

Elizabeth stopped three feet away.

"Give them back."

Pathwell looked down.

"Hello to you too."

"My books."

"Yes. Those."

"Give them back."

He shifted both under one arm and checked the street behind her.

"In a minute."

"You said soon."

"This is soon."

"No. This is later."

He considered that.

"Fair."

She held out her hand.

Pathwell looked at it, then at her.

"I need to stop at a bookstore."

"I do not care."

"You may once you see it."

"That is not how ownership works."

"No," he said. "Apparently tonight is very educational for me."

He started walking.

Elizabeth stayed where she was.

He made it six steps before noticing.

"Lizzy."

"Elizabeth."

"Elizabeth. We really do need to move."

"Then move."

Pathwell looked at the books under his arm.

She looked at the books under his arm.

He sighed.

"You are making a compelling procedural argument."

"I am going to start screaming."

"Less compelling. More immediate."

He glanced toward the end of the block. Nothing moved there except traffic.

Then, quieter: "I need both hands where we're going. You'll get them back at the counter."

"Why the counter?"

"Because 'because I said so' was received poorly."

"Correct."

"Also, if something follows us again, I would prefer not to explain to a bookseller why your grandmother's cookbook dissolved in an alley."

Elizabeth folded her arms.

"That almost sounded reasonable."

"I have moments."

"I still don't trust you."

"Also reasonable."

He waited.

Not patiently. His fingers tapped once against the cookbook and stopped.

Elizabeth pointed at it.

"If you damage another page—"

"I will endeavor to damage someone else's history first."

"That is a terrible answer."

"It's been a difficult night."

He turned down an alley.

Elizabeth followed him.

He still had her books.

She still wanted answers.

Neither fact improved her opinion of him.


The alley ended at a concrete wall.

No door. No windows. Just an old loading bay bricked over years ago, a rusted pipe, and three milk crates stacked beside a dumpster.

Pathwell set the books on the driest crate.

Elizabeth stepped between him and them.

"What are you doing?"

"Bookstore."

"That is a wall."

"Only from this side."

He pulled a folded piece of paper from inside his coat and pressed it flat against the concrete.

Nothing happened.

Pathwell frowned.

He lifted the paper, rotated it ninety degrees, and tried again.

Light leaked from the creases.

"There we are."

"You put it on sideways?"

"Orientation matters."

"You didn't know which way was up."

"Those are different statements."

The light stretched past the edges of the paper until it outlined a door where there had not been one. Steam hissed from the seam. A brass handle pushed itself out of the concrete with the embarrassed reluctance of something being asked to work after hours.

Pathwell picked up the books and opened it.

Rows of shelves filled the other side.

Old paper breathed into the alley.

Leather. Dust. Binding glue. Ink. Something bitter underneath it all.

Pathwell stepped through.

Elizabeth stayed outside.

He turned back.

"You wanted the counter."

She looked toward the mouth of the alley.

A car passed. Someone laughed. A bottle broke somewhere and nobody screamed afterward.

Her meeting was still getting closer. Her apartment was still missing a door. Her laundry ticket was still in a sewer.

Her diary and cookbook were ten feet away in a bookstore that did not fit behind a wall.

Elizabeth stepped through.

The door clicked shut behind her.

The city disappeared so completely she could hear the shelves settling under their own weight.

She turned.

The doorway had become shelving.

"Pathwell."

A shush came from somewhere deep in the shop.

Elizabeth looked toward it.

"Was that you?"

Another shush.

"I'm going to assume no."

The place was cramped and had apparently decided this was an aesthetic rather than a problem.

Books were only part of it. Journals with split spines. Bundles of letters tied in ribbon. Recipe cards spotted with oil. Children's drawings under glass. Envelopes held together by a rubber band that had fossilized around them. Notebooks swollen from rain and dried crooked. Loose pages filed in wooden boxes with labels that looked older than the boxes.

Elizabeth could not see any system.

The certainty that there was one made it worse.

She walked between two shelves.

Something brushed across her that was not a hand.

A dog asleep beneath a table. Someone laughing while trying not to. The hollow grief of a father she had never buried.

Elizabeth stopped.

The nearest journal sat quietly on its shelf.

Three steps later came anticipation, bright and nervous. Then embarrassment. Then a love so old it had worn smooth around the edges.

She gripped the end of a bookcase.

"Pathwell?"

No answer.

Her apartment came back. Nana. The funeral. The boxes. Tomorrow's meeting. Everyone else apparently having received an orientation packet for adulthood while hers had been lost in the mail.

The aisle seemed to narrow.

Elizabeth sat down before her knees made the decision for her.

Dust and old wool pressed into her palm.

Solid enough.

When she looked up, a thin orange cat was sitting directly in front of her.

It blinked.

Elizabeth blinked back.

"You seem underqualified."

The cat climbed halfway into her lap.

"Right. Of course."

A plain white mug appeared in front of her face.

Pathwell was crouched on the other side of the cat.

"Drink."

Elizabeth took the mug.

"What's in it?"

"Coffee."

"Why did you hesitate?"

"I didn't."

She smelled it.

It smelled like coffee that had been left somewhere warm and punitive.

She drank anyway.

It was awful. Dollar-store beans through a reused filter, cooked long enough to develop a personal grievance.

It tasted like two in the morning and highlighters and a paper due in six hours.

Elizabeth had made this coffee herself a hundred times.

She took another drink.

"That is terrible."

"I know."

"Why does that help?"

"Familiar things are useful."

"You could have given me water."

"Then you'd just be overwhelmed and hydrated."

The cat pushed its head under her hand.

She scratched behind one ear.

A claw caught the base of her thumb.

"Ow."

The cat studied the bead of blood with satisfaction.

"He likes documentation," a voice said.

Behind a broad oaken counter sat a round man in round-rimmed glasses, making ticks in a ledger with the concentration of someone balancing the universe to the nearest cent.

A small brass plate read:

THE SPACE BETWEEN

The cat licked one paw.

"I'd let him have his victory," the man said. "He becomes vindictive when challenged."

Pathwell handed Elizabeth a paper napkin.

She stared at it.

"You blasted apart whatever was in my apartment and your solution to this is a napkin?"

"It's a scratch."

"I am trying to understand your standards."

"Don't. It'll ruin them."

Elizabeth wrapped the napkin around her thumb.

Pathwell stood.

"Come on."

"My books."

He pointed toward the counter.

The cookbook and diary were there.

Elizabeth crossed the shop.

The shopkeeper put one finger on the cookbook.

She stopped.

"His transaction first."

Elizabeth looked at Pathwell.

"You said I'd get them back at the counter."

"And look," Pathwell said. "A counter."

"That is not the same thing."

"It's alarmingly close."

The shopkeeper looked from one of them to the other.

"Are these part of your account?" he asked Pathwell.

"No."

"Do they belong to you?"

"No."

"Good."

He moved his finger.

Elizabeth reached again.

"After settlement."

Her hand stopped.

"Why?"

"Because he brought unsecured outside property into an active transaction area, and I dislike paperwork."

Pathwell nodded. "He's very strict."

"You are the reason I am strict."

"Historically overstated."

The shopkeeper put on his glasses.

Pathwell's smile changed.

Only a little.

The shopkeeper opened the ledger to a page already carrying Pathwell's name.

There were more entries beneath it than Elizabeth could count. Some had dates. Some did not. One had NEVER AGAIN written in the margin.

Pathwell leaned over.

"That seems editorial."

"It was written during the horse incident."

"Which one?"

The shopkeeper stared at him.

Pathwell nodded. "Fair."

"Your outstanding balance."

Pathwell looked at the number.

"Reasonable."

"It has been reasonable for eighty-three years."

"I was letting it mature."

"Debt is not cheese."

"Some cheeses would disagree."

The shopkeeper ignored him and pointed toward the far end of the counter.

A stack of books waited there.

No. Several stacks.

Hardcovers. Journals. Blank archival paper. Two wrapped bundles. A wooden crate tied with twine. Enough weight to make the counter complain beneath it.

Pathwell brightened.

"Excellent."

The shopkeeper looked over his glasses.

"Did Camp Cunnan ask you for all of this?"

Pathwell became interested in the crate.

"They need it."

"That was not what I asked."

"No."

The shopkeeper let the answer sit.

"The order and the old balance together, then."

Pathwell reached into his coat and set an old coin on the counter.

The shopkeeper did not look at it.

A second coin.

Nothing.

A folded note.

The shopkeeper's eyes moved to it and away.

"No."

"You haven't even read it."

"I have met you before."

Pathwell put the note away.

He looked at the books.

At the ledger.

"Future."

Elizabeth looked at him.

The shopkeeper took off his glasses.

"Yours?"

"I have become very particular about that distinction."

"Yours?"

"Yes."

The irritation left the shopkeeper's face.

"You're thin."

Pathwell smiled.

"Still standing."

"Those are not the same condition."

"They overlap enough for commerce."

The shopkeeper looked at him for another second.

"The price is the price."

Pathwell's smile disappeared.

Elizabeth looked between them.

"What does that mean?"

Neither answered her.

Pathwell put his hand flat on the ledger.

For a second, nothing happened.

Then pale lines opened from his shadow.

They spread over the floor and split beneath the shelves, each division becoming two, then four, then too many to follow. They were not pictures of anything. Looking at them gave Elizabeth only the dizzy certainty that every line could keep becoming another line if nobody stopped it.

Pathwell inhaled.

The shopkeeper put two fingers on the ledger.

Branches folded inward.

Went dark.

A pipe ticked in the wall.

The cat cleaned one foot.

More branches disappeared.

Where there had been a crowded spill of pale divisions around Pathwell, empty carpet showed through.

His fingers tightened against the ledger.

The last light vanished.

His knees hit the floor.

He caught the counter with one hand.

The shopkeeper put his glasses back on.

"Settled."

Pathwell stayed there, breathing through his nose. His right arm had begun to shake. He tucked it close to his side as though posture had become the problem.

The shopkeeper made three entries in the ledger and closed it.

"Thank you, Mr. Pathwell."

Pathwell looked up.

"Don't sound so pleased."

"I have been waiting eighty-three years to write that zero."

"Vindictive."

"Accurate."

Pathwell pulled himself upright.

The shopkeeper slid Nana's cookbook and Elizabeth's diary across the counter.

"These were never his to spend."

Elizabeth took them.

The diary was cool under her hand.

The cookbook felt heavier, which was stupid. Missing a page should have made it lighter.

Her thumb found the ragged place in the binding.

Pathwell saw and looked away.

"You paid with your future," she said.

He flexed the shaking hand once.

"Part of it."

"What part?"

"If I knew that precisely, I would make worse decisions with greater confidence."

"That is supposed to reassure me?"

"No. I think we're past that stage of our relationship."

Elizabeth looked at the stacks waiting on the other end of the counter.

"Who are those for?"

"Camp."

"Camp what?"

"Cunnan."

"That's not an answer either."

"No. But it is a noun. Progress."

The shopkeeper pulled a handcart out from behind the counter.

It unfolded twice, extended once, and stopped at a size that should not have fit there in the first place.

Pathwell stared at it.

"You had that the entire time?"

"Yes."

"And you've watched me carry things out of here by hand."

"Yes."

"For decades."

The shopkeeper put one wrapped bundle on the cart.

"Your account is settled. I feel no need to lie now."

Pathwell looked genuinely wounded.

Elizabeth laughed.

One short, surprised sound.

Both men looked at her.

"Don't make anything of that."

Pathwell smiled.

"Wouldn't dream of it."

"I know what you do when people say that now."

"You're learning."

They loaded the cart.

Elizabeth held her books against her chest while Pathwell moved the Camp order. He tried the crate one-handed. His arm shook hard enough that he put it back down.

He waited.

Then used both hands.

When everything was stacked, the shopkeeper walked to a narrow door beside a shelf of ledgers.

Elizabeth was certain it had not been there before.

He opened it.

Cold pine air entered the shop.

Beyond the threshold waited trees and a dirt path silvered by a moon the city had hidden.

Pathwell took the cart handle.

"Right."

Elizabeth looked behind her.

There was no city door.

The shopkeeper followed her glance.

"If you want the sidewalk, I can return you to the sidewalk."

Pathwell stopped with his back to her.

Elizabeth looked down at the diary and cookbook in her arms.

Hers again.

Her apartment still existed. Hotels existed. Police existed, although explaining the front door remained an ambitious project. Her meeting was still out there getting closer.

Nobody in the shop was stopping her.

Pathwell's fingers tapped the cart handle once.

Elizabeth looked through the open door.

A forest. A Camp Cunnan. Several stacks of books somebody apparently needed badly enough for Pathwell to leave part of his future on a ledger without being asked.

She could go home.

Instead she stepped toward the cart and put Nana's cookbook on top of the Camp books.

Then the diary beside it.

"Don't lose those."

Pathwell looked at the books, then at her.

"Wouldn't dream of it."

"Again. I know what that means from you now."

"Fair."

Elizabeth put both hands on the side of the cart.

"Where are we taking these?"

Pathwell's smile returned slowly.

"Camp Cunnan."

"If you say that like it explains anything one more time, I'm going back to the sidewalk."

"Understood."

He pulled the cart through the doorway.

Elizabeth went with him.

The shopkeeper closed the door behind them.
