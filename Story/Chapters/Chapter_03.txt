Chapter Three

She found Pathwell staring into a streetlight.

Again.

He stood beneath it with Nana's cookbook tucked under one arm and her diary under the other, looking up into the yellow glow as though the bulb had said something interesting and was refusing to repeat itself.

Elizabeth stopped three feet away.

"Give them back."

Pathwell looked down.

"Hello to you too."

"My books."

"Yes. Those."

"Give them back."

He shifted both under one arm and checked the street behind her.

"In a minute."

"You said soon."

"This is soon."

"No. This is later."

He considered that.

"Fair."

She held out her hand.

Pathwell looked at it, then at her.

"I need to stop at a bookstore."

"I do not care."

"You may once you see it."

"That is not how ownership works."

"No," he said. "Apparently tonight is very educational for me."

He started walking.

Elizabeth stayed where she was.

He made it six steps before noticing the absence beside him.

"Lizzy."

"Elizabeth."

"Elizabeth. We really do need to move."

"Then move."

Pathwell looked at the books under his arm.

She looked at the books under his arm.

He sighed.

"You are making a compelling procedural argument."

"I am going to start screaming."

"Less compelling. More immediate."

He glanced toward the end of the block. Nothing moved there except traffic.

Then, quieter: "I need both hands where we're going. You'll get them back at the counter."

"Why the counter?"

"Because I said so was received poorly."

"Correct."

"And because if something follows us again, I would prefer not to spend the next hour explaining to a bookseller why your grandmother's cookbook was dissolved in an alley."

Elizabeth folded her arms.

"That almost sounded reasonable."

"I have moments."

"I still don't trust you."

"That also seems reasonable."

He waited.

This was different from the dumpling stand. Barely, but enough to be annoying.

Elizabeth pointed at the cookbook.

"If you damage another page—"

"I will endeavor to damage someone else's history first."

"That is a terrible answer."

"It's been a difficult night."

He turned down an alley.

Elizabeth followed because he still had her things.

And because she wanted answers.

Both reasons irritated her.


The alley ended at a concrete wall.

No door. No windows. Just an old loading bay bricked over years ago, a rusted pipe, and three milk crates stacked beside a dumpster.

Pathwell set the books carefully on the driest crate.

Elizabeth stepped between him and them.

"What are you doing?"

"Bookstore."

"That is a wall."

"Only from this side."

He pulled a folded piece of paper from inside his coat and pressed it flat against the concrete.

Nothing happened.

Pathwell frowned.

He lifted the paper, rotated it ninety degrees, and pressed it back down.

Light leaked from the creases.

"There we are."

"You put it on sideways?"

"Orientation matters."

"You didn't know which way was up."

"Those are different statements."

The light stretched beyond the edges of the paper until it outlined a door where there had not been one. Steam hissed softly from the seam. A brass handle pushed itself out of the concrete with the embarrassed reluctance of something being asked to work after hours.

Pathwell picked up the books.

He opened the door.

Rows of shelves filled the other side.

Old paper breathed out into the alley.

Leather. Dust. Binding glue. Ink. Something herbal and bitter underneath it all.

Pathwell stepped through.

Elizabeth stayed outside.

He turned back.

"You wanted the counter."

She looked behind her.

The city was still there. A car went past at the alley mouth. Somewhere somebody laughed. Somewhere else a bottle broke and no one screamed afterward.

Ordinary sounds.

Her meeting was still getting closer.

Her apartment was still missing a door.

Her laundry ticket was still in a sewer.

Her diary and cookbook were now ten feet away in a bookstore that did not fit behind the wall it occupied.

Elizabeth stepped through.

The street disappeared.

Not faded. Gone.

The door shut behind her with a click, and the city cut off so completely she could hear the shelves settling under their own weight.

Then the door itself thinned into a line of light and vanished.

Elizabeth turned around.

There was shelving where the doorway had been.

"Pathwell."

A shush came from somewhere deep inside the shop.

She looked toward the sound.

"Was that you?"

Another shush.

"I'm going to assume no."

The shop was cramped and had apparently decided this was an aesthetic rather than a problem.

Books were only part of it. Journals with split spines. Bundles of letters tied in ribbon. Recipe cards spotted with oil. Children's drawings under glass. A stack of envelopes held together by a rubber band that had fossilized around them. Notebooks swollen from rain and dried crooked. Margins thick with pencil. Loose pages filed in wooden boxes with labels that looked older than the boxes.

Nothing appeared organized in a way Elizabeth understood.

Everything appeared organized in a way someone understood extremely well.

That was somehow worse.

She walked between two shelves.

The air changed.

Not temperature. Pressure.

The shelf on her left felt warm against her sleeve without touching it. A flash hit her—not an image exactly, more like the memory of an image. A dog that had never been hers asleep beneath a table. Someone laughing while trying not to. The sharp hollow grief of a father she had never buried.

Elizabeth stopped.

The feeling passed.

She looked at the nearest journal.

Nothing about it moved.

She did not touch it.

Three steps later another shelf pressed something against her chest: anticipation this time, nervous and bright, then embarrassment, then a love so old it had worn itself smooth around the edges.

Elizabeth gripped the end of a bookcase.

"Pathwell?"

No answer.

Her own feelings arrived behind the borrowed ones.

That was the problem.

The shop did not replace them. It gave them company.

Her apartment. Nana. The funeral. The boxes. The meeting. The particular exhaustion of having no idea what she was doing with her life while everyone else kept behaving as though adulthood came with an orientation packet she had misplaced.

The aisle narrowed.

No. It didn't.

Her breathing did.

Elizabeth sat down before her knees made the decision without her.

The carpet smelled like dust and old wool.

She pressed her palm against it.

Solid.

Ordinary.

For a while she concentrated on that.

When she looked up, a thin orange cat was sitting directly in front of her.

It blinked.

Elizabeth blinked back.

"You seem underqualified."

The cat stood, walked forward, and climbed halfway into her lap without asking.

"Right. Of course."

A plain white mug appeared in front of her face.

Pathwell was crouched on the other side of the cat.

"Drink."

Elizabeth took the mug.

"What's in it?"

"Coffee."

"Why did you hesitate?"

"I didn't."

She smelled it.

It smelled like coffee that had been left somewhere warm and punitive.

She drank anyway.

It was awful.

Not interesting-awful. Not charming-awful. Dollar-store beans through a reused filter, cooked long enough to develop a personal grievance.

It tasted like two in the morning and highlighters and a paper due in six hours.

Elizabeth had made this exact coffee herself a hundred times.

The familiarity cut through the pressure in her chest.

She took another drink.

"That is terrible."

"I know."

"Why does that help?"

"Familiar things are useful."

"You could have given me water."

"Then you'd just be overwhelmed and hydrated."

The cat pressed its head under her hand.

She scratched behind one ear.

A claw caught the base of her thumb.

"Ow."

The cat looked at the bead of blood with satisfaction.

"He likes documentation," a voice said.

Elizabeth looked up.

Behind a broad oaken counter sat a round man in round-rimmed glasses, making ticks in a ledger and carrying ones with the solemn concentration of a person balancing the universe to the nearest cent.

A small brass plate on the counter read:

THE SPACE BETWEEN

The cat licked one paw.

"I'd let him have his victory," the man said. "He becomes vindictive when challenged."

Pathwell handed Elizabeth a paper napkin.

"You have magic that can blast apart whatever that thing was in my apartment, and your solution to this is a napkin?"

"It's a scratch."

"I am trying to understand your standards."

"Don't. It'll ruin them."

Elizabeth wrapped the napkin around her thumb.

Pathwell stood.

"Come on."

"My books."

He pointed toward the counter.

Nana's cookbook and her diary were sitting there.

Not hidden. Not opened. Not being used.

Just waiting.

Elizabeth moved toward them.

The shopkeeper put one finger on the cookbook.

She stopped.

"His transaction first," he said.

Elizabeth looked at Pathwell.

"You said I'd get them back at the counter."

"And look," Pathwell said. "A counter."

"That is not the same thing."

"It's alarmingly close."

The shopkeeper looked from one of them to the other.

"Are these part of your account?" he asked Pathwell.

"No."

"Do they belong to you?"

"No."

"Good."

The shopkeeper removed his finger from the cookbook.

Elizabeth reached for both books.

"After settlement," he said.

Her hand stopped again.

"Why?"

"Because he brought unsecured outside property into an active transaction area, and I dislike paperwork."

Pathwell nodded. "He's very strict."

"You are the reason I am strict."

"Historically overstated."

The shopkeeper put on his glasses.

Pathwell's smile changed by half a degree.

Elizabeth noticed.

The shopkeeper opened the ledger to a page already containing Pathwell's name.

There were more entries beneath it than Elizabeth could count at a glance.

Some had dates.

Some did not.

One had a notation in the margin that simply read NEVER AGAIN in handwriting that was almost certainly the shopkeeper's.

Pathwell leaned over.

"That seems editorial."

"It was written during the horse incident."

"Which one?"

The shopkeeper stared at him.

Pathwell nodded. "Fair."

The shopkeeper turned the ledger around.

"Your outstanding balance."

Pathwell looked at it.

"Reasonable."

"It has been reasonable for eighty-three years."

"I was letting it mature."

"Debt is not cheese."

"Some cheeses would disagree."

The shopkeeper ignored him and pointed toward the far end of the counter.

A stack of books waited there.

Then Elizabeth realized it was not a stack.

It was several stacks.

Hardcovers. Journals. Blank archival paper. Two wrapped bundles. A wooden crate with a lid tied on by twine. Enough weight to make the counter complain quietly beneath it.

Pathwell looked pleased.

"Excellent."

The shopkeeper looked over his glasses.

"Did Camp Cunnan ask you for all of this?"

Pathwell was suddenly very interested in the crate.

"They need it."

"That was not what I asked."

"No."

The shopkeeper waited.

Pathwell waited better.

The shopkeeper sighed and looked back at the ledger.

"The order and the old balance together, then."

Pathwell reached into his coat and set an old coin on the counter.

The shopkeeper did not look at it.

Pathwell added a second.

Nothing.

A folded note.

The shopkeeper's eyes moved to it and away.

"No."

"You haven't even read it."

"I have met you before."

Pathwell put the note back into his coat.

He looked at the stacks.

Then at the ledger.

Then at the shopkeeper.

"Future."

Elizabeth looked at him.

The shopkeeper took off his glasses.

"Yours?"

"I have become very particular about that distinction."

"Yours?"

"Yes."

The shopkeeper leaned back.

For the first time, the dry irritation left his face.

He looked at Pathwell the way a doctor looks at an X-ray that has confirmed something they hoped would not be there.

"You're thin."

Pathwell smiled.

"Still standing."

"Those are not the same condition."

"They overlap enough for commerce."

The shopkeeper looked at him for another second.

Then he turned the ledger around.

"The price is the price."

Pathwell's smile disappeared.

Not dramatically.

Just gone.

Elizabeth looked from one man to the other.

"What does that mean?"

Neither answered her.

Pathwell put his hand flat on the open ledger.

Nothing glowed.

Nothing burned.

For a second Elizabeth thought nothing was happening at all.

Then the room became too large.

Not physically.

Her eyes insisted the counter was still three feet away, the shelves still where they had been, the ceiling still low and crowded with hanging lamps.

But around Pathwell something opened.

Fine lines of pale light spread from his shadow across the floor.

They branched.

Then branched again.

And again.

Too many to follow. Thin bright divisions slipping beneath shelves, up the counter, through one another—not roads, not pictures, not visions of places, just the unbearable impression of continuation. Of things that might still go somewhere.

Pathwell inhaled.

The shopkeeper placed two fingers on the ledger.

Some of the branching light folded inward.

Went dark.

Nothing else changed.

That was the worst part.

No thunder.

No cosmic announcement.

The shop remained the shop.

A pipe ticked somewhere in the wall.

The cat cleaned one foot.

And something around Pathwell had become narrower.

Elizabeth knew that without knowing how she knew it.

Pathwell's fingers tightened against the ledger.

The last pale lines vanished.

His knees hit the floor.

He caught himself on the edge of the counter with one hand.

The shopkeeper immediately put his glasses back on.

"Settled," he said.

Pathwell stayed there, breathing through his nose.

His right arm had begun to shake.

He tucked it close to his side as if this were a matter of posture.

The shopkeeper made three entries in the ledger.

One line for the order.

One for the old balance.

One Elizabeth could not read because his hand covered it.

Then he closed the book.

"Thank you, Mr. Pathwell."

Pathwell looked up from the floor.

"Don't sound so pleased."

"I have been waiting eighty-three years to write that zero."

"Vindictive."

"Accurate."

Pathwell pulled himself upright using the counter.

The shopkeeper slid Nana's cookbook and Elizabeth's diary toward her.

"These," he said, "were never his to spend."

Elizabeth took them.

The diary was cool under her hand.

The cookbook was heavier.

Not actually heavier.

Missing one page should have made it lighter.

It didn't.

She ran her thumb along the torn place in the binding and stopped there.

Pathwell saw.

He looked away first.

"You paid with your future," Elizabeth said.

Pathwell flexed his shaking hand once.

"Part of it."

"What part?"

"If I knew that precisely, I would make worse decisions with greater confidence."

"That's supposed to reassure me?"

"No. I think we're past that stage of our relationship."

The shopkeeper began arranging receipts.

"Most practitioners prefer not to know too precisely," he said.

Elizabeth looked at him.

"Why?"

"Because people are very good at mistaking measurement for control."

Pathwell pointed at him.

"See? This is why no one invites him anywhere."

"You are in my shop."

"Technically I invited myself."

"That has also been noted."

Elizabeth looked at the stacks of books.

Then at Pathwell's hand, still trembling despite his effort to make the rest of him casual.

"Who are those for?"

"Camp."

"Camp what?"

"Cunnan."

"That's not an answer either."

"No. But it is a noun. Progress."

The shopkeeper pulled a handcart out from behind the counter.

It unfolded twice, extended once more, and stopped at a size that should not have fit behind the counter in the first place.

Pathwell stared at it.

"You had that the entire time?"

"Yes."

"And you've watched me carry things out of here by hand."

"Yes."

"For decades."

The shopkeeper put one wrapped bundle on the cart.

"The account is settled. I feel no need to lie now."

Pathwell looked genuinely wounded.

Elizabeth laughed before she could stop herself.

It escaped once, short and surprised.

Both men looked at her.

She immediately stopped.

"Don't make anything of that."

Pathwell smiled.

"Wouldn't dream of it."

"I know what you do when people say that now."

"You're learning."

They loaded the cart.

Elizabeth held her own books against her chest while Pathwell moved the Camp order into place. He tried lifting the crate one-handed.

His arm shook hard enough that he put it back down.

He waited a second.

Then used both hands.

No one commented.

When everything was stacked, the shopkeeper walked to a narrow door beside a shelf of ledgers.

Elizabeth was certain it had not been there before.

He opened it.

Cold pine air entered the shop.

Beyond the threshold was darkness, trees, and a dirt path silvered by a moon that had not been visible from the city.

Pathwell took the cart handle.

"Right."

Elizabeth looked behind her.

There was no city door.

The shopkeeper followed her glance.

"If you want the sidewalk, I can return you to the sidewalk."

Pathwell stopped.

He did not turn around.

That mattered.

Elizabeth looked down at the diary and cookbook in her arms.

They were hers again.

No technicality.

No Pathwell walking away with them.

No wet thing in the hall forcing her feet to move.

She could go back.

Her apartment was a problem, not an impossibility. Hotels existed. Police existed, though the explanation remained difficult. Her meeting had not yet happened. Her life had been damaged in ways that ranged from metaphysical to extremely annoying, but it was still there.

The shopkeeper waited.

Pathwell waited too.

For once, badly.

His fingers tapped the cart handle once and stopped.

Elizabeth looked through the open door.

A forest.

Somewhere beyond it, apparently, a Camp Cunnan.

A stack of books someone needed badly enough that Pathwell had just spent part of his own future on them without being asked.

A man she did not trust who had nevertheless put his hand on a ledger and come away shaking.

A world behind a concrete wall that served the worst coffee she had ever tasted.

She could go home.

Elizabeth stepped toward the cart.

Pathwell looked at her.

She put Nana's cookbook on top of the Camp books.

Then her diary beside it.

"Don't lose those."

Pathwell looked at the two books, then at her.

"Wouldn't dream of it."

"Again. I know what that means from you now."

"Fair."

Elizabeth put both hands on the side of the cart.

"Where are we taking these?"

Pathwell's smile came back slowly.

"Camp Cunnan."

"If you say that like it explains anything one more time, I'm going back to the sidewalk."

"Understood."

He pulled the cart through the doorway.

Elizabeth followed because she chose to.

The shopkeeper closed the door behind them.
