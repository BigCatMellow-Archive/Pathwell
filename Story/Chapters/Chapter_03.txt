Chapter Three

She found him staring into a streetlight.

"And where exactly do you think you're going?"
"I just need to swing by a bookstore real quick."
"I'm not going anywhere with you until you start answering some questions."
"Lizzy, I could answer all your questions, even the ones you'll have next. But we really do have places to be."
"I said I'm not going anywhere with you. Are you even listening to me?"


Her clenched brow softened. "What kind of bookstore is even open this late anyway?"
Pathwell turned. "The right kind."


"Well don't think for a second that I forgot you still have my cookbook."
"This old thing?" He shuffled the stack of books balanced against his hip and held it out. "Yours. Take it."
Her hands were on it before she'd decided. She ran them over the cover, checking the spine.

Her copy of The Joy of Cooking was in his stack. The worn paperback on French technique she'd bought at a garage sale and never actually used. The diary, its spine charred at the corner. All of them wedged between Pathwell's journals and letters like they belonged there, which they didn't — they'd been sitting in a box in her apartment she had never unpacked.

"Those are mine."
"Are they? Could've sworn I grabbed those from the— yes, actually, those are yours."
She reached for them. Stopped.

The diary was in the middle of the stack. Charred corner, pages swelled from moisture at some point and never quite returned to flat. She pulled it free and put it in her coat.

Beside her, Pathwell was opening a portal in the concrete wall, a folded piece of paper pressed flat, light leaking through the creases.

She had a meeting at nine.

Nine o'clock. A door. Whatever came next.

She took her books and put them on his stack.


"I didn't get the lo mein by the way," she said.
"I'll add it to your tab." The light stretched past the edges of the paper until a doorway stood where the wall had been, steam hissing from its seams. Pathwell stepped through without looking back.

Rows of books filled the frame.

From inside, a loose page drifted out, turned once in the air, settled at her feet. A smell followed it. Old paper, and dried leather. Binding glue gone brittle. And beneath all of it, dust that had been settling longer than she'd been alive.

She picked up the page. The paper was warm from the light.

She walked through.

The street noise cut the moment she crossed — not gradually, all at once, like someone had shut a window she hadn't known was open. No cars, no sirens, no wind. Just the soft groan of shelves under centuries of weight.

Behind her the city kept going. She could feel it the way you feel a room on the other side of a closed door — present, pressing, doing its thing without her. The cold was still on her sleeves. The low tide smell that had come through her apartment door had followed her this far, clinging to the inside of her coat, but it went no further. Whatever this place was, it had its own idea about what the air should be doing.

The shop held opinions about itself. It was cramped and it knew it, and it had decided this was a feature rather than a flaw. Floor to ceiling, wall to wall: journals with cracked spines, letters bundled in ribbon, recipes inked on index cards spotted with oil. All shoved into corners with the conviction of someone who has run out of room and doesn't particularly care who notices.

Nothing was labeled. The organization was not alphabetical or by subject — it was by something else, some system she couldn't read, the way you can tell someone has a system for their desk even if you can't explain what it is. Titles in three languages, margins thick with pencil. A bundle of envelopes held together with a rubber band that had gone brittle at the center. Everything here had been held, repeatedly, by people who had found it and not put it back.

"Lizzy, would you mind closing the door?" Pathwell's head appeared from around a corner. She reached for it, but it swung closed on its own and dissolved into the wall. The shelves collapsed inward where it had been, filling the gap like the shop had simply decided the entrance was unnecessary now.


She turned around and he was gone. She looked toward the corner he'd disappeared around. Nothing came back.

"Pathwell?"

A shush from somewhere deep between the rows.

The air pressed close. Not threatening — more like a waiting room that had been waiting for a very long time. Her pace slowed without asking permission. The shelf on her left was warm, faintly, the way surfaces are warm when people have been touching them for a long time. She had not reached out for it. Her hand had found it on its own.

She pulled it back.

Something stopped halfway through an inhale. Then the next shelf she passed hit her: a flash of a pet she'd never had, a love she'd never felt, a parent she'd never buried. Tears ready, for something she couldn't name and didn't own.

The shop was full of things like that.

She stopped. Her fingers found the nearest spine on their own — a journal, cracked along the edge. Warm. She pulled her hand back.

The next turn came up fast, and something that was not the shop's doing began to rise in her chest. Pressure, slow at first — not pain but the shape of pain, the way a door feels before it opens. The aisle was the same width. It only seemed closer.

She pressed herself against the shelf and breathed out. The breath didn't finish right. The borrowed grief and her own arrived at the same time and she couldn't sort one from the other — what the shop had given her and what had been there already: the specific and durable ache of not knowing what she was supposed to be doing with her life, which had been sitting in her chest for longer than this shop had been standing and which the shop had simply found and poured into.

The pressure built. Not fast — patient, which was worse. The kind of thing that doesn't announce itself. Just keeps being there until there's nothing else.

Her knees found the carpet.

She stayed there a moment — the shelves on either side holding still, the air not asking anything of her. From somewhere deeper in the shop, the tick of something settling under weight.


A thin orange cat was sitting in front of her when her eyes focused. It blinked. On the other side of it, Pathwell crouched down, holding out a plain white mug on a plain white saucer, sloshing something dark over the rim. "Just in time."

She took the cup and drank.

It was awful.

Dollar store beans through reused filters, left to sit in a pot that had given up. The kind of coffee that tasted like 2 a.m. and highlighters and a draft due tomorrow. She'd made it herself a hundred times, in a kitchen she didn't live in anymore.

She held the mug with both hands and didn't put it down.

Behind a large oaken counter, a round man in round-rimmed glasses was making ticks in a ledger, carrying ones. A brass plate on the counter read, in small engraved letters: The Space Between.

The cat pressed against her shins and she reached down automatically. A single claw found the soft skin at the base of her thumb, precise as a signature, and blood welled up in a thin line.

The cat's eyes gleamed. It licked a paw.

"He wants his tribute," the shopkeeper said without looking up. "I'd let him have it."

Pathwell was already moving.

He was already pulling a strip of paper from his jacket — without ceremony, the way someone checks the time — and pressing his thumb to the corner. A brief warmth. Gone. He laid the strip across the cut and it faded into her hand, taking the pain with it, leaving behind the specific, inexplicable feeling of a boy she'd known in middle school.

He rubbed a thumb over what remained. "Unfortunately, scars are outside my wheelhouse."

She looked at her hand.

"Finish the coffee," he said, dropping into the chair across from her. "Ask whatever you want."

She gulped the rest of it.

"I'll take that as a yes."

The barter opened with Pathwell pulling a coin from his jacket — too old to look real, though it clearly was — and setting it flat on the counter without ceremony. The shopkeeper put his glasses on, picked it up between two fingers, and held it under the lamp.

"Lydian," he said.

"The very one."

"I have nine of these."

"Yours now are ten."

The shopkeeper set it on the stack of his own and took the glasses off. "Rai Stone?"

"Lost it," Pathwell said, which was clearly untrue and clearly the answer.

The shopkeeper put the glasses back on.

It had the texture of an old argument between people who had established long ago where it ended. The shopkeeper's glasses coming off and going back on. Pathwell losing interest in looking nervous around the time the coins came up.

"I've got potential to sell," Pathwell said finally. "Lots."

The shopkeeper looked at him over his glasses. "You did."

Pathwell laughed. "Can we do this before I change my mind?"

The shopkeeper opened something — a panel, a cabinet, a door that hadn't been there — and placed a leather-bound tome on the counter with the care of something irreplaceable. Pressure filled the room. Light at the edge of everything, roots or branches or both. The counter. Her hands. The brass plate.

Pathwell's voice, changed — not in tone, but in the way sound changes when it gets expensive.

Then it was over. He was on his knees, catching himself with one hand against the counter's edge. The room felt lighter than it had, the way rooms do after a window's been opened.

"Thank you, Mr. Pathwell." The shopkeeper was already writing in the ledger. "Your bill has been paid in full. Will that be paper or plastic?"

Pathwell looked up. His arm was shaking.

"Surprise me," he said.

His arm shook. He did not look at it. He kept his attention on the shopkeeper and the ledger and the middle distance, and the arm kept its own opinion about the situation.

The shopkeeper slid an envelope across the counter and went back to carrying ones.
