Chapter Fourteen

The threshold opened onto the unfinished hand of Hearts.

Pathwell stepped through first.

He looked worse than he had at the museum.

A square of gauze was taped against his temple. One side of his coat had dried blood at the collar. He held himself carefully through the ribs.

Stansbury came through behind him carrying the museum's green first-aid box in one hand and a paper bag in the other.

The threshold closed.

For half a second nobody moved.

Then Milo pointed at Pathwell. "You made him drop his cards."

Stansbury looked at the cards scattered around Shade's boots.

"Good morning to you too."

"He had the queen."

Shade's right hand was still open toward Pathwell. The rest of him had gone very still.

Pathwell looked at the hand, then at the green jacket, then at the card table. It was quick enough that he might have believed no one noticed. Elizabeth did.

"You changed coats," he said.

Shade looked down at himself. "Yes."

"Too many pockets."

"I changed my mind about those."

"Dangerous habit," Pathwell said.

Shade's hand trembled, and he closed it with his left. "I'm experimenting."

The older woman in the knitted cap collected the cards nearest her feet.

"Are we finishing this hand?"

Everyone looked at her.

She looked back.

"I was winning."

"I was winning," Shade said.

Milo made a rude noise.

The archivist bent to retrieve the queen of spades and held it up. "Evidence is inconclusive." He laid it on the card table.

For one strange second the morning almost resumed. Then Pathwell swayed.

Stansbury caught his elbow. "Sit down."

"I'm standing beautifully."

"You're listing."

"That's a nautical term."

"You are doing it on land. Sit."

Pathwell looked toward Elizabeth. His eyes found the sling first, and the humor left his face.

"How bad?"

"It was out. It's back in."

Pathwell looked at the sling again. "Lizzy—"

"Elizabeth."

He closed his eyes briefly. "Elizabeth."

That was all he said. It wasn't an apology, and he didn't try to make it one.

Stansbury pushed a low stool behind Pathwell's knees.

Pathwell sat.

The paper bag landed beside him. "Breakfast," Stansbury said.

"I don't want breakfast."

"Then hold it until you develop judgment."

"That could spoil."

"So could you."

Milo looked at Shade. "Are they always like this?"

Shade's mouth tightened. "Apparently."

"You would know."

Shade looked at him, and Milo's face changed. "Sorry."

"It's fine." Then, after a beat: "Mostly."

Milo started gathering cards.

Mama Baga stood near the steps of her wagon with both hands resting on the rail.

She hadn't said anything since Pathwell arrived.

Pathwell looked toward her. "Mama."

"No."

"I haven't asked anything."

"I am getting ahead."

"That's my habit."

"I know."

Her eyes went to his temple. "Healer."

"Stansbury did it."

"That is not the same thing."

Stansbury looked offended. "I used antiseptic."

"With resentment."

"It improves compliance."

Mama Baga pointed toward her wagon. "Later."

Pathwell touched the gauze. "I am being medically outnumbered."

"Good."

Pathwell's left hand went into the inside of his coat and came back holding nothing dramatic: three brass pins no longer than a little finger, a narrow loop of dull metal, and a length of pale waxed cord wound around a flat wooden card.

Pathwell laid them on his knee beside the paper bag.

Stansbury glanced down. "What are you doing?"

"Trying not to have this conversation with my entire coat digging into my ribs."

He pulled another small brass piece from a pocket and set it beside the others.

Stansbury looked at the collection. "You carry an unreasonable amount of hardware."

"You carry foam swords."

"Occupationally."

"So do I."

"Your occupation is causing paperwork."

Elizabeth felt the foam dagger at the back of her waistband and didn't mention it.

Pathwell unwrapped the waxed cord.

His fingers moved more slowly than usual, and the injury made every small motion look like a concession. At the time, it looked like fidgeting.

Shade had not taken his eyes off the brass. "Where did you get that?" he asked.

Pathwell looked at the loop. "Had it."

"I know."

Shade rubbed his right wrist. "I remember buying the first one."

Pathwell's fingers stopped on the cord. "Do you."

"Rain. Somewhere with stone streets. You argued about the price for forty minutes and paid exactly what he asked."

Stansbury sighed. "That sounds right."

Pathwell looked down at the brass. "Prague."

Shade shook his head. "I've never been there."

"Then don't borrow mine."

Shade's face hardened. "I'm not trying to."

Pathwell heard himself. The brass loop rested in his palm, and he closed his hand around it. "No," he said. "You're not."

He set the loop on the dirt between his boots, then one brass pin beside it, then another.

Stansbury opened the paper bag and took out a biscuit. "Eat."

Pathwell accepted the biscuit with his right hand while his left pushed the first pin into the dirt.

"You are confusing care with tyranny."

"Family tradition."

"Inherited badly."

Shade's right hand opened again, hard, and his arm turned toward Pathwell before he caught it. The motion knocked the edge of the card table.

Milo grabbed the deck before it fell. "Again?"

"It seems so," Shade said.

Pathwell watched the hand. "How close does it feel?"

Shade laughed once. "You're here."

"Before I came through."

"Stronger."

"Direction?"

Shade looked at him. "You know the answer."

"I know what I feel."

"Then use yours."

Pathwell broke the biscuit in half and didn't eat either half. With his thumb he pressed the second brass pin into the dirt. The two pins sat a foot apart with the dull loop between them. It looked like nothing.

"The museum curator hates you," Stansbury said.

"She hated me already."

"Not like this."

"Good to preserve novelty."

"She wants an inventory by tonight."

Pathwell threaded the waxed cord through the brass loop. "She'll have one."

"And a written account."

"Cruel."

"In your handwriting."

Pathwell finally looked offended. "Now you're being punitive."

Elizabeth said, "You should do it."

Pathwell looked at her. "I will."

There was no joke in it. Then his attention went back to the cord. He passed one end around the first pin and the other around the second: a narrow line between two fixed points.

Shade stood, and the stool scraped the dirt. The older woman in the knitted cap looked up. Milo froze with the cards squared between his palms.

Pathwell didn't look at Shade. "Sit down."

"What is that?" Shade's voice had gone less casual.

Pathwell adjusted the loop until it rested exactly halfway between the pins.

"A way to look at the connection without you dislocating a shoulder every time I get close."

Elizabeth stared at him. "You are not making that joke today."

Pathwell's face tightened. "No." He moved the loop a fraction. "Sorry."

Shade stepped around the table, his right hand dragging toward Pathwell as he moved. He held it against his chest. "That's not diagnostic."

Pathwell looked up. Shade pointed with his left hand. "Two anchors. One return."

Stansbury stopped chewing.

Pathwell's face went blank in the way it did when expression would admit too much.

Shade stared at the brass. "You're building a rejoining frame."

Nobody at the table moved for a second.

Milo looked from Shade to Pathwell. "What does that mean?"

The archivist turned to him. "Milo. Archive. Put these back in the drawer and bring me the north-route copy."

Milo frowned. "But—"

"Now."

Milo took the deck and ran toward the archive wagon, his sock falling as he went, and disappeared through the open door.

Elizabeth looked at Pathwell. "Tell me he's wrong."

Pathwell put the untouched biscuit on the paper bag. "He isn't."

Stansbury stood. "Little brother."

"Don't."

"I was going to ask what you think you're doing."

"I know."

"Then save me the sentence."

Pathwell took the third brass pin from his knee. Shade's eyes followed it.

"No." It was quiet.

Pathwell held the pin between thumb and forefinger. Shade stepped closer, and the draw pulled his right arm forward. This time he let the arm hang open instead of fighting it, palm toward Pathwell. His choice was in the rest of him, which stayed still.

"No," Shade said again.

Pathwell looked at him. "You don't know what I'm going to do."

"I know the shape."

"You remember the shape."

"Yes."

"That isn't the same as understanding it."

"There he is," Shade said.

Shade pointed at the two pins and the loop. "You are going to use the existing connection as the return line. You're going to put the unresolved part back against its source and make the working decide that we're one settled structure instead of two."

Pathwell said nothing.

"Am I wrong?"

"Not entirely."

"What happens to me?"

Pathwell's eyes shifted to the third pin.

Shade waited. "What happens to me?"

"I don't know exactly."

Shade looked down at the green jacket: one hand in a pocket he had decided he liked, the other still dragged open toward Pathwell.

"Do I remain myself?"

Pathwell stood too quickly. Pain hit him across the ribs and forced one hand to the stool. Stansbury moved toward him, and Pathwell waved him back.

"I think the distinction can survive."

Shade's face did not move. "Can."

"Yes."

"Not will."

Pathwell looked away. "No."

Shade nodded once. "Then no."

He didn't try to tear the frame apart. He simply stood where he was and refused.

Elizabeth looked at Pathwell. "You heard him."

Pathwell pressed a hand to his ribs. "I heard him."

"Then put it away."

"If I leave this unresolved, the draw stays. The blobs stay confused. He stays tied to me and I stay tied to him."

Shade said, "Then leave it unresolved."

Pathwell looked at him.

Shade's right hand shook in the air between them. "I am the person living with it," he said. "No."

He looked at Elizabeth. "You don't have to stay for this."

"I'm going to be there when he decides."

Mama Baga came off her steps. Her bracelets didn't click, and that silence moved through Camp faster than a shout. She stopped beside the two brass pins and looked at the loop, then at the third pin in Pathwell's hand.

"Stop."

"Mama—" Pathwell began.

"No."

"You haven't seen the completed frame."

"I don't need the completed frame."

She pointed at the line between the pins. "You built a return working."

"A rejoining working."

"Same arrogance in a better coat."

Shade glanced at his green jacket. Nobody laughed.

Mama Baga looked at Pathwell's hand. "Put the third pin away."

"The connection is already unstable," Pathwell said.

"Then do not put more of yourself into it."

"That's the only part I can control."

Stansbury heard it too. "No," he said. "You are not paying for this."

"It accepts my pruning directly."

"I understood the sentence."

Stansbury stepped between Pathwell and the frame, not onto it and not touching anything, just close enough that ignoring him would take effort.

"You spent yourself at the Space Between because you decided Camp needed books. You spent yourself for the old job because you decided somebody else's problem required your method. How many times are you going to call that responsibility before you notice the rest of us are still here?"

Pathwell's face went flat. "Move."

"No."

Shade said, "Don't make him the reason."

Everyone looked at him.

Shade's right hand remained open. "If this fails because Stansbury stood there, he'll spend the rest of his life explaining that part instead of the choice."

Stansbury looked at Shade. Then he stepped aside. That took the excuse away, and Pathwell knew it.

"Stop," Mama Baga said again.

Pathwell looked down at the third pin. For a moment his hand loosened, and the pin nearly fell.

Elizabeth could see the exit. So could he. Put the pin away. Pull the cord loose. Leave the morning unfinished, and let the problem stay a problem.

Pathwell closed his fingers around the pin. "I can't keep leaving damage behind me."

Elizabeth stared at him. "This is leaving damage behind you."

"Not if it works."

Shade's face changed. "You still think the result gets to decide whether the choice was acceptable."

Pathwell looked at him. "I am trying to make this right."

"For who?"

Pathwell had no answer fast enough.

Shade nodded. "No."

Mama Baga said, "Stop."

Elizabeth said, "Pathwell."

He drove the third pin into the dirt.

The brass loop lifted an inch above the ground. The waxed cord went taut between all three pins, though no hand pulled it. Stansbury swore. Mama Baga stepped back.

"Do not release payment," Mama Baga said.

Pathwell's breathing had gone shallow. "I know."

"Then stop now."

The dirt beneath his boots changed. Elizabeth had seen something like it once before, in the Space Between: pale branching light spreading from Pathwell's shadow, possibility given a shape only because the eye needed something to fail at understanding. At the Space Between, the tree had grown to his chest before it ever began to branch. Here the branches were thinner and older, and still his.

The brass frame recognized them, and the loop turned toward the light.

Shade stumbled. The draw had gone rigid between them. His right arm extended fully toward Pathwell, and he planted his feet and let it happen.

Elizabeth's hand went to the foam dagger at the back of her waistband. Stansbury had made it to cut things that don't separate easily, and had said it was designed not to cut people. Whatever held Shade's arm out now might be a binding, and might be him. She didn't know what it would cut, or whose it was to cut. She left it where it was.

"I am not fighting you," Shade said.

Pathwell looked at him. "I know."

"Then whatever happens next is yours."

"I know."

Mama Baga crouched beside the frame without touching it. "Pathwell. Your intent is split."

He looked at her. "No."

"Yes."

She pointed at the floating loop. "You built it to rejoin what you are calling one structure. But you are also trying to keep him separate."

Pathwell swallowed. "I can preserve him inside the settlement."

Shade laughed once. "Inside."

Mama Baga's eyes stayed on Pathwell. "You don't know that."

"No."

Mama Baga pointed at his hands. "The working knows what you mean, not what you wish you meant. You are giving it two incompatible answers."

The pale branches shivered. The brass loop dipped, and for one second the whole structure began to loosen. Pathwell could still let it. Mama Baga saw it too.

"Lift your hand," she said.

Pathwell's right hand was hovering over the loop.

"Lift it," Mama Baga said. "Let the frame fall. Nothing has released yet."

Shade's arm trembled toward Pathwell. Shade held his eyes. "Nothing changing is acceptable to me."

The frame loosened another fraction. Pathwell's hand rose. Almost.

Then his eyes went to Elizabeth's sling. The museum gauze at his temple had started bleeding through again. He looked past Mama Baga at the archive wagon, its door standing open. Elizabeth knew what was on those shelves: the blue road notebook, weather ledgers, family letters, Nana's cookbook on the front reserve shelf, her diary in the intake rack by the entrance. All the things people had trusted the place to hold.

Pathwell looked back at Shade. "If I stop, the cleanup problem remains."

"Yes."

"The draw remains."

"Yes."

"You remain tied to me."

"Yes."

Pathwell closed his eyes. Elizabeth knew what he was going to do before he did it.

"Don't."

His hand came down, and he forced the loop flat. The branches broke toward it, committed, and possibility folded inward from Pathwell's shadow and released into the working.

The physical effect hit him first. His body bowed over the frame. The gauze at his temple darkened. His right arm shook hard enough that his hand blurred, and the brass pins sank another inch into the dirt. The future he had offered entered the structure, and the structure had nowhere coherent to put it.

The loop pulled toward Shade, then toward Pathwell, then tried to hold both positions at once. The waxed cord split into two shadows. One said together. One said separate. Neither was language, and Elizabeth understood them anyway.

Pathwell looked up, and fear reached his face before confidence could stop it.

"Hands off," Mama Baga said.

"It's released."

"I know. Stop forcing it."

"I can still settle it."

"No."

Pathwell put both hands around the brass loop.

Shade didn't move. His right hand stayed open toward Pathwell because the draw required it and everything else about him stayed exactly where he had chosen to stand.

"Pathwell," Elizabeth said.

His eyes found hers. "Let it fail," she said.

His fingers tightened. "I can contain it."

"That's what you said at the museum."

The loop screamed, a real sound this time: metal under more force than metal had agreed to carry. The first brass pin tore out of the dirt and struck Pathwell in the chest hard enough to throw him backward. The second followed. The frame collapsed toward him, not toward Shade or Elizabeth, but toward the practitioner who had supplied the contradiction and the payment.

Pathwell hit the ground, and the released possibility came with him.

Pale branches snapped inward around his body and then burst apart into loose, ownerless threads across the dirt.

Fresh spillage.

The third pin held for one impossible second, then ripped free. The brass loop shot sideways and hit the hanging lantern beside the archive door, still lit from the night. Glass broke. The lantern fell. Oil crossed the wooden threshold in a bright ordinary sheet, and flame followed it.

For half a heartbeat the fire was only fire, small and yellow. It caught the edge of the narrow woven runner just inside the archive door.

Then the lower wooden wall, old and dry enough to take.

The archivist moved first. "Water!"

People ran. The flame climbed. Inside the wagon something heavy crashed, and Elizabeth turned toward the sound.

The shallow intake rack had tipped into the entrance aisle.

Her diary slid across the floor and stopped near the threshold, cover open, ordinary pages fanned against the boards.

Beyond it, on the front reserve shelf, Nana's cookbook was still visible inside its support.

A second crash came from deeper in the wagon. Then Milo shouted, more surprised than afraid. "Hey!"

The archivist went white. "Milo?"

Smoke moved across the doorway. The fire reached the first shelf of charged records, and only then did the room change. A hundred small human impressions broke loose into the heat at once.

Rain on canvas.

Bread rising.

A road under wagon wheels.

Someone laughing at a funeral and hating themselves for it.

Someone writing a daughter's name three times because once had not felt sufficient.

The ordinary fire had found history, and history answered badly. The wagon lurched as released charge pushed against the burning structure from a dozen incompatible directions.

A shelf inside went over. Milo cried out. "I'm stuck!"

Elizabeth was already moving.

Her shoulder screamed inside the sling. Shade was one breath behind her. Stansbury ran toward the archive from the other side, and Mama Baga shouted for the bucket line.

Pathwell lay in the dirt among the pale loose threads of his failed payment, conscious and unable to stand.

Shade was still alive.

The draw between them was still there.

The archive was burning.

And inside it, Milo shouted again.
