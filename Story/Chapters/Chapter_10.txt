Chapter Ten

The road had the quality of roads in early morning — not empty, but not yet claimed.

The fields on either side held their dark at the edges. Elizabeth walked on the shoulder facing traffic because somebody had taught her to do that when she was nine and apparently the lesson had survived everything else.

The receipt from the diner was folded in her pocket.

Three turns written on the back in the waitress's blocky handwriting.

Second left after the water tower underlined twice.

The foam dagger rested against the back of Elizabeth's waistband. She had moved it there before leaving the diner and checked for it twice since, which was more attention than she had ever expected to devote to a toy weapon before sunrise.

Her shoulder hurt where the seatbelt had caught her in the crash.

Her heel hurt because her shoes had not consented to becoming hiking equipment.

Her mouth still tasted like coffee.

That was the worst injury by a considerable margin.

She walked another hundred yards before the taste pulled up a different cup.

White ceramic.

Orange cat.

Pathwell's hand holding it out. *Just in time.*

Space Between coffee.

Somehow worse than the diner's.

Late nights spent studying. Familiar enough to cut through a room full of other people's feelings.

*Familiar things are useful.*

Elizabeth smiled despite herself.

Then stopped smiling because Pathwell had been right about something and the morning had already taken enough from her.

She kept walking.

Nothing answered the memory.

No voice arrived in her head.

No invisible hand turned her around.

There was only cold road, bad shoes, the folded receipt, and the question Shade had left with her.

What did Pathwell choose?

Elizabeth walked toward the bar because that was where the waitress's directions led.

If Pathwell was not there, she would decide what came next when she reached that fact.

For once she was willing to let the next decision wait until it existed.


Stansbury lived two miles from the ditch.

This had not saved the Cadillac.

It had only made the walk home shorter.

The house sat behind a low stone wall with a detached garage, a square white work van parked beside it, and enough porch repairs in progress to suggest Stansbury believed finishing one project before beginning another was a moral weakness.

By the time he and Pathwell reached the kitchen, the eastern windows had started turning gray.

Stansbury opened the freezer.

"Peas."

Pathwell was leaning against the table with both hands.

"Is that a diagnosis?"

"It's what I have."

Stansbury tossed him the bag.

Pathwell caught it against his chest and winced.

"Your bedside manner has declined."

"My car is in a drainage culvert."

"She did apologize."

"I know."

"You sound remarkably calm."

"I have not reached the axle emotionally."

Pathwell put the peas against the back of his neck.

For a few seconds neither man spoke.

The house was quiet in the particular way houses are quiet just before morning. Refrigerator motor. Pipes. A clock somewhere that ticked too loudly because nothing else was competing with it.

Stansbury looked at Pathwell.

"Can you find her?"

Pathwell looked toward the east-facing window.

The weak tension was still there.

Shade.

Not Elizabeth.

"No."

"Good."

Pathwell looked at him.

"That was a strange response."

"I wanted to make sure you knew the difference."

Pathwell took the peas away from his neck.

"The draw finds him."

"Barely."

"Barely is still him."

Stansbury nodded.

"And not her."

"No."

"So don't use it like it is."

Pathwell looked offended.

"I had not planned to."

Stansbury waited.

Pathwell sighed.

"I had considered whether proximity might—"

"No."

"I said considered."

"And now you've finished."

Pathwell put the peas back against his neck.

"You become authoritarian before breakfast."

"Library."

Stansbury walked out of the kitchen.

Pathwell followed.


The room Stansbury called the library had once been a dining room.

The table was still there, but bookshelves had colonized every wall and then continued into the corners as if waiting for permission had proved inefficient. Half the shelves held ordinary books. The other half held boxes, wrapped folios, labelled envelopes, old notebooks, ledgers, and the kind of small household papers most people threw away until enough time passed that somebody else decided they were history.

A workbench occupied the far wall.

Foam swords in various stages of undignified seriousness leaned beside it.

Stansbury set a flat archival box on the table.

Pathwell looked at the label.

ELLISON ESTATE — PERSONAL CORRESPONDENCE — ORIGINALS COPIED

"You kept those?"

"I keep many things. Quietly."

"That's my line."

"No. You keep receipts until they become archaeological sites."

Stansbury opened the box.

Inside were letters separated by sheets of thin paper.

He passed over several and stopped at one folded into quarters.

No envelope.

No stamp.

Blue ink gone brown at the edges.

Pathwell did not touch it.

He did not need to.

The ache in the paper reached him from across the table.

"Unsent," he said.

"Yes."

"Unanswered."

"By definition."

Pathwell looked at Stansbury.

"That's not always how letters work."

"She wrote to the same man for six years and mailed none of them. He married somebody else in year two. Her niece sold the papers with the rest of the estate. I copied everything before I separated the originals."

Pathwell looked back at the folded page.

"If I use this, it's gone."

"I know."

"The copy keeps the words."

"Not the thing."

Pathwell's hand remained beside the letter.

Stansbury watched him.

"You asked me once why I copy before I buy."

"I remember."

"This is why."

"That doesn't make destroying it free."

"No."

Stansbury nudged the letter toward him.

"It makes the cost mine to approve."

Pathwell looked up.

Stansbury held his eyes.

"Find her."

Pathwell touched the letter.


The page was not about finding anyone.

That mattered.

It was about someone absent who occupied more room than people who were present.

About writing toward a person who would never read the words.

About wanting distance to become smaller and having no right to demand that it did.

Pathwell put two fingers flat against the center fold.

The ink warmed beneath them.

Stansbury moved the other papers farther away.

"What else?"

"A way to know which person."

"Name?"

"Names are cheap."

"Says the man named Pathwell."

"Especially names."

The first line of the letter began to fade.

Not burn.

Not glow.

The handwriting simply loosened from the paper as if the page had stopped being able to remember it.

Pathwell closed his eyes.

Longing came through first.

Not his.

A woman's patience worn raw around one particular absence. A porch in summer. The idea of a train arriving. The knowledge that it probably would not.

The impressions were incomplete and private and already fading at their edges.

Pathwell worked with the shape rather than the details.

Someone absent.

Someone specific.

A connection that mattered because it had actually existed.

The letter disappeared beneath his fingers.

The blank paper followed.

Gone.

Stansbury looked at the empty table where it had been.

"Now what?"

"Now I give it something real between us."

"You and Elizabeth."

"Yes."

Pathwell reached for a memory.

Neon reflected in wet pavement.

Dumplings.

Elizabeth standing at the Chinese place looking at him like he'd personally invented inconvenience.

Nothing.

He let it go.

Camp.

Bark tea.

The turnip boy's sock refusing employment.

Nothing.

The archive wagon.

Elizabeth watching her grandmother's cookbook disappear onto the reserve shelf.

The working tightened and slipped.

Too much of the memory belonged to her and not enough to him.

Pathwell opened his eyes.

"Again," Stansbury said.

"Helpful."

"I'm excellent at this part."

Pathwell closed them.

The Space Between.

The cat.

Elizabeth in a chair, overwhelmed by a hundred lives she had never lived.

A white mug in his hand.

*Just in time.*

Her face after the first swallow.

*That's terrible.*

His answer.

*I know.*

Something in the working caught.

Not hard.

Clean.

Like two pieces of thread finally crossing at the same point.

Pathwell opened his eyes.

"Coffee."

Stansbury stared.

"Coffee."

"Terrible coffee."

"That distinction rescued the sentence."

"We both remember it."

"You hope."

Pathwell looked at the place where the letter had been.

"No."

The connection held.

Thin.

Real.

"She does."

Stansbury's expression sharpened.

"Can she feel this?"

"No."

"Can you make her turn around?"

"No."

"Can you talk to her?"

"No."

"Good."

Pathwell looked at him.

"Again with the strange enthusiasm."

"I am developing standards."

"Dangerous at your age."

The connection began to loosen.

Pathwell held up one hand.

"List."

Stansbury was already opening another box.

He pulled out three small scraps of paper.

The first had been written on the back of an envelope in blunt pencil.

MILK
ONIONS
ASPIRIN
STAMPS
SHOE POLISH

Pathwell took it.

"Thrilling."

"Authentic."

"I can feel the shoe polish."

"Try to survive."

The list carried almost no dramatic emotion.

That was useful.

It had been written because somebody meant to leave one place, go to several others, and return having done what they intended to do.

Movement with sequence.

Nothing more romantic than errands.

Pathwell activated it.

The pencil marks darkened.

The page softened beneath his fingers.

For several seconds the borrowed sense of motion had nowhere to attach.

Then it found the thin coffee-memory connection.

Pathwell turned his head.

Not because something moved it.

Because he suddenly knew which road came next in the same practical way the writer of the list had once known which shop came after milk.

"East," he said.

Stansbury picked up the keys to the work van.

"How far?"

"No idea."

"Of course."

"Moving."

"Better."

The grocery list faded completely.

Pathwell followed Stansbury out.


Elizabeth reached the water tower and checked the receipt.

Second left.

The road there was narrower, patched in strips, with a drainage ditch on either side and telephone wires sagging between poles.

She turned.

The decision was hers.

It felt disappointingly ordinary.

Her mouth still tasted faintly of coffee.

She wished it didn't.

A mile later the heel of her right shoe became actively hostile.

Elizabeth stopped beside a mailbox and took the shoe off.

The back of her heel was rubbed red but not bleeding.

"Excellent," she told it.

The mailbox declined responsibility.

She put the shoe back on.

Kept walking.


Stansbury's work van was white, square, and honest about being a work van.

One rear door was a slightly different white from the rest.

The passenger floor contained two screwdrivers, a coffee thermos, three zip ties, and a foam shield with a smiling dragon on it.

Pathwell sat with the bag of peas against his ribs and the second shopping list balanced on one knee.

"How long does this one last?" Stansbury asked.

"Until it doesn't."

"I continue to regret asking you questions."

They passed the first crossroads.

The directional impression weakened.

Pathwell waited.

Gone.

"Pull over."

Stansbury did.

Pathwell took the second list.

SOAP
THREAD
COFFEE
PEACHES
KEROSENE

He looked at Stansbury.

"Coffee."

"Do not anthropomorphize the evidence."

"I was making conversation."

"That's worse."

Pathwell activated the list.

The words disappeared one by one.

The temporary echo of errands settled against the same shared-memory connection.

Road.

Turn.

Continue.

Nothing about Elizabeth's thoughts.

Nothing about what she wanted.

Only where the next movement in the sequence currently pointed.

Pathwell opened his eyes.

"Left at the water tower."

Stansbury put the van in gear.

"That I can use."


Elizabeth heard the engine before she saw the van.

She moved farther onto the shoulder.

The vehicle slowed behind her.

White.

Square.

One rear door a different shade.

For half a second she considered stepping into the ditch.

Then she saw Stansbury behind the wheel.

The van pulled ahead and stopped.

The passenger door opened.

Pathwell got out with a bag of frozen peas held against the back of his neck.

He set it on the roof of the van and had to put one hand against the door before he walked toward her.

He looked worse than she did.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey."

Elizabeth looked at the van.

Then at him.

"How did you find me?"

Pathwell glanced at Stansbury.

Stopped himself.

Looked back at her.

"A letter that deserved better, two grocery lists, and the worst cup of coffee I've had this century."

Elizabeth stared.

"The Space Between."

"Yes."

Her stomach tightened.

"Did you put that memory in my head?"

"No."

The answer came fast.

Then, because fast certainty had become its own problem, Pathwell slowed down.

"I couldn't. You had to remember something we both actually remembered. I kept trying shared things until the working caught one. The lists told us where to look next for a little while. That's all."

Elizabeth looked at Stansbury through the windshield.

He lifted one hand from the wheel.

"I asked the same questions," he said through the open window.

"Repeatedly," Pathwell added.

"Because you repeatedly require supervision."

Elizabeth looked back at Pathwell.

The coffee memory still felt exactly like hers.

Annoying.

Ordinary.

Unchanged except that somebody else had happened to remember it too.

She let that stand.

Pathwell waited, looking for the shape of the conversation.

She didn't give him one.

"Tell me what you did," she said.

He went quiet.

"Shade said you know." She held his eyes. "I'm not asking you whether it's a good idea to tell me. I'm not asking what you think I can handle. I'm asking what you did."

Pathwell put his hands in his pockets. "Lizzy—"

"No."

He stopped.

"Elizabeth," he corrected.

"You don't get to make me the part of this you explain around."

He went quiet.

Then: "Okay."

She walked past him to the van.

Stansbury was somewhere between impressed and unsurprised, which seemed to be his usual territory.

Elizabeth opened the side door and got in.

Pathwell came around, collected the peas from the roof, and climbed into the passenger seat.

"Camp," he said.

Elizabeth looked at him.

"No."

Stansbury kept the van in park.

Pathwell turned.

"No?"

"Not later. Not at Camp. Now."

The gray morning waited outside the windows.

Nobody had anywhere to hide the question by moving.

Pathwell rubbed both hands over his face.

"I pruned ahead."

Elizabeth watched him.

"That isn't an answer."

"No."

He looked down at the bag of peas in his lap as if it had become an advisory committee.

"It is the answer I use when I don't want to say the other one."

Stansbury said nothing.

Elizabeth waited.

Pathwell moved the peas from one knee to the other.

"Someone asked me for help."

"Shade?"

"No. Before Shade."

"What did they ask you to do?"

Pathwell's mouth tightened.

"Help."

Elizabeth stared at him.

"You are doing it again."

"Yes."

That stopped her for half a second.

Pathwell looked out through the windshield.

"They had a problem. A real one. They came to me because I knew things they didn't and because I had a reputation for getting unpleasant things finished."

"Did they ask you to prune?"

Pathwell went still.

"No."

The van seemed smaller.

Elizabeth looked at Stansbury.

He was looking straight ahead.

"Did they know you were going to?"

"They knew I was going to handle it."

"That is not what I asked."

"No."

Pathwell's thumb pressed a dent into the plastic bag.

"They did not ask me to prune. I did not ask whether they wanted me to."

Elizabeth felt the words arrive without any magic helping them.

"Why?"

"Because I thought I knew the cleanest way through it."

"For them."

"Yes."

"Without asking them."

"Yes."

"And the prune was yours."

Pathwell looked at her.

"Yes."

"Your future."

"Mine."

Elizabeth leaned back against the metal side of the van.

That part mattered.

Not because it made the choice better.

Because Pathwell had apparently spent himself and still somehow turned that sacrifice into permission.

"So what did pruning do?"

Pathwell took a breath.

"Nothing by itself."

Stansbury looked at him then.

Pathwell nodded once, as if agreeing to keep the explanation honest.

"I built a working around the result I wanted. The working could accept future possibility as payment. I decided that was the quickest way to settle the problem, and I paid with mine."

"Quickest."

"And cleanest, I thought."

Elizabeth almost laughed.

She didn't.

"What went wrong?"

Pathwell's attention dropped to his hands.

"The payment released. The working didn't settle cleanly."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

"You don't know, or you don't want to tell me?"

Pathwell looked up.

"I don't know. I know what malformed pruning looks like. I know what unclaimed possibility looks like afterward. I know there was more of it than there should have been. I know I failed to contain all of it."

"And then Shade."

Pathwell's expression changed.

Less defense.

More something Elizabeth did not trust herself to name.

"And then Shade," he said.

"You made him."

Pathwell did not answer quickly.

That mattered too.

"My choice produced the failure he came from," he said. "That much is mine."

"But you don't know how the failure became a person."

"No."

"Does anyone?"

"Not that I've found."

"Could you do it again?"

"I wouldn't know how."

"That wasn't the question."

Pathwell closed his eyes for a moment.

"No. I would not try."

Elizabeth watched him open them again.

"Shade remembers pieces of you."

"I know."

"Old pieces."

"Yes."

"He has a thing in his hand that keeps pointing him back at you."

"Yes."

"The blobs keep confusing the two of you."

"Apparently."

"And you still don't know exactly what he is."

Pathwell's mouth started toward an answer.

Stopped.

"No."

Elizabeth looked down at her hands.

Shade at the diner: *For him to stop deciding what I am before I get to.*

She looked back at Pathwell.

"He thinks you'll decide for him."

Pathwell shifted in the passenger seat.

"I heard him."

"That's not the same as listening."

"No."

The answer was so quiet she almost missed it.

Elizabeth let the silence stay where it was.

Then she asked, "The person who came to you. Did they ask for your future?"

Pathwell frowned.

"No."

"Did they ask you to sacrifice anything?"

"No."

"So you paid the cost yourself."

"Yes."

"And that made you feel like the method was yours to choose."

Pathwell looked at her.

He did not answer.

Elizabeth waited.

Stansbury did too.

Finally Pathwell said, "At the time, I would have called that responsibility."

"What do you call it now?"

His hand tightened around the peas.

"I don't know yet."

Elizabeth shook her head.

"No. You know enough."

Pathwell looked at her.

"Someone asked you for help."

"Yes."

"They did not ask you to prune."

"No."

"You decided pruning was the best way."

"Yes."

"You decided paying with your own future meant you could make that decision without asking them."

Pathwell's face went still.

Elizabeth held his eyes.

"And whatever happened after that became Shade."

"Yes."

"But you still chose it."

The sentence sat between them.

No magic.

No philosophy.

No explanation large enough to climb around it.

Pathwell looked down once.

Then back at her.

"Yes."

Elizabeth breathed out.

That was the answer.

Not enough to make anything better.

Enough to stop pretending the question was technical.

Stansbury loosened one hand from the wheel.

"Where now?" he asked.

Pathwell turned toward him automatically.

Elizabeth said, "Camp."

Both brothers looked at her.

She had expected Pathwell to answer first.

He didn't.

"Why Camp?" Stansbury asked.

Elizabeth looked out the side window at the paling fields.

"Because Mama Baga doesn't belong to either of them."

Pathwell's eyebrows rose.

"Neither does Camp," Elizabeth continued. "I want somewhere this isn't just Pathwell telling me what Shade is and Shade telling me what Pathwell did. I want people around who aren't inside the argument."

Stansbury nodded once.

"Camp, then."

Pathwell looked at Elizabeth.

"There isn't a reliable threshold from here."

She waited.

He caught himself.

"The nearest one I know," he said more carefully, "is at the museum. The graffiti wall. If it's still carrying enough history, it can anchor the door."

Stansbury started the van.

Elizabeth looked at him.

"You know the place?"

"Unfortunately."

"Why unfortunately?"

"Pathwell likes museums."

Pathwell looked offended.

"Museums like me."

"They do not."

The van pulled back onto the road.

Elizabeth settled against the side panel.

The question was answered.

The problem was not.

For once, those felt like different things.

"Museum," she said.

Stansbury turned east.

Pathwell did not correct the route.
