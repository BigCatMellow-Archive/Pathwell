Chapter Ten

The road had the quality of roads in early morning — not empty, but not yet claimed. The fields on either side held their dark at the edges. She walked on the shoulder, facing traffic the way she'd been taught, though there wasn't any.

The apartment was a few hours behind her, back through town, back through the accident. Her boxes were still there. It was hers on paper. Whatever meeting she'd missed this morning had already happened without her, and she found she couldn't make herself care which way it had gone. She kept walking in the direction she was already going.

The cold came through her coat in the particular way of early morning cold — not sudden, just there, the way things are there when they've always been there. Her arm hurt where Shade had held it on the road. The dagger was gone, somewhere in the wreck or the dirt, and she had not gone back for it. Her shoes were wrong for walking and she kept walking anyway.

Twice she stopped where the gravel thinned into weeds and listened for a car that did not come. Her heel had started to rub raw. The shoulder offered bottle caps, old leaves, and one glove gone stiff with weather. She could turn around at any of them. She did not, which might have been courage and might only have been the difficulty of reversing once her body had picked a direction.

It was possible to go home.

That was the part she kept stepping around. There was no wall in the road. No paper door. No Pathwell with his hand already in his coat. There was only the direction she had chosen and the direction she had not, and the old ache in her chest that wanted someone else to make one of them matter.

No one did.

Twenty minutes of gravel underfoot and then an engine slowed behind her. Stansbury's maroon Cadillac, one window gone and the front end riding lower than it had, pulled over ahead of her and stopped. It had no right to be moving.

Pathwell got out.

Across the hood, he had a bag of frozen peas on the back of his neck. He set it on the roof of the car, left it there, and had to put one hand on the hood before he walked toward her. He looked worse than she did.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey."

He waited, looking for the shape of the conversation. She didn't give him one.

"Tell me what you did," she said.

He went quiet.

"Shade said you know." She held his eyes. "I'm not asking you whether it's a good idea to tell me. I'm not asking what you think I can handle. I'm asking what you did."

Pathwell put his hands in his pockets. "Lizzy — "

"No." She looked at the road, then back at him. "You don't get to make me the part of this you explain around."

He went quiet. Then: "Okay."

She walked past him to the car.

Stansbury was somewhere between impressed and unsurprised, which seemed to be his usual territory. She opened the door and got in.

Pathwell came around and collected his bag of peas off the roof and got in the passenger side.

"Camp," he said.

"I know," Stansbury said, and pulled onto the road.


They did not get far before Elizabeth said, "No."

Stansbury kept driving. Pathwell looked out the side window.

"No?" he said.

"Not later. Not at camp. Now."

The fields moved past in the dark, fence posts appearing in the headlights and vanishing behind them. The road was straight and it went on. Pathwell said nothing and the road said nothing and the fence posts kept appearing and vanishing, and the car held the quiet with the particular patience of cars that have held worse.

Pathwell rubbed both hands over his face. "I pruned ahead."

Stansbury's hands tightened on the wheel.

"That isn't an answer," Elizabeth said.

"No." Pathwell looked down at the bag of peas in his lap as if it had become an advisory committee. "It felt important at the time, which should have been my first warning."

"Pathwell."

"It is the ugliest part of one." He let his hands fall. "I cut away possibilities I didn't intend to use. Some were mine. Some should have been mine. Some were..." He stopped.

The car kept moving. A fence post. Another.

"Him."

Pathwell did not look at her. "Enough of him to matter."

She held that.

No one moved except the car. Stansbury kept both hands on the wheel, ten and two, same as always. The bag of peas slid a little in Pathwell's lap and left a wet crescent on his coat.

Outside, the fields were changing — not lighter yet, but less dark, the way things get before dawn commits to anything. The line between field and sky was somewhere and she couldn't find it. She looked for it for a moment.

"And now he has to come back to you."

"Yes."

"Because you made him."

Pathwell's mouth opened. Closed.

"Yes," he said.

Elizabeth sat with that.

She thought about what she wanted to say next and found she didn't want to say any of it. Not because it wasn't true. Because it was all true and there was too much of it and the car was moving and the fence posts were going past and Shade was somewhere behind them doing something she didn't know about with a life that had been made for him by the man in the passenger seat who was now holding a bag of frozen peas in his lap and looking out the window.

"Camp," she said.

No one corrected her.
