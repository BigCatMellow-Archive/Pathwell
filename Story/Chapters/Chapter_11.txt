Chapter Eleven

The drive took most of what was left of the night. The museum sat at the edge of a small town, a Civil War building with a flagpole, a strip of lawn and a sign out front listing the parking lot's hours.

The lot held one dark sedan.

Elizabeth recognized it before Stansbury had finished turning the work van in. "Shade."

Pathwell had already gone still. It wasn't the draw. He knew the car too.

Stansbury drove past the empty disabled spaces by the door and on toward the far end of the lot.

"Where are you going?" Pathwell said. "Just park."

"Well, I don't want to get towed for parking in a blue spot. What if someone comes along and needs it?"

"Are you serious?"

"Look, just because we're trying to find a bad guy doesn't mean we need to be one."

Shade was standing beside his sedan with both hands in his coat pockets. The right one was making that difficult. Every few seconds the fabric shifted as the hand tried to open toward Pathwell, and Shade closed it again. He looked tired, the drive-too-long kind of tired, and it made him more plainly separate from Pathwell than the face did.

Elizabeth got out of the van. The foam dagger was still at the back of her waistband.

Shade looked at her first. "You found him."

"He found me."

Shade's eyes moved to Pathwell. "Of course he did."

"It involved grocery lists."

"That somehow makes it worse."

"And very bad coffee."

"That makes it Pathwell."

Pathwell shut the van door harder than necessary. "Good morning to you as well."

Shade's right hand opened inside his pocket, and he caught the wrist through the coat with his left. "It would be better if you stood farther away."

Pathwell stopped where he was. "You left the crash with her."

"Yes."

"You took her from me."

Shade looked at Elizabeth, then back at him. "She wasn't yours to take from."

"How did you know about the museum?" Elizabeth asked.

"I didn't know you were coming here."

"That's not what I asked."

"The pull brought me this direction. Then I recognized the building. Or he did." Shade looked toward the museum. "The memory was already there. Graffiti wall. Camp route. A cannon aimed at the gift shop for reasons nobody has corrected."

"So you guessed he'd use it."

"I guessed he might pass somewhere I already knew, while the thing in my arm kept insisting he was this way."

"And if he hadn't?"

"More driving. Worse coffee."

Stansbury came up from the far end of the lot. "The cannon is historically appropriate."

"It is aimed at the gift shop."

"History has casualties."

Pathwell started toward the entrance. Elizabeth didn't move. He made it five steps before he noticed, and stopped.

"Are you trying to get to Camp?" Elizabeth asked Shade.

"I'm trying to get somewhere this stops being the only direction my body cares about."

"That's not an answer."

Something like recognition passed over Shade's face. "Yes," he said. "Camp."

"So am I."

Pathwell turned back from the door. "That's not ideal."

Elizabeth looked at him.

He held up one hand. "I'm aware that sentence has recently performed poorly."

"Then why did you say it?"

"Muscle memory."

Shade looked at his right hand. "Careful with that excuse."

Stansbury stepped past all of them. "Can we go inside before the sun comes up and somebody has to explain why four adults are holding a philosophical dispute in a parking lot?"


Pathwell had a key.

Elizabeth stopped at the side entrance when she saw it. "You have a key to the museum."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"I support the arts."

Stansbury made a sound behind her.

Pathwell looked over his shoulder. "I do."

"He donated the climate-control system after arguing with the board for eleven years," Stansbury said.

"Nine."

"The first two years still count if nobody invited you."

Pathwell put the key in the lock. "This is why younger brothers should not be allowed to preserve history."

"I am older than you."

"Emotionally debatable."

The lock turned, and cold museum air came out around them: dust without dirt, wood polish, old fabric, paper kept out of the sun.

Elizabeth went in first. Shade followed after a pause, then Stansbury. Pathwell came in last and locked the door behind them.

The museum was dim, the sparse glow of a few security lights cutting corners off the shadows around the exhibits, and the high windows had only just started to go gray. Front and center sat a cannon, flanked by two soldiers, one in dark blue and the other in gray: two brothers split by land and by ideology. Around them were the remnants of the war, its mementos and its treasures, photographs of still-framed ghosts, letters home. Elizabeth knew plenty about the war. She had never taken the time to see what it did to people.

The cannon was pointed at the gift shop.

Elizabeth walked around it. "I see his point."

"Thank you," Shade said.

"Traitor," Pathwell said.

A display case beside the cannon held buttons: a row of mismatched brass buttons mounted on faded blue cloth, each with a tiny accession card beneath it. The cards were handwritten, narrow and slanted, in fountain pen rather than a label maker.

One card had been corrected twice.

REGIMENT UNKNOWN.

Then UNKNOWN crossed out.

Then 19TH VIRGINIA? in smaller handwriting.

Then, beneath that, in different ink:

NO. COAT FOUND IN HENRY VALE'S ATTIC, 1978. FAMILY SAYS HIS FATHER WORE IT TO HIS WEDDING AFTER THE WAR.

Elizabeth leaned closer. "That seems like important information to leave until the third try."

Pathwell stopped beside her. "Museums are arguments conducted very slowly."

"Who wrote the cards?"

"The first curator. Margaret Bell. She labeled everything herself until arthritis made her handwriting look like a seismograph."

"Did you know her?"

"She banned me twice."

The museum opened inward through a run of rooms that had once been the original building and had since acquired additions nobody had managed to hide. A soldier's poem hung in a plain frame by the artillery room door: a man looking back on the troubles he'd had as a boy and finding them small now, and wondering if he would ever again know the smell of lilac on his wife.

For several minutes they moved like ordinary museum visitors who had arrived before opening because one of them had a key and poor boundaries. Elizabeth read labels. Stansbury corrected a date under his breath and then refused to say which one. Shade recognized things in pieces and stopped himself from saying *we* when he meant Pathwell. Pathwell knew which floorboard squeaked near the artillery room and stepped around it. Shade stepped on it on purpose, and the sound cracked through the quiet. Pathwell looked back.


The graffiti wall took up the far gallery. It was a section of the original building, kept behind a low barrier, its boards crowded with names, dates, initials, crude faces, insults, arithmetic, unit numbers, towns, and one carefully, badly drawn horse. Some marks had been scratched with knives and some written in pencil. Some had been darkened by hands that traced them for generations before the museum started asking people not to. The room around it had been rebuilt twice. The wall hadn't.

Side cases stood around the gallery, the museum's best things kept close to its oldest one.

Shade had stopped at one of them. Five folded letters were arranged around a photograph of a young man who looked annoyed at the existence of photography. A typed label identified the writer as Daniel Vale, 1863–1864. Beneath it sat one original envelope, open at the top, with DAN written across it in a woman's hand.

Shade leaned closer without touching the glass. "He complained about socks."

"What?" Pathwell said.

Shade tapped one finger against his own coat pocket. "Memory. Old. I remember reading these. One of them is mostly about socks."

Pathwell looked at the case, and something in his expression shifted. "March of sixty-four."

Shade closed his eyes for half a second. "His sister kept knitting them too narrow."

"She thought his feet were smaller than they were."

"He thought this was deliberate."

Elizabeth looked from one to the other. Pathwell saw her looking.

"That one is harmless," he said.

Shade opened his eyes. "To you."

Elizabeth read the museum's summary. Daniel survived the war. His sister didn't survive the winter of 1865. The letters had come to the museum through his granddaughter, and one sentence near the bottom explained that he had kept every pair of socks.

She stood there longer than she meant to. "People are ridiculous."

"Generally," Stansbury said.

"That's the encouraging part," Pathwell said.

"How?"

"History books make everyone sound like they spent all day becoming history. Mostly they were worrying about socks."

A medical case held forceps, folded bandages browned by time, and a handwritten supply ledger that gave an entire page to missing soap.

A civilian display held a woman's household notebook with arithmetic in the margins.

FLOUR 12

LAMP OIL 8

MENDING 3

Then, halfway down a page of prices:

SAM HOME TODAY. THIN AS A FENCE RAIL. ATE THREE EGGS.

Elizabeth stopped again. "That's all?"

"All what?" Shade asked.

"The war ends and he gets three eggs."

"For her, perhaps that was the end of the war," Pathwell said.

Nobody made a joke after that.

Elizabeth stopped at the barrier. "That's the door?"

"That's the graffiti house," Pathwell said. "What's left of it. Civil War soldiers would come through and leave graffiti. Draw something silly. They were messages home, or attempts to be remembered as something beyond literal cannon fodder. These were men and boys looking for a chance not to be lost to time."

Elizabeth felt the weight of it then. It wasn't like the Space Between. Nothing pressed itself into her mind. The wall simply had weight.

Shade had come away from the cases. He looked at the wall.

"I remember this."

Pathwell stayed several yards away from him. "I gathered that."

"Not the people. The route."

"Yes."

Shade looked at Elizabeth. "You chose Camp?"

"Yes."

"Not him?"

"Camp."

Shade nodded and stepped toward the low barrier.

Pathwell moved at the same time. Elizabeth heard his shoe on the floor.

"Stop."

Both men stopped.

She looked at Pathwell first. "What are you doing?"

"Watching him."

"You moved."

"Watching can involve locomotion."

"Not today."

Stansbury leaned against the doorframe.

"I need the wall," Shade said to Elizabeth.

"To open Camp?"

"Yes."

Pathwell said, "We do not know what happens if you use it."

"You don't know what happens if I eat breakfast either."

"Breakfast has a substantial experimental record."

"For you."

Elizabeth stepped between the argument and the wall. "I came here to go to Camp."

"I know," Pathwell said.

"Then let him open it."

"Elizabeth—"

"I don't need to be rescued."

Pathwell went still.

Elizabeth held his eyes. "I'm standing here. I know who he is. I know who you are. I chose Camp. He knows the route. Let him open it."

Pathwell looked at Shade. Shade said nothing. He waited.

Pathwell looked back at Elizabeth. "All right."

For one second she believed him.

Elizabeth stepped aside to give Shade the wall. He crossed the low barrier and went toward it slowly. His left hand lifted toward a cluster of names near shoulder height. His right hand, the one the draw kept trying to turn toward Pathwell, stayed clenched at his side. He touched nothing yet.

"There should be a route mark under the—"

He leaned closer, and a board creaked under his shoe. Pathwell flinched. Shade turned his head, only his head, and his right hand snapped open toward Pathwell.

The draw caught hard enough to lift his arm and turn his body with it. He grabbed his wrist with his left hand, and the effort pulled him off balance in the narrow space behind the barrier. His boot caught the brass base, and he stumbled sideways over it, toward Elizabeth.

She put out her hand on instinct. Shade caught her forearm to keep from taking them both down.

For half a second, from Pathwell's side of the room, it was simple and ugly: Shade moving fast, Elizabeth recoiling, Shade's hand on her arm.

Shade let go at once. "Sorry."

"I'm fine," Elizabeth said. She looked at Pathwell. "He slipped."

Pathwell's hand was already inside his coat.

"No," Elizabeth said.

His hand came out holding a folded page, old paper with dark handwriting on it.

Stansbury came off the doorframe. "That's from my library. You took that while I was upstairs."

Shade recognized the posture before Elizabeth recognized the paper. "Don't."

Pathwell unfolded it. "Stay where you are."

Shade laughed once, without humor. "You first."

Elizabeth moved toward Pathwell. "Put it away."

The page was a letter, and Pathwell's fingers were already on it. The top line showed in brown ink: ELSIE—KEEP THE CHILDREN AT YOUR SISTER'S. A quarantine letter. Care that meant stay away. Elizabeth understood the shape of it too late.

"Pathwell."

"I am separating the problem."

"There is no problem. He slipped."

"He grabbed you."

"Because he was falling. I said I'm fine."

The first line of the letter began to disappear.

Stansbury crossed the room. "Cancel it."

Pathwell's eyes stayed on Shade. "I can contain this."

Elizabeth heard the work van inside the sentence. *Quickest. Cleanest. I can handle it.*

"I said no."

Pathwell didn't look at her. The page released. The handwriting vanished all at once, and the paper went white between Pathwell's fingers and tore itself apart from the fold outward.

Something hard entered the room. A transparent plane snapped into existence between Pathwell and Shade with the force of a door slammed by a giant hand. Shade was thrown backward into the low barrier. The barrier tore loose from the floor. Its brass post struck the nearest display case, and the glass burst inward.

The plane kept growing. Pathwell had meant to contain one man. The working tried to make distance physical, and it shoved chairs, signs, the broken barrier, loose wood and three hundred pounds of nineteenth-century display case away from its center.

Elizabeth, a step from the plane's edge, felt the floor move under her feet.

"Down!" Stansbury shouted.

The first case to go over was the Daniel Vale letters. The glass hit the floor. One framed page slid free, caught under the falling wooden edge, and tore cleanly through the middle. The original was destroyed before anything magical happened to it.

Then the room inhaled.

Elizabeth felt homesickness so sharp she turned toward a house that had been gone for more than a century. A kitchen door in summer. A sister furious about socks. Rain against canvas. A man's embarrassed certainty that he would make it home. They weren't memories. They were pieces flung loose without anyone shaping them.

The second case shattered. The household notebook struck the floor open, and a pane of glass cut across the binding. SAM HOME TODAY split down the middle.

Relief hit the room, huge and impossible and pointed nowhere. Someone was home. Someone wasn't dead. Someone needed eggs.

Elizabeth started crying and had no idea why.

Then the physical effect followed. Every loose object in the gallery lurched toward the doorway, as if the room had remembered people coming home and decided home must be outside. A chair crossed the floor. A label stand became a projectile. Stansbury caught Elizabeth around the waist and pulled her sideways.

The plane buckled. Pathwell lost hold of its shape. The quarantine letter was already gone, so there was nothing left to stop using. The working had discharged. Now the damage was feeding the damage.

A third display tipped against the graffiti barrier, and its wooden frame cracked. Inside it, the medical supply ledger tore from its mount, and the page about missing soap ripped across two punched holes.

Panic came into the room, a quartermaster's rather than a battlefield's: not enough bandages, not enough soap, too many names, someone asking where the clean water had gone.

The overhead lights flashed on, though nobody had touched a switch, and then off. A cabinet door slammed again and again. In the lobby, the cannon rolled six inches on a wheel that had been locked for twenty years.

"Pathwell!" Stansbury shouted.

Pathwell was on one knee, one arm across his ribs, blood running from a cut at his temple.

The pressure collapsed, not cleanly. It folded inward and threw one last shove across the gallery.

Elizabeth saw the nearest case coming. She moved, not fast enough. Stansbury hit her from the side, and the case missed her head. Its wooden corner caught her left shoulder and drove her to the floor.

The sound came first. Then nothing. Then pain so bright the room lost its edges.

Elizabeth heard herself make a noise she would later deny. Her arm stopped belonging anywhere reasonable.

Stansbury was beside her first. He put one hand behind her head and the other against her upper arm without trying to move it.

"Look at me."

She didn't want to.

"Elizabeth. Look at me."

She found his face.

"Can you breathe?"

"Yes."

"Hands numb?"

She flexed the fingers on the hurt side. Pain ran up the arm. "No."

"Good. Don't move it."

Across the gallery, Shade pushed the broken barrier off his legs. He was bleeding from one palm.

Pathwell tried to stand.

Stansbury pointed at him without looking away from Elizabeth. "Stay down."

Pathwell stayed down.

The last of the loose charge moved through the museum in dying waves: a burst of homesickness, a flash of a wet road, a woman's relief over three eggs, the smell of old soap. Then less, and then less again. The museum settled. Glass clicked onto glass. A sprinkler pipe groaned but didn't open. Somewhere in the lobby, the cannon finished another inch of its journey toward the gift shop and stopped.

Shade got to his feet. He looked at Elizabeth first, then at Pathwell, then at the broken cases.

"You said all right."

Pathwell's face had gone pale. "I know."

Shade looked at the ruined Daniel Vale letter. "Do you?"

Pathwell didn't answer.

Elizabeth pushed herself upright with Stansbury's help. The movement made the shoulder flare white.

"Don't," Stansbury said.

"Camp."

"Yes."

"Now."

Pathwell started to speak. Elizabeth turned her head toward him, and whatever he saw stopped the sentence.

Shade stepped over broken glass to the graffiti wall. Part of its frame had cracked, but the original boards were still standing, and the names were still there, hundreds of them.

Shade looked across the surface. "I remember this part."

Pathwell sat against the base of a display case with blood down one side of his face. "Third board from the left. Low."

Shade's head turned.

Pathwell caught himself. "If you want the route," he said, "that's where the mark used to be."

Shade looked at Elizabeth. She nodded.

Shade crouched. Near the bottom of the third board, beneath two carved initials and the badly drawn horse, his fingers found a shallow scratched line almost hidden in the older graffiti. He traced it once.

The room changed around the wall. It wasn't the charged pressure of the destroyed records. It was something architectural, a seam remembering it had another side. Light showed along the edge of two boards: firelight, woodsmoke, and the distant sound of a fiddle stopping halfway through a tune because someone had apparently forgotten the next part.

Camp.

Shade stood. "Open."

Elizabeth looked at the threshold, then at Pathwell. He was still on the floor. For once he didn't tell her what to do.

Stansbury kept one steadying hand at her back. "Can you walk?"

"Yes."

"You don't have to prove it."

"I'm not."

"Find the healer before you do anything else."

Elizabeth looked at Shade. "Go through first."

He did. He stepped through and turned to wait on the other side.

Elizabeth moved toward the wall. Each step jarred her shoulder. She stopped once, breathed, and kept going.

At the threshold she looked back: broken glass, accession cards scattered across the floor, and Pathwell sitting in the middle of it.

"I chose Camp," she said.

Pathwell swallowed. "I know."

"I'm still choosing it."

Then she stepped through, and the wall closed behind her.


The gallery went quiet in the ordinary way after that.

Stansbury stood for several seconds facing the graffiti wall. Then he turned to Pathwell. "Can you stand?"

"Probably."

"That wasn't confidence-inspiring."

"I am adjusting my brand."

Through the gallery door, Stansbury could see the soldier's poem by the artillery room, still in its frame. He looked at it for a moment. Then he went to the museum office and came back with a green first-aid box, a roll of gauze, antiseptic wipes, and a cold pack that took both hands and an insult to activate. He tossed the cold pack to Pathwell.

"Ordinary medicine?"

"You have lost museum privileges for the morning."

"Only the morning?"

Stansbury knelt and cleaned the cut at Pathwell's temple. Pathwell hissed.

"Hold still."

"You are enjoying this."

"I was. Then I looked at the cases."

Pathwell followed his gaze: the Daniel Vale letters, the household notebook, the medical ledger. Things that had survived war, attics, grandchildren, bad storage, moving trucks, committees, and Pathwell's own repeated presence in the building. Until now.

"I can explain what happened," Pathwell said.

Stansbury taped gauze against his temple. "That is not the same as fixing it."

Stansbury sat back on his heels. "And before you offer your future, your coat, your expertise, or a charmingly illegal replacement scheme: no."

Pathwell looked toward the torn notebook. "The information can be copied from the museum scans."

"The information."

Pathwell closed his eyes.

Stansbury stood. "We are going to secure the room. Then we are going to call the curator. Then you are going to tell her exactly what you did."

Pathwell opened his eyes. "That will be unpleasant."

"Good. After that, we go to Camp."

Pathwell pressed the cold pack against his ribs. "Elizabeth is hurt."

"I noticed."

"I did that."

Stansbury looked at him for a long moment. "Yes."

Outside the high windows, it was daylight.
