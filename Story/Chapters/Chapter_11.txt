Chapter Eleven

The museum was a civil war building on the edge of a town that had grown up around it and then quietly moved on. The sign out front was for the parking lot hours. The lot itself was empty.

Shade's car was already there, half on the curb, doors hanging open.

Stansbury pulled in and turned off the engine. The three of them sat for a moment looking at the open doors.

"He beat us," Stansbury said.

"I can see that," Pathwell said.

Stansbury parked properly, took his time, and got out. Pathwell was already at the building's front entrance.

The locks were open. The glass door was propped against the frame by something small and deliberate — a folded note card. The lobby inside was dark, with the hush of rooms full of things behind glass. A cannon pointed at the front door. Two soldiers flanked it, one in blue, one gray.

The museum had the smell of a place that considers itself important. Elizabeth's footsteps were louder than she expected and she slowed down before she realized she was doing it.

The kind of place that had made its peace with the past. The past had not entirely returned the courtesy.

She was in the back.

Pathwell moved through the exhibits without turning on the lights. Lit rooms made you visible. He ducked between display cases and tried not to knock anything off its stand.

Elizabeth passed a case of buttons — uniform buttons, sorted by regiment, labeled in handwriting that had been doing this long before the museum was famous for anything. She kept moving.

"This is what you brought me all the way out here for. A hut."

He stopped where he was.

"Not just a hut." A pause that felt theatrical. "The graffiti house."

He leaned around the display case he'd been hiding behind.

On the far wall of a side gallery, barely visible in the ambient light from the exit signs, were the words. Hundreds of them, scratched into wood paneling — names and dates and small drawings, the lines crowded together where the writer had run out of room and kept going anyway. The wall breathed with it.

The person talking about the wall was Pathwell.

His double was talking. Pathwell's head tilted.

Same coat. Same hands.

The gesture was wrong.

Pathwell had made it ten thousand times. From outside, it was like reading your own handwriting in a letter you didn't remember sending — the shapes right, the rhythm off, something that could only be wrong because the real version lived somewhere below thought. The gesture finished and left him with nothing he had a name for.

He ducked back behind the case.

Stansbury arrived at his elbow. Pathwell pointed, silently.

Stansbury looked. "Ah," he said.

"Quiet," Pathwell breathed.

"Ah," Stansbury said again, considerably louder.


"Pathwell." The other voice — his voice — came from the gallery, calm and unhurried. "You can come out. I know where you are."

Pathwell stepped into the light. He had the look of a man who had been walking toward this conversation for a long time.

Elizabeth stood near the graffiti wall, arms crossed. "Hey," she said.

"Are you okay?"

"I'm fine."

"We came to — "

"I know why you came." She looked at him steadily. "I don't need to be rescued."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. "What?"

Shade stepped out of the shadows on the other side of the gallery. The resemblance at close range was worse.

"She means it," Shade said. "She came in here on her own."

"I know who you are," Pathwell said. "You're the one who took her from the road."

"I am."

"You're wearing my coat wrong."

"Yes," Shade said. "That keeps coming up."

"Also you took her from me."

Shade tilted his head. "She wasn't yours to take from."

The gallery went quiet. Elizabeth stood between them and did not move.

"You know what I want," Shade said.

"Tell me anyway."

"What you owe me." His voice stayed level. The corner of the paper folded under his thumb. "You know."

"No," Pathwell said.

Shade smiled with Pathwell's mouth, and it fit badly. "There he is."

Pathwell's hands were at his sides. He had been counting what was in his coat since they walked in and was fairly certain it wasn't enough.

"You pruned ahead," Shade said. "You looked away before it stood up."

The word came out of Pathwell's mouth before he'd decided to say it. "I didn't think it would — "

"I know you didn't."

The graffiti wall breathed behind them. Names and dates and messages — written in the dark, still there.

Shade took one step toward Elizabeth.

One careful step. Palm open. Apology built into the angle of his wrist.

Pathwell reached into his coat.

He'd told himself he wasn't going to. His hands hadn't gotten the message.

The paper came out and flattened between them, a ward spreading too quickly, too wide, trying to make a rule for the whole room at once. It caught the wall behind Elizabeth. It caught the glass cases. It caught every charged scrap in the gallery and pulled them tight.

The overload went inward.

For half a second nothing moved. Then the room clenched.

Elizabeth hit the display case sideways. The glass broke around her. Her shoulder struck the floor at an angle that wasn't right. The impact arrived as sound before pain — something that made the room go white at the edges. She stayed down. The pain arrived a second later and did not ask whether she was ready.

Pathwell had half a breath before the recoil folded through him and took his legs out.

The room was very still.

Stansbury was the first one upright. Pathwell on the floor. The display case. Elizabeth beside it, still.

"For the record," Stansbury said, "I hate you both."

He went to Elizabeth first.

She was sitting up by the time he reached her, bracing herself on one hand, her other arm held close. Across the wreckage, Pathwell lay on his back with one arm raised.

"I'm fine," he said, to the ceiling.

"You're not," she said.

Shade was at the wall. He moved along the graffiti, and when he reached her he held out a hand.

Her fingers twitched once.

"Camp Cunnan," he said. "Mama Baga will look at the arm." He paused. "He'll find you when he's ready."

Her shoulder throbbed once, hard enough to put the room out of order.

She took his hand and got up.

"Elizabeth," Pathwell said.

She turned.

His mouth opened.

Closed.

"Get some rest," she said. "I'll see you at camp."

Shade held out a folded sheet of paper and she pressed it against the graffiti wall. The wall opened — not because it wanted to, but because the paper told it to — all at once, with conviction. Light through the gap, and the sound of a fire somewhere.

They went through.

The wall closed behind them.


Stansbury found a poem in the civil war exhibit — a soldier writing home, the smell of a field in summer, the hope of making it back. He pressed it to Pathwell's chest with one hand on his forehead and said a word, and the sheet did what Stansbury's things did when he put them to work.

Pathwell lay there while it ran its course.

When it was done, Stansbury sat on the floor beside him. The gallery was a wreck. The cannon was still pointed at the front door.

"What have you gone and done," Stansbury said. Not a question.

"I don't know," Pathwell said.

"Little brother." He hadn't said it in a long time. He rested his elbows on his knees and looked at the graffiti wall, which was just a wall again. "Little brother, you are going to have to figure out what you owe her."

Pathwell counted ceiling tiles. There were fewer than the moment required.

"The truth," Stansbury said. "And the rest of it."

He didn't answer.
