Chapter Eleven

The museum was a Civil War building on the edge of a town that had grown around it, outgrown it, and then left it behind with excellent signage.

The sign out front listed parking lot hours in three fonts.

The lot itself contained one dark sedan.

Elizabeth recognized it before Stansbury finished turning the work van into the entrance.

"Shade."

Pathwell had already gone still.

Not because the draw moved him.

Because he knew the car too.

Stansbury pulled into a space, centered the van between the lines, backed out, corrected by four inches, and parked again.

Elizabeth watched him.

"Really?"

"I have suffered one vehicular loss tonight. I am restoring standards."

Pathwell opened his door.

"Your standards are why we will die in parking lots."

"My standards are why the surviving vehicle is between the lines."

Shade was standing beside his sedan.

He had both hands in his coat pockets.

The right one was making that arrangement difficult.

Every few seconds the fabric shifted as his hand tried to open toward Pathwell and Shade closed it again.

He looked tired.

Not dramatically tired.

Coffee tired.

Drive-too-long tired.

The kind that made him more obviously separate from Pathwell than the face did.

Elizabeth got out of the van.

Shade looked at her first.

"You found him."

"He found me."

Shade's eyes moved to Pathwell.

"Of course he did."

"It involved grocery lists."

Shade looked back at her.

"That somehow makes it worse."

"It involved very bad coffee too."

"That makes it Pathwell."

Pathwell shut the van door harder than necessary.

"Good morning to you as well."

Shade's right hand opened inside his pocket.

He caught his wrist through the coat with his left hand.

"It would be better if you stood farther away."

Pathwell stopped where he was.

For the moment.

Elizabeth looked at Shade's car.

"How did you know about the museum?"

"I didn't know you were coming here."

"That's not what I asked."

Shade nodded once.

"The pull brought me this direction. Then I recognized the building. Or he did."

He looked toward the museum.

"The memory was already there. Graffiti wall. Camp route. A cannon pointed at the front door for reasons no one has corrected."

"So you guessed he'd use it."

"I guessed he might pass somewhere I already knew while the thing in my arm kept insisting he was this way."

"And if we hadn't?"

Shade looked at his sedan.

"More driving. Worse coffee."

Stansbury got out.

"The cannon is historically appropriate."

Shade looked at him.

"It is aimed at the gift shop."

"History has casualties."

Pathwell walked toward the entrance.

Elizabeth did not move.

He made it five steps before noticing.

Again.

He stopped.

Elizabeth looked at Shade.

"Are you trying to get to Camp?"

Shade took his right hand out of his pocket.

It opened immediately toward Pathwell.

He closed it with visible effort.

"I'm trying to get somewhere this stops being the only direction my body cares about."

"That's not an answer."

A small expression passed over Shade's face.

Recognition, maybe.

"Yes," he said. "Camp."

Elizabeth nodded.

"So am I."

Pathwell turned back from the door.

"That is not ideal."

Elizabeth looked at him.

He held up one hand.

"I am aware that sentence has recently performed poorly."

"Then why did you say it?"

"Muscle memory."

Shade looked at his right hand.

"Careful with that excuse."

Pathwell's mouth tightened.

Stansbury stepped past both of them.

"Can we enter the building before the sun comes up and somebody has to explain why four adults are holding a philosophical dispute next to a bronze cannon?"

"Three adults," Pathwell said.

Stansbury looked back.

"Do not make me decide which one of you I meant."


Pathwell had a key.

Elizabeth stopped at the side entrance when she saw it.

"You have a key to the museum."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"I support the arts."

Stansbury made a sound behind her.

Pathwell looked over his shoulder.

"I do."

"He donated the climate-control system after arguing with the board for eleven years," Stansbury said.

"Nine."

"The first two years still count if nobody invited you."

Pathwell put the key in the lock.

"This is why younger brothers should not be allowed to preserve history."

"I am older than you."

"Emotionally debatable."

The lock turned.

Pathwell opened the door.

Cold museum air moved out around them.

Dust without dirt.

Wood polish.

Old fabric.

Paper kept away from sunlight.

The particular dry smell of things whose survival depended on people remembering to care about humidity.

Pathwell held the door.

Elizabeth stepped inside first because she had chosen Camp and the route belonged to that choice.

Shade followed after a pause.

Stansbury came next.

Pathwell entered last and locked the door behind them.

The lobby lights were off, but morning had begun pressing a weak gray through the high windows.

The cannon was exactly where Shade remembered it.

Pointed toward the gift shop.

Elizabeth walked around it.

"I see his point."

"Thank you," Shade said.

"Traitor," Pathwell said.

A display case beside the cannon held ordinary things.

That was what made Elizabeth stop.

Not a sword.

Not a bullet.

Buttons.

A row of mismatched brass buttons mounted on faded blue cloth, each with a tiny accession card beneath it. The handwriting on the cards was narrow and slanted, done in fountain pen rather than printed by a label maker.

One card had been corrected twice.

REGIMENT UNKNOWN.

Then UNKNOWN crossed out.

Then 19TH VIRGINIA? in smaller handwriting.

Then, beneath that, in different ink:

NO. COAT FOUND IN HENRY VALE'S ATTIC, 1978. FAMILY SAYS HIS FATHER WORE IT TO HIS WEDDING AFTER THE WAR.

Elizabeth leaned closer.

"That seems like important information to leave until the third try."

Pathwell stopped beside her.

"Museums are arguments conducted very slowly."

"Who wrote the cards?"

"First curator. Margaret Bell. She labeled everything herself until arthritis made her handwriting look like a seismograph."

Elizabeth looked at the last card.

"Did you know her?"

"She banned me twice."

Stansbury passed behind them.

"Three times."

"The third was personal."

Shade had stopped at a different case.

Letters.

Not grand ones.

Five folded sheets arranged around a photograph of a young man who looked annoyed at the existence of photography.

A typed museum label identified the writer as Daniel Vale, 1863-1864.

Beneath the typed label sat one original envelope, open at the top, with DAN written across it in a woman's hand.

Shade leaned closer without touching the glass.

"He complained about socks."

Pathwell looked over.

"What?"

Shade tapped one finger against his own coat pocket.

"Memory. Old. I remember reading these. One of them is mostly about socks."

Pathwell looked at the case.

Something in his expression shifted.

"March of sixty-four."

Shade closed his eyes for half a second.

"His sister kept knitting them too narrow."

"She thought his feet were smaller than they were."

"He thought this was deliberate."

Elizabeth looked from one to the other.

Same memory.

Different men standing on either side of glass.

Pathwell saw her looking.

"That one is harmless."

Shade opened his eyes.

"To you."

Pathwell did not answer.

Elizabeth read the museum summary.

Daniel survived the war.

His sister did not survive the winter of 1865.

The letters had come to the museum through his granddaughter.

One sentence near the bottom explained that he kept every pair of socks.

Elizabeth stood there longer than she intended.

"People are ridiculous," she said.

"Generally," Stansbury said.

"That's the encouraging part," Pathwell said.

Elizabeth looked at him.

"How?"

"History books make everyone sound like they spent all day becoming history. Mostly they were worrying about socks."

That was irritatingly good.

She kept walking.


The museum opened inward through a sequence of rooms that had once been part of the original building and then acquired additions nobody had successfully hidden.

A medical case contained forceps, folded bandages browned by time, and a handwritten supply ledger that devoted an entire page to missing soap.

A civilian display held a woman's household notebook with arithmetic in the margins.

FLOUR 12

LAMP OIL 8

MENDING 3

Then, halfway down a page of prices:

SAM HOME TODAY. THIN AS A FENCE RAIL. ATE THREE EGGS.

Elizabeth stopped again.

"That's all?"

"All what?" Shade asked.

"The war ends and he gets three eggs."

Pathwell looked at the notebook through the glass.

"For her, perhaps that was the end of the war."

Nobody made a joke after that.

The gray morning strengthened in the windows.

For several minutes they moved like ordinary museum visitors who had arrived before opening because one of them had a key and poor boundaries.

Elizabeth read labels.

Stansbury corrected one date under his breath and then refused to say which.

Shade recognized things in fragments and stopped himself from saying we when he meant Pathwell.

Pathwell knew which floorboard squeaked near the artillery room.

He avoided it.

Shade stepped on it deliberately.

The sound cracked through the quiet.

Pathwell looked back.

Shade's mouth moved at one corner.

His own smile.

Small.

Elizabeth saw it and stored it without deciding to.


The graffiti wall occupied the far gallery.

Calling it a wall was technically correct in the way calling the ocean damp was technically correct.

It was a section of the original building preserved behind a low barrier, boards crowded with names, dates, initials, crude faces, insults, declarations, arithmetic, unit numbers, towns, and one carefully drawn horse with the proportions of an unsuccessful dog.

The marks overlapped.

Some had been scratched with knives.

Some written in pencil.

Some darkened by hands that had traced them for generations before the museum began asking people not to.

The room around it had been rebuilt twice.

The wall had not.

Elizabeth felt it before Pathwell said anything.

Not like the Space Between.

Nothing pressed itself into her mind.

The wall simply had weight.

Human weight.

Hundreds of people insisting, in one form or another, that they had been there.

Shade stood at the gallery entrance.

His right hand opened toward Pathwell.

He shut it again.

Then he looked at the wall.

"I remember this."

Pathwell stayed several yards away from him.

"I gathered that."

"Not the people. The route."

"Yes."

Shade looked at Elizabeth.

"You chose Camp?"

"Yes."

"Not him?"

"Camp."

Shade nodded.

That answer apparently satisfied something.

He stepped toward the low barrier.

Pathwell moved at the same time.

Elizabeth heard his shoe against the floor.

"Stop."

Both men stopped.

She looked at Pathwell first.

"What are you doing?"

"Watching him."

"You moved."

"Watching can involve locomotion."

"Not today."

Stansbury leaned against the doorframe.

"I support this policy."

Pathwell ignored him.

Shade looked at Elizabeth.

"I need the wall."

"To open Camp?"

"Yes."

Pathwell said, "We do not know what happens if you use it."

Shade looked at him.

"You don't know what happens if I eat breakfast either."

"Breakfast has a substantial experimental record."

"For you."

Pathwell's jaw tightened.

Elizabeth stepped between the argument and the wall.

"I came here to go to Camp."

"I know," Pathwell said.

"Then let him open it."

"Elizabeth—"

"I don't need to be rescued."

The sentence changed the room.

Not magically.

More effectively.

Pathwell went still.

Elizabeth held his eyes.

"I am standing here. I know who he is. I know who you are. I chose Camp. He knows the route. Let him open it."

Pathwell looked at Shade.

Shade did not smile.

Did not provoke him.

He waited.

Pathwell looked back at Elizabeth.

"All right."

For one second she believed him.

Elizabeth stepped aside enough to give Shade the wall.

He crossed the low barrier and approached it slowly.

His left hand lifted toward a cluster of names near shoulder height.

His right hand, the one the draw kept trying to turn toward Pathwell, stayed clenched at his side.

He touched nothing yet.

"There should be a route mark under the—"

He leaned closer.

A board creaked beneath his shoe.

Pathwell flinched.

Shade turned his head.

Only his head.

His right hand snapped open.

Toward Pathwell.

The draw caught hard enough that Shade's arm lifted and his torso turned with it.

He grabbed his wrist with his left hand.

The effort pulled his balance off the narrow space behind the barrier.

His boot caught the brass base.

Shade stumbled sideways over it.

Toward Elizabeth.

She put out her good hand on instinct.

Shade caught her forearm to keep from taking both of them down.

For half a second the scene was simple and ugly from Pathwell's side of the room: Shade moving fast, Elizabeth recoiling, Shade's hand on her arm.

Then Shade let go immediately.

"Sorry."

"I'm fine," Elizabeth said.

She looked at Pathwell.

"He slipped."

Pathwell's hand was already inside his coat.

"No," Elizabeth said.

His hand came out holding a folded page.

Old paper.

Dark handwriting.

Stansbury straightened.

"Pathwell."

Shade's face changed.

He recognized the posture before Elizabeth recognized the paper.

"Don't."

Pathwell unfolded it.

"Stay where you are."

Shade laughed once without humor.

"You first."

Elizabeth moved toward Pathwell.

"Put it away."

The page was a letter.

Pathwell's fingers were already on the original.

The top line showed through in brown ink.

ELSIE—KEEP THE CHILDREN AT YOUR SISTER'S.

A quarantine letter.

Protection by distance.

Care expressed as stay away.

Elizabeth understood the shape of it too late.

"Pathwell."

"I am separating the problem."

"There is no problem. He slipped."

"He grabbed you."

"Because he was falling. I said I'm fine."

The first line of the letter began to disappear.

Stansbury crossed the room.

"Cancel it."

Pathwell's eyes stayed on Shade.

"I can contain this."

The answer from the work van came back to Elizabeth inside the sentence.

Quickest.

Cleanest.

I can handle it.

"I said no."

The page released.

The handwriting vanished all at once.

The paper went white between Pathwell's fingers and then tore itself apart from the fold outward.

Something hard entered the room.

Not light.

Pressure.

A transparent plane snapped into existence between Pathwell and Shade with the force of a door slammed by a giant hand.

Shade was thrown backward into the low barrier.

The barrier tore loose from the floor.

Its brass post struck the nearest display case.

Glass burst inward.

The pressure plane expanded.

Pathwell had meant to contain one man.

The working tried to make distance physical.

It shoved chairs, signage, the broken barrier, loose wood, and three hundred pounds of nineteenth-century display case away from its center.

Elizabeth felt the floor move under her feet.

"Down!" Stansbury shouted.

The first case went over.

It was the Daniel Vale letters.

The glass hit the floor.

One framed page slid free, caught under the falling wooden edge, and tore cleanly through the middle.

The original was destroyed before anything magical happened to it.

Then the room inhaled.

Elizabeth felt homesickness so sharp she turned toward a house that had been gone for more than a century.

A porch in summer.

A sister furious about socks.

Rain against canvas.

A man's embarrassed certainty that he would make it home.

The impressions were not memories.

They were pieces flung loose without anyone shaping them.

The second case shattered.

The household notebook struck the floor open.

A pane of glass cut across the binding.

SAM HOME TODAY split down the center.

Warmth hit the room.

Not heat.

Relief.

Huge and impossible and directionless.

Someone was home.

Someone was not dead.

Someone needed eggs.

Elizabeth started crying and had no idea why.

Then the physical effect followed.

Every loose object in the gallery lurched toward the doorway as if the room itself had remembered people coming home and decided outside was where home must be.

A chair crossed the floor.

A label stand became a projectile.

Stansbury caught Elizabeth around the waist and pulled her sideways.

The pressure plane buckled.

Pathwell lost control of its shape.

The quarantine letter was already gone.

There was nothing to stop using.

The working had discharged.

Now the damage was feeding damage.

A third display tipped against the graffiti barrier.

The wooden frame cracked.

Inside it, the medical supply ledger tore from its mount.

The page about missing soap ripped across two punched holes.

Panic entered the room.

Not dramatic battlefield panic.

Administrative panic.

Not enough bandages.

Not enough soap.

Too many names.

Someone asking where the clean water had gone.

The overhead lights flashed on though nobody had touched a switch.

Then off.

A cabinet door slammed again and again.

The cannon in the lobby rolled six inches on a wheel that had been locked for twenty years.

"Pathwell!" Stansbury shouted.

Pathwell was on one knee.

Blood ran from a cut at his temple.

He looked at the room as though seeing the difference between a plan and its consequences for the first time in real time.

The pressure field collapsed.

Not cleanly.

It folded inward and threw one final shove across the gallery.

Elizabeth saw the display case coming.

She moved.

Not fast enough.

Stansbury hit her from the side.

The case missed her head.

Its wooden corner caught her left shoulder and drove her to the floor.

The sound came first.

Then nothing.

Then pain so bright the room lost its edges.

Elizabeth heard herself make a noise she would later deny.

Her arm stopped belonging anywhere reasonable.

Stansbury was beside her immediately.

Not Pathwell.

Not Shade.

Stansbury.

He put one hand behind her head and the other against her upper arm without trying to move it.

"Look at me."

She did not want to.

"Elizabeth. Look at me."

She found his face.

"Can you breathe?"

"Yes."

"Hands numb?"

She flexed the fingers on the injured side.

Pain ran up the arm.

"No."

"Good. Don't move it."

Across the gallery, Shade pushed the broken barrier off his legs.

He was bleeding from one palm.

His right hand opened toward Pathwell even while he was on the floor.

Shade closed it against his chest.

Pathwell tried to stand.

Stansbury pointed at him without looking away from Elizabeth.

"Stay down."

Pathwell stopped.

Possibly because Stansbury's tone had finally discovered a frequency capable of overriding him.

Elizabeth breathed through her teeth.

The uncontrolled charge moved through the museum in dying waves.

A burst of homesickness.

A flash of a wet road.

A woman's relief over three eggs.

The smell of old soap.

Then less.

Then less again.

The museum settled.

Glass clicked onto glass.

A sprinkler pipe groaned but did not open.

Somewhere in the lobby, the cannon completed another inch of its journey toward the gift shop and stopped.

No one commented.

For a while.

Shade got to his feet.

He looked first at Elizabeth.

Then at Pathwell.

Then at the broken cases.

"You said all right."

Pathwell's face had gone pale.

"I know."

Shade looked at the ruined Daniel Vale letter.

"Do you?"

Pathwell did not answer.

Elizabeth pushed herself upright with Stansbury's help.

The movement made the shoulder flare white.

"Don't," Stansbury said.

"Camp."

"Yes."

"Now."

Pathwell started to speak.

Elizabeth turned her head toward him.

Whatever he saw stopped the sentence.

Shade stepped over broken glass toward the graffiti wall.

A section of its surrounding frame had cracked, but the original boards were still standing.

The names remained.

Hundreds of them.

Still there.

Shade looked across the surface.

"I remember this part."

Pathwell sat against the base of a display case with blood on one side of his face.

"Third board from the left. Low."

Shade's head turned.

Pathwell caught himself.

"If you want the route," he said, "that's where the mark used to be."

Shade looked at Elizabeth.

She nodded.

His choice next.

Shade crouched.

Near the bottom of the third board, beneath two carved initials and a badly proportioned horse, his fingers found a shallow scratched line almost hidden in the older graffiti.

He traced it once.

The room changed around the wall.

Not the charged pressure from the destroyed records.

Something architectural.

A seam remembering it had another side.

Light appeared along the edge of two boards.

Warm firelight.

Woodsmoke.

The distant sound of a fiddle stopping halfway through a tune because someone had apparently forgotten the next part.

Camp.

Shade stood.

"Open."

Elizabeth looked at the threshold.

Then at Pathwell.

He was still on the floor.

For once he did not tell her what to do.

Stansbury kept one steadying hand at her back.

"Can you walk?"

"Yes."

"You don't have to prove it."

"I'm not."

Elizabeth looked at Shade.

"Go through first."

He did.

No argument.

No theatrical offer of his hand.

He stepped through and turned on the other side.

Elizabeth moved toward the wall.

Each step jarred her shoulder.

She stopped once.

Breathed.

Kept going.

At the threshold she looked back at the museum.

Broken glass.

The ripped household notebook.

The Daniel Vale letter in two pieces.

Accession cards scattered across the floor.

The cannon still aimed badly.

Pathwell sitting among the consequences of protecting her after she told him not to.

Elizabeth looked at him.

"I chose Camp."

Pathwell swallowed.

"I know."

"I'm still choosing it."

Then she stepped through.

The wall closed behind her.


The gallery became quiet in the ordinary way after that.

Which was worse.

Stansbury stood for several seconds facing the graffiti wall.

Then he turned to Pathwell.

"Can you stand?"

"Probably."

"That wasn't confidence-inspiring."

"I am adjusting my brand."

Stansbury did not smile.

He went to the museum office and came back with a green first-aid box, a roll of gauze, antiseptic wipes, and a cold pack that required both hands and an insult to activate.

He tossed the cold pack to Pathwell.

"Ordinary medicine?"

"You have lost museum privileges for the morning."

"Only the morning?"

Stansbury knelt and cleaned the cut at Pathwell's temple.

Pathwell hissed.

"Hold still."

"You are enjoying this."

"I was. Then I looked at the cases."

Pathwell followed his gaze.

The Daniel Vale letters.

The household notebook.

The medical ledger.

Things that had survived war, attics, grandchildren, bad storage, moving trucks, committees, and Pathwell's own repeated presence in the building.

Until now.

"I can explain what happened," Pathwell said.

Stansbury taped gauze against his temple.

"That is not the same as fixing it."

Pathwell looked at him.

Stansbury sat back on his heels.

"And before you offer your future, your coat, your expertise, or a charmingly illegal replacement scheme: no."

Pathwell looked toward the torn notebook.

"The information can be copied from the museum scans."

"The information."

Pathwell closed his eyes.

"Yes."

Stansbury stood.

"We are going to secure the room. Then we are going to call the curator. Then you are going to tell her exactly what you did."

Pathwell opened his eyes.

"That will be unpleasant."

"Good."

Stansbury looked toward the graffiti wall.

"After that, we go to Camp."

Pathwell pressed the cold pack to his ribs.

"Elizabeth is hurt."

"I noticed."

"I did that."

Stansbury looked at him for a long moment.

"Yes."

No softening.

No correction.

Outside the high windows, morning finally committed to daylight.
