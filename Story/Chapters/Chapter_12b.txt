Chapter Twelve, Part Two

Pathwell arrived at first light, with Stansbury behind him.

He came in the way he always came in: owning the place before the place had been consulted. Then Shade was there, and he went still.

Mama Baga stayed on her steps. One fiddle note died wrong.

Pathwell walked toward Shade. Elizabeth stayed where she was.

Shade didn't step back. He stood at the edge of the firelight and waited.

His right hand opened.

Small. Palm up, fingers loose, the same empty answer he'd made in Mama Baga's wagon. This time it pointed toward Pathwell.

Mama Baga's bracelets went still. The healer stopped beside the cot. Half the people who had just decided, cautiously, that he might be useful went quiet.

Shade closed the hand into a fist.

"You took her," Pathwell said. "From the road. From the crash."

"Yes."

"I'm going to end this."

"I know. You've been building to it since the bar."

Pathwell's hands went into his coat. He found the text he'd been carrying since the Space Between — a letter, unopened, taken as payment once and never spent.

The cut took shape in his hands. The letter warmed.

He held the cut.

Shade's face found the moment before the rest of him did.

Relief, unfortunately.

Pathwell held the cut and could not close his hand.

Something moved at the edge of the firelight.

The child. The one who had been awake before dawn, running errands between the wagons. Standing at the edge of the scene with one sock falling down.

Pathwell's hand shook.

He turned the tool.

Not the cut — the opposite. The letter went hot in his hand. The bar, the road, the crash, every time his hands had reached for something too late.

It tore.

The sound was not a sound. A crack that went through everyone in the camp at once, the way cold comes in under a door. The fire went sideways. Mama Baga gripped the railing of her steps. The child sat down in the dirt.

Shade staggered.

Pathwell went down to one knee, the letter burning out in his hands, coat smoking at the hem.


The archive caught from the tear.

Not immediately — a moment passed, a held breath, and then the heat found the archive wagon and what was inside it, all those sheets of paper that had been sitting there loaded for decades, and the threshold between ignition and fire was very short.

The archivist came to the door of the archive wagon with alarm on his face — smoke at the eaves, Pathwell down on one knee, the camp already moving around him.

Dawn had barely started. The archivist had not opened yet.

The camp moved. Mama Baga was shouting, people answering — away from the archive, to the wagons nearest it, a chain forming.

The tear spat something low across the dirt.

Not a blob. Not yet. A slick, black at the edges, rolling toward the closest fire and the child standing beside it.

Elizabeth's hand went into her bag.

The diary came out bent at the corners, charred along the missing page from the apartment, thick with pages she had pressed flat and shut away. She opened it.

The first page was from the week after the funeral. Three lines about buying trash bags. A fourth line pressed so hard through the paper that the next page carried the bruise.

She knew what it was. Not an object — a tether. The thing she had carried because putting it down meant the rest of it was real. Meant the week after the funeral and the trash bags and the flat she'd taken across the city had actually happened in the order they'd happened, and she had lived through all of it by keeping one hand on this and calling that enough.

The slick moved toward the child.

She thought about putting it back.

The child would have been fine. The healer was nearby. Mama Baga was on her steps. There were people at this camp who had been handling this kind of thing since before Elizabeth had known it existed.

She was not the only option.

Elizabeth put the diary between them.

The page caught.

Not with ordinary fire. The words lifted first, black ink peeling up into the air like gnats, and then the heat came through her hands. Crossed-out lines. Lists that had gone nowhere. Sentences dug so hard into the page they had bruised it. Three weeks of bare walls and boxes she'd never opened. A life she'd carried in her hands all the way here.

She shoved the open book down into the slick at the child's feet.

The slick recoiled.

The diary burned.

The child came to its feet and ran.

Toward the archive wagon.

Elizabeth ran.

A child was a child. She reached the archive door the same moment the child did, grabbed them by the arm and pulled. The child pulled back the way children pull back, and Elizabeth sat down in the dirt with the child in her arms and held on while the wagon burned.

The diary was behind her, burning down to its spine.

She held the child.

Stansbury was at the archive door.

He'd gotten there while Elizabeth was pulling the child out, moving with deliberate calm. He went in.

He came back out with two bundles of documents from the front shelf, coughing, and Pathwell was there to grab them from him. Stansbury went back in.

The doorway had changed since the first time. Lower, hotter, the paper on the nearest shelf already curling black at the edges before the flame reached it. He went in anyway, the way a man steps off a curb without checking — not brave, just already decided.

He came out the second time slower, and he was burning at the sleeve and along the back of one hand, and Pathwell caught him at the door frame and held him up while the camp moved in around them with water and cloth, and the healer came at a run.

The burn on Stansbury's hand was already blistering.

He reached for his coat. His hands found the inside pocket, found nothing useful, found the next pocket, found nothing useful. He stood there holding his brother's arm with his pockets empty.

"It's fine," Stansbury said.

"I can — "

"It's fine." He pulled his arm back gently. "Let it be."

The archive collapsed inward.

Stansbury didn't stop moving. Burned hand cradled against his chest, he was already pointing — that wagon, water there, get the mother and the boy back from the smoke — and camp people who had spent the last several minutes reacting started, for the first time that morning, doing what he told them. Nobody asked why they were listening to him. They just were.

The blobs came.

The largest was the size of a wagon. It crossed the clearing toward the fire, toward the diary burning down behind Elizabeth, toward the child still clinging to her coat.

Pathwell pushed himself up on one hand.

The blob turned.

Not toward Elizabeth. Not toward the child. Toward him.

It kept coming.

Pathwell stared at it, his coat hanging open, one hand still empty in the wrong pocket.

"Oh," he said.

Shade stepped between them.

"Shade," Pathwell said.

"I know." He looked once toward Elizabeth and the child. "Already."

He walked into the blob's path with the slight forward lean of someone running slightly late for something they'd already decided to do. The draw went with him, the tether between him and Pathwell pulling taut and then going slack.

Eye contact with Pathwell, for a moment.

The draw went quiet. Not violently. The way a bell goes quiet when you put your hand on it.

Then Shade was gone.

The blob dispersed, patient and unhurried, into the open spaces of the camp, absorbed by the ground and the morning air.

The archive was ash. The fire was done.


Mama Baga came down her steps. The archive was a black frame and smoke. Pathwell was still in the dirt. She went back up her steps.

Pathwell sat in the dirt for a long time.

Stansbury came and sat beside him, his burned hand wrapped, awake past usefulness. He didn't say anything. Elizabeth came back across the camp, arm in the sling, the child having found something more interesting than adults and detached from the scene entirely.

She sat near Pathwell. She didn't speak either.

The camp made its own sounds. Someone was crying. Someone else was being practical.

Stansbury lifted his wrapped hand. "You owe the camp for the archive."

"I know."

"And Elizabeth." He said it without looking at either of them. "You owe her for the rest."

Pathwell turned to her.

She didn't fill the silence for him.

The diary was ash.

Elizabeth put her hand against the empty place at her hip.


The camp handled its grief in daylight. Somewhere behind her a bucket line was still working water into ash that no longer needed it, more habit than use.

She sat with her back against a wagon wheel until her legs remembered how to hold her.

Her hand kept finding the empty place at her hip. Not searching — checking. The way a tongue finds a missing tooth.

No cookbook. No diary. Three weeks of boxes in an apartment across a city that felt, from here, like someone else's address.

She thought she would cry. What came instead was smaller and stranger: a kind of room opening up where the weight had been — the specific quiet of a hand that has set something down after carrying it a long way.

Shade was gone too — folded into the list only now, after the diary, after the cookbook, after the wheel that hadn't been new in a long time. He had told her true things in a diner at four in the morning and again at a tree line before dawn, and neither time had he asked her to owe him anything for it.

Nobody at camp said his name. She understood, distantly, that this was its own kind of funeral — the kind where the only mourner doesn't get to say so out loud, because saying it would mean explaining a man who wore another man's face wrong and still told her the truth more plainly than the original ever had.

She let herself have that for exactly as long as the fire took to burn all the way down.

Then she got up, because the camp was still moving, and there was a child somewhere with a scraped knee who probably needed a hand to hold.
