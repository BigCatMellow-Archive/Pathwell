Chapter Seven

The parking lot was empty. Or it looked empty, which turned out to be a different thing.

The smell arrived first: low tide, wet rot, the specific version of wrong that had come through her apartment door. Elizabeth's hand went to the dagger in her waistband.

"Ah," Pathwell said.

Stansbury stopped beside him. "That one of yours?"

"Define mine."

"No."

The blob came from behind the Cadillac. It was the size of the car, or close enough to make the comparison uncomfortable, and it moved with the patient quality of something that did not have anywhere else to be.

It went for Elizabeth.

Pathwell's hand flashed toward his coat. Stansbury said something that might have been a warning and might have been profanity. The blob crossed the lot faster than anything that shape had a right to move.

Elizabeth stepped back.

It did not matter. The first edge of it touched her boot and climbed.

Cold went through her ankle. Not water-cold. Something worse, something that had opinions about being inside her skin. Her knee locked. Her mouth filled with salt.

She tried to pull away and the blob came with her, up her shin, over her thigh, heavy and soft and wrong. The weight of it settled across her ribs and her lungs went shallow, breath coming in the wrong direction, too fast and too small. Not panic — the thing underneath panic, the one that had been there before panic learned its name.

"Elizabeth," Pathwell said.

His voice reached her from a distance.

Stansbury was moving. The foam sword hit the blob and sank halfway in. The blob shivered, annoyed, and kept climbing.

"Cut it," Stansbury said.

"With what?" Pathwell snapped.

"Not you."

The dagger was still in Elizabeth's hand.

Her fingers were numb around the handle. The blob had reached her ribs. Her breath came shallow against the pressure.

She cut.

The dagger was foam. It should have folded. Instead it bit into the black edge of the thing around her and opened a seam. The blob recoiled.

She cut again.

The pressure loosened around her chest. Air came in all at once, ugly and loud. She swung down, not neatly, not like anyone who knew what they were doing, just hard enough to make a way out. The dagger tore a line from her ribs to her knee. The blob peeled back.

She fell out of it into the dirt.

Hit the ground on her hands and knees and the air came back in all at once — tasting like gravel and the particular nothing of open space, which at that moment was the best thing she'd ever breathed.

Then she got up.

The blob rolled toward her again.

"No," she said, and swung.

It split where the dagger struck. Stansbury came in from the side with both foam swords and drove it back. Pathwell finally got a paper out of his coat, but by then the thing was losing shape, breaking into pieces that hit the dirt like wet sandbags and went still.

The parking lot was quiet. The neon sign in the bar window buzzed. Inside, Pathwell's darts were probably still in the board.

Elizabeth stood with the dagger in her hand, shaking so hard the blade wobbled.

Nobody came out of the bar. The screen door stayed shut, the dartboard behind it unwatched, the same tired song still playing to the same half-empty room. Whatever had just happened in the parking lot had happened in a world the bar had decided not to notice.

Pathwell looked at the pieces of blob. Then at her. He straightened his jacket.

"That was close," he said.

Stansbury turned slowly toward him. Then he looked at Elizabeth — the dagger, her hand, the line she'd cut — for a beat longer than the moment needed, the way you look at something you're recalibrating.

Elizabeth was breathing hard, which no one commented on. The cold was still in her leg where the blob had touched her. Pathwell was already moving toward the car, his eyes on the door handle and not on her hand or her leg or any part of what had just gone through her.


They got in the car. Stansbury took the passenger seat because Pathwell had opened that door first and then forgotten why. Pathwell climbed into the back, objected to the arrangement, and then objected to Stansbury's objection to his objection.

Elizabeth started the engine.

The bar's parking lot fell away behind them, the neon dartboard-blue of the window shrinking to a smudge and then to nothing.

Out on the road it was dark now, the fields on either side running to black. The headlights cut a short way forward and not much more.

"You always do this," Stansbury said.

"I always do many things," Pathwell said from the back seat. "It is one of my more reliable qualities."

"You turn people into errands."

"I turn errands into solutions."

"You turn people into errands and then call the errands solutions."

"That is a very elegant sentence for a man who has had too much domestic beer."

Elizabeth kept both hands on the wheel.

"She's sitting right there," Stansbury said.

"I know she's sitting right there."

"Do you?"

Pathwell made a small, offended sound. "Lizzy, you understand that when he gets like this he is mostly talking to a version of me from 1912."

The road bent left. Stansbury and Pathwell kept going, one in the passenger seat and one behind her, their voices crossing the car like she was a table between them. The wheel sat under her hands, steady and real. The dagger was still in her waistband, pressing into her lower back. She hadn't taken it out after the lot. She didn't take it out now.

The headlights found a mile marker, then a reflector, then nothing again. She knew this shape of quiet from other cars, other arguments that weren't hers — her own name used like punctuation neither speaker meant to hit. Two voices going back and forth over a decade she hadn't lived through, and her hands doing the only useful thing in the car.

For one clean second, nobody was looking at her.

She turned.
