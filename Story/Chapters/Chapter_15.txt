Chapter Fifteen

Elizabeth reached the archive before the bucket line existed. The door breathed smoke around her.

Someone was calling about the woods. Elizabeth looked back.

Shade was coming toward the fire from the far side of the ruined brass frame, his right arm still pulled toward Pathwell. He was trying to use the other one to wave people toward the archive.

He took a step. His next one stopped.

He looked down.

Something black had closed around his boot, nearly flat against the dirt. Beyond it the rest of the shape was forcing itself between two trees. The air there pulled inward around its edges, as though Camp were making the gap too small for it. The shape came through anyway.

Low tide under the smell of burning oil.

Shade yanked his leg. The blackness moved with him, and then tightened. He caught at the edge with his free hand and pulled it away again.

"Help!"

Pathwell lay on his side by the collapsed frame, among the loose pale threads. His head had turned toward Shade. He tried to get his elbow beneath him, but he couldn't stand.

A man from the bucket line dropped what he was carrying and ran toward Shade. He took hold of the green jacket with both hands. Shade grabbed his arm, and they pulled. His shoulder came forward. The boot did not.

"Again," Shade said.

A shelf went over inside the archive.

"Milo!" the archivist shouted.

From inside came a child's cry, thin with coughing. "Help! I'm stuck!"

Shade looked at the doorway. Elizabeth had heard both of them.

He looked at her.

Her right hand had found the foam dagger behind her waist. She had cut Pathwell out of one of these things at the bar. The seam had taken several cuts, and she had needed both arms to pull him free. Her left shoulder hurt inside the sling before she'd moved it at all.

The man caught Shade's jacket again. Mama Baga was calling people back from the burning wall. On the far side of the wagon Stansbury was pushing through the people carrying water.

"Elizabeth!" Milo shouted.

Shade was still trying to pull his leg free. He had heard Milo too.

Elizabeth turned and ran through the archive door.

Heat pressed against the right side of her face.

Heat pressed against the right side of her face. The woven runner at the threshold was fully involved now, flame running up the dry wooden wall in quick yellow tongues. Deeper inside, the first shelf of charged records had begun throwing pieces of other people's lives into the air.

Rain on canvas.

Bread.

A woman's relief so sudden Elizabeth almost laughed. Then somebody else's grief arrived on top of it and the laugh died before it reached her mouth.

None of it belonged to her. All of it was in the way.

"Milo!"

"Here!"

His voice came from low and left.

Elizabeth stepped into the doorway.

Her diary lay open in the entrance aisle. She nearly stepped on it. The cover had folded backward, and three pages fanned across the floorboards. Her own handwriting looked up at her through smoke.

CALL ELECTRIC.

BUY BOXES.

CHANGE ADDRESS.

FIND NANA'S SPOON.

The last line was checked twice.

Elizabeth stopped, for less than a second.

Her body knew the book before her mind finished identifying it. The old checking motion started in her right hand, fingers closing toward an object she no longer owned and had once refused to be separated from.

The fire cracked farther inside.

"Elizabeth!"

Milo knew her name now.

She stepped over the diary.

The front reserve shelf was three feet farther in. Nana's cookbook sat exactly where it had that morning, spine supported, flour-stained cover turned slightly toward the aisle.

JONES FAMILY COOKBOOK.

DONOR: ELIZABETH.

One page used.

Ask before use.

The fire had not reached it yet. The shelf was close enough to touch.

Behind it Milo shouted again.

Elizabeth ran past the cookbook too.


The fallen shelf had caught Milo across the lower legs. It had pinned him without crushing him, and for the next several seconds that difference was the entire world.

He was on his back between two narrow aisles with one arm over his face. The shelf had gone diagonally across him, its upper corner wedged against another cabinet. Boxes and loose papers had spilled across his chest and the floor.

His sock had finally stayed up. Elizabeth hated that detail immediately.

"Can you move your feet?"

Milo lowered his arm. His face was gray under the soot. "One."

"Which one?"

"Left."

"Move it."

His left boot scraped the floor.

"Other one?"

"Hurts."

"That's not the same as can't. Try."

He tried. His right foot shifted half an inch beneath the shelf.

Elizabeth let out a breath. "Good."

"Doesn't feel good."

"I know."

The answer came out in Shade's voice and Pathwell's voice and her own.

The shelf was too heavy to lift one-handed. She knew this before trying, and tried anyway. Her right hand found the edge. She pulled. Nothing moved.

Her left shoulder throbbed inside the sling like something offended to still be attached to her. The healer's instruction returned with perfect timing.

You are not using this arm today.

Elizabeth looked at Milo. "I'm going to need you to do something unpleasant."

"More unpleasant?"

"Probably."

"Okay."

There were advantages to children who had spent the morning taking instructions from people carrying turnips.

Elizabeth tore the sling loose with her teeth and right hand. Her left arm dropped three inches, and pain went white behind her eyes.

"You okay?" Milo asked.

"No."

A support strut from the broken shelf lay beside him. Elizabeth kicked it beneath the shelf edge, crouched, got her right hand around the upper end, and pushed down.

The shelf rose perhaps an inch.

Milo pulled. His leg didn't come free.

"Again," Elizabeth said.

"It hurts."

"I know. Again."

The fire reached something behind her. A handwritten page tore in the heat, and for one second Elizabeth was desperately homesick for a kitchen she had never seen: a blue bowl, a window over a sink, somebody humming badly while cutting apples.

Then the impression broke apart.

The shelf settled. Milo made a sound between his teeth.

Elizabeth looked at the strut, and then at her left shoulder. "This next part is stupid," she said.

"Pathwell stupid?"

Despite everything, Elizabeth almost smiled. "Worse. Mine."

She wedged her left forearm under the strut as a fulcrum and braced her shoulder against the shelf.

The pain was immediate and enormous. It didn't make the choice for her.

Her right hand pushed down. Her left side held. The shelf lifted two inches.

"Now."

Milo pulled his right knee hard toward his chest. His boot caught.

Elizabeth made a sound she had no intention of discussing later.

"Again!"

Milo twisted. The boot came off, and his leg came free, and the shelf slammed down.

Elizabeth collapsed against the aisle. Milo crawled over her.

"Can you stand?" he asked.

She laughed once, because the question was offensive. "Can you?"

Milo stood: bare right foot, one sock still somehow present on the left.

"Then help me."

He gave her both hands. Elizabeth got one knee under herself and stood without using the left arm. The shoulder was still in place, probably. That was a problem for later.

Smoke folded across the aisle.

"Door," she said.

They moved.


The diary was still at the entrance. They reached it together.

Milo saw the book and bent instinctively. Elizabeth caught the back of his shirt with her right hand.

"No."

"But—"

"Outside."

He looked at her, and then at the diary, and then he obeyed.

The cookbook remained on the reserve shelf. The flame had reached the shelf below it, and the support around Nana's damaged spine had begun to darken at one corner.

Elizabeth saw that too. She didn't stop.

She got Milo through the door.

That was the choice.

Everything else burned afterward.


Outside, the bucket line had moved.

Elizabeth supported Milo with her right arm. He was coughing, but he was out. She looked past the smoke toward the ruined brass frame.

Shade was no longer standing there.

The man who had pulled at his jacket was on his knees. He was staring at his hands. Beyond him the black mass moved across the ground where the pale threads had fallen. It crossed one, and the loose light vanished beneath it. Then another.

"Where is he?" Elizabeth asked.

The man looked at the empty place beside him. "We couldn't get him loose."

He swallowed. "It took him."

Milo leaned harder against her. Elizabeth moved him away from the frame, into the cleared space beside the archive. Pathwell was still on the ground on the other side of the black shape. His face was turned toward the place where Shade had been.

The creature folded past Pathwell. For a moment it leaned toward him. Then it took up the remaining pale threads, searched the dirt, and withdrew toward the trees.

There was nothing left at the frame that looked like Shade. No green jacket. No outstretched hand.

Elizabeth held Milo upright. He was breathing.


Stansbury hadn't used the front door. Elizabeth discovered this when she came out with Milo and nearly collided with him at the side of the archive wagon. He had a wool blanket wrapped around one forearm and a small hammer in the other hand.

"He's out," Elizabeth said.

Stansbury looked at Milo and counted limbs. Then he counted Elizabeth. "Good."

Then he smashed the small side window.

Elizabeth stared at him. "What are you doing?"

"Different problem."

The answer was so Stansbury she almost objected on principle.

He knocked the remaining glass inward with the hammer, threw the blanket over the sill, and climbed halfway through.

"Stansbury."

"The front's gone. This section isn't."

Smoke pushed through the broken window around him.

"You don't need to save everything."

He looked back at her. "I know."

Then he disappeared inside.

Elizabeth moved Milo farther from the wagon. The archivist caught him and dropped to both knees in the dirt.

"Leg?"

"Hurts."

"Move your toes."

Milo did.

The archivist closed his eyes for one second. Then he opened them. "Healer."

Someone was already running.

Camp had become motion around them: buckets, blankets, people dragging the nearest wagon away from the archive before the fire could bridge the gap. Mama Baga stood where three different lines of work intersected and made no attempt to own all of them. She sent water where water could still matter. She sent people away from the side where the charged records were coming apart. She didn't tell Elizabeth what to feel.

Stansbury came back out through the side window carrying the blue road notebook under one arm and a cloth-wrapped bundle against his chest. His hair was singed at the temple.

"Take these."

The archivist took them. The blue notebook, with its creek crossings and marriages and somebody's joke about a mule that refused to cross Maryland, was still itself. The wrapped bundle was a packet of family letters.

Stansbury turned back toward the window.

Elizabeth grabbed his sleeve. "No."

He looked at her hand, and then through the window. "One more shelf."

"You said you don't need to save everything."

"I said I know."

"That isn't the same thing."

His expression changed. She hadn't convinced him; he was actually checking.

Heat rolled from the broken window. The roof canvas above it sagged.

Stansbury looked once toward the rear section and shook his head. "Not that shelf."

Elizabeth let go.

He moved three feet farther down instead, ripped open a narrow exterior compartment built into the wagon wall, and pulled out two flat archive boxes that hadn't yet caught. He got the first under his arm. The second stuck. He pulled.

A burning strip of canvas came down from the roof.

Stansbury turned his face away. The canvas struck the back of his right hand and wrapped once around his wrist.

He let go of both. For one second he made no sound. Then he tore the burning cloth away with his left hand and threw it into the dirt.

The skin across the back of his right hand was already red and shining.

"Stansbury."

"I know."

He picked up the box that hadn't stuck with his left hand and brought it away from the wagon.

Then he stopped going back.

The healer reached him with a basin and wet cloth and sat him on an overturned bucket, and Stansbury gave her the burned hand without argument.


The archive kept burning. The records inside broke apart in bursts of borrowed feeling, but no other cleanup creature came for them. This was still an ordinary fire.




Elizabeth turned back toward the archive.

The front reserve shelf was burning. Nana's cookbook had caught along the bottom edge. There was no glow, and nothing magical was consuming it. Ordinary flame moved through old paper and flour-stained cardboard, because that was what fire did.

The support around the spine blackened. The cover folded inward. Elizabeth watched until she couldn't tell the handwritten pages from any other burning pages inside the wagon.

Her diary was closer, still in the entrance aisle. The fire reached it from the runner. One corner curled, then the next. The cover caught.

Her own ordinary handwriting disappeared without being used for anything.

No spell.

No sacrifice.

No last message.

Just fire.

Elizabeth stood with her right hand empty. Milo was alive behind her.

She didn't regret the order.


The archive roof sagged.

"Back," Mama Baga said.

Elizabeth moved back because the request made sense.

Stansbury was sitting on the overturned bucket again while the healer wrapped his right hand and wrist. He watched the archive burn without trying to stand.

The blue road notebook lay on the ground beside the archivist, damp from somebody's bucket and intact. One archive box sat beside it. The family letters remained wrapped.

Not everything. Something.

Pathwell was still in the dirt. Nobody had gone to him yet. There were other injuries, other work, other people.

Elizabeth looked toward the place where Shade had been. The man who had tried to pull him free had returned to the bucket line. Nothing remained at the frame except trampled dirt.

She thought of the coffee. The green jacket. Shade calling Milo a cheat. Shade saying he didn't know yet what else he liked.

That hand was promising.

The archive roof collapsed inward. The sound pushed everyone another step back.

Elizabeth kept looking at the empty patch of ground.

The low-tide smell had thinned into the smoke.

The draw did not return.

Shade did not either.
