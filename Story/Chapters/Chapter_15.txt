Chapter Fifteen

Elizabeth reached the archive before the bucket line existed.

That was not bravery.

It was distance.

She had already been moving when Milo shouted the second time.

The archive door breathed smoke around her.

Heat pressed against the right side of her face. The woven runner at the threshold was fully involved now, flame running up the dry wooden wall in quick yellow tongues. Deeper inside, the first shelf of charged records had begun throwing pieces of other people's lives into the air.

Rain on canvas.

Bread.

A woman's relief so sudden Elizabeth almost laughed.

Then somebody else's grief arrived on top of it and the laugh died before it reached her mouth.

None of it belonged to her.

All of it was in the way.

"Milo!"

"Here!"

His voice came from low and left.

Elizabeth stepped into the doorway.

Her diary lay open in the entrance aisle.

She nearly stepped on it.

The cover had folded backward. Three pages fanned across the floorboards. Her own handwriting looked up at her through smoke.

CALL ELECTRIC.

BUY BOXES.

CHANGE ADDRESS.

FIND NANA'S SPOON.

The last line was checked twice.

Elizabeth stopped.

Not long.

Long enough.

Her body knew the book before her mind finished identifying it. The old checking motion started in her right hand, fingers closing toward an object she no longer owned and had once refused to be separated from.

The fire cracked farther inside.

"Elizabeth!"

Milo knew her name now.

That decided it.

She stepped over the diary.

The front reserve shelf was three feet farther in.

Nana's cookbook sat exactly where it had that morning, spine supported, flour-stained cover turned slightly toward the aisle.

JONES FAMILY COOKBOOK.

DONOR: ELIZABETH.

One page used.

Ask before use.

The fire had not reached it yet.

She could take it.

Right hand, one motion.

The shelf was close enough to touch.

Behind it Milo shouted again.

Elizabeth ran past the cookbook too.


The fallen shelf had caught Milo across the lower legs.

Not crushed.

Pinned.

That distinction was the entire world for the next several seconds.

He was on his back between two narrow aisles with one arm over his face. The shelf had gone diagonally across him, its upper corner wedged against another cabinet. Boxes and loose papers had spilled across his chest and the floor.

His sock had finally stayed up.

Elizabeth hated that detail immediately.

"Can you move your feet?"

Milo lowered his arm.

His face was gray under the soot.

"One."

"Which one?"

"Left."

"Move it."

His left boot scraped the floor.

"Other one?"

"Hurts."

"That's not the same as can't. Try."

He tried.

His right foot shifted half an inch beneath the shelf.

Elizabeth let out a breath.

"Good."

"Doesn't feel good."

"I know."

The answer came out in Shade's voice and Pathwell's voice and her own.

She ignored that.

The shelf was too heavy to lift one-handed.

She knew this before trying.

She tried anyway.

Her right hand found the edge. She pulled.

Nothing.

Her left shoulder throbbed inside the sling like something offended to still be attached to her.

The healer's instruction returned with perfect timing.

You are not using this arm today.

Elizabeth looked at Milo.

"I'm going to need you to do something unpleasant."

"More unpleasant?"

"Probably."

"Okay."

There were advantages to children who had spent the morning taking instructions from people carrying turnips.

Elizabeth tore the sling loose with her teeth and right hand.

Her left arm dropped three inches.

Pain went white behind her eyes.

She stopped breathing.

Milo watched her.

"You okay?"

"No."

It was getting easier to say.

A support strut from the broken shelf lay beside him.

Elizabeth kicked it beneath the shelf edge.

Not a lever yet.

A possibility.

She crouched, got her right hand around the upper end, and pushed down.

The shelf rose perhaps an inch.

Milo pulled.

His leg did not come free.

"Again," Elizabeth said.

"It hurts."

"I know. Again."

The fire reached something behind her.

A handwritten page tore in the heat.

For one second Elizabeth was desperately homesick for a kitchen she had never seen.

A blue bowl.

A window over a sink.

Somebody humming badly while cutting apples.

Then the impression broke apart.

The shelf settled.

Milo made a sound between his teeth.

Elizabeth looked at the strut.

Looked at her left shoulder.

"This next part is stupid," she said.

"Pathwell stupid?"

Despite everything, Elizabeth almost smiled.

"Different category."

She wedged her left forearm beneath the strut and used the side of her shoulder as a brace.

The pain was immediate and enormous.

It did not make the choice for her.

Her right hand pushed down.

Her left side held.

The shelf lifted two inches.

"Now."

Milo pulled his right knee hard toward his chest.

His boot caught.

Elizabeth made a sound she had no intention of discussing later.

"Again!"

Milo twisted.

The boot came off.

His leg came free.

The shelf slammed down.

Elizabeth collapsed against the aisle.

Milo crawled over her.

"Can you stand?"

She laughed once because the question was offensive.

"Can you?"

Milo stood.

Bare right foot.

One sock still somehow present on the left.

"Then help me."

He gave her both hands.

Elizabeth got one knee under herself and stood without using the left arm.

The shoulder was still in place.

Probably.

That was a problem for later.

Smoke folded across the aisle.

"Door," she said.

They moved.


The diary was still at the entrance.

They reached it together.

Milo saw the book and bent instinctively.

Elizabeth caught the back of his shirt with her right hand.

"No."

"But—"

"Outside."

He looked at her.

Then at the diary.

Then obeyed.

The cookbook remained on the reserve shelf.

The flame had reached the shelf below it.

The support around Nana's damaged spine had begun to darken at one corner.

Elizabeth saw that too.

She did not stop.

She got Milo through the door.

That was the choice.

Everything else burned afterward.


Stansbury did not use the front door.

Elizabeth discovered this when she came out with Milo and nearly collided with him at the side of the archive wagon.

He had a wool blanket wrapped around one forearm and a small hammer in the other hand.

"He's out," Elizabeth said.

Stansbury looked at Milo.

Counted limbs.

Counted Elizabeth.

"Good."

Then he smashed the small side window.

Elizabeth stared at him.

"What are you doing?"

"Different problem."

The answer was so Stansbury she almost objected on principle.

He knocked the remaining glass inward with the hammer, threw the blanket over the sill, and climbed halfway through.

"Stansbury."

"The front's gone. This section isn't."

Smoke pushed through the broken window around him.

"You don't need to save everything."

He looked back at her.

"I know."

That mattered.

Then he disappeared inside.

Elizabeth moved Milo farther from the wagon.

The archivist caught him and dropped to both knees in the dirt.

"Leg?"

"Hurts."

"Move your toes."

Milo did.

The archivist closed his eyes for one second.

Then opened them.

"Healer."

Someone was already running.

Camp had become motion around them.

Buckets.

Blankets.

People dragging the nearest wagon away from the archive before the fire could bridge the gap.

Mama Baga stood where three different lines of work intersected and made no attempt to own all of them.

She sent water where water could still matter.

She sent people away from the side where the charged records were coming apart.

She did not tell Elizabeth what to feel.

That also mattered.

The side window exploded outward.

Stansbury came through carrying the blue road notebook under one arm and a cloth-wrapped bundle against his chest.

His hair was singed at the temple.

"Take these."

The archivist took them.

Blue notebook.

The one with creek crossings and marriages and somebody's joke about a mule that refused to cross Maryland.

Still itself.

The wrapped bundle was the family-letter packet that had sat beside the cookbook.

Stansbury turned back toward the window.

Elizabeth grabbed his sleeve.

"No."

He looked at her hand.

Then through the window.

"One more shelf."

"You said you don't need to save everything."

"I said I know."

"That isn't the same thing."

His expression changed.

Not because she had convinced him.

Because he was actually checking.

Heat rolled from the broken window.

The roof canvas above it sagged.

Stansbury looked once toward the rear section.

Then shook his head.

"Not that shelf."

Elizabeth let go.

He moved three feet farther down instead, ripped open a narrow exterior compartment built into the wagon wall, and pulled out two flat archive boxes that had not yet caught.

He got the first under his arm.

The second stuck.

He pulled.

A burning strip of canvas came down from the roof.

Stansbury turned his face away.

The canvas struck the back of his right hand and wrapped once around his wrist.

He dropped the box.

For one second he made no sound.

Then he tore the burning cloth away with his left hand and threw it into the dirt.

The skin across the back of his right hand was already red and shining.

"Stansbury."

"I know."

He picked up the box with his left hand.

Not the one that had stuck.

One.

He brought it away from the wagon.

Then he stopped going back.

The healer reached him with a basin and wet cloth.

Stansbury surrendered the burned hand without argument.

That was also a choice.


The smell arrived beneath the smoke.

Wet rot.

Low tide.

Elizabeth knew it before anyone said anything.

The entire clearing seemed to recognize it with her.

People who had been facing the archive looked toward the trees.

The fire kept burning.

The strange emotional weather around it kept breaking loose—relief, hunger, rain, an old irritation about somebody borrowing a coat and never returning it.

The smell ignored all of that.

It came for something else.

Shade was standing near the failed brass frame.

Pathwell remained on the ground ten feet away, one elbow beneath him, the fresh pale spillage scattered through the dirt like threads the morning could not decide whether to see.

Shade's right hand was open toward him.

Still.

Even now.

The blob came through the trees without urgency.

It did not charge.

It did not roar.

It simply arrived.

Six feet of dark matter that refused a clean edge, then seven, then more as it folded itself between trunks. The eye slid off it and had to return. Low-tide stink rolled ahead of it.

Mama Baga swore.

Pathwell tried to stand.

His arm failed.

"No," Stansbury said from beside the healer.

Pathwell looked at him.

"There is fresh spill."

"I am aware."

"Then move people back."

Stansbury stared at him.

"You can ask."

Pathwell stopped.

The correction landed even here.

He looked toward Mama Baga instead.

"Fresh pruning spill by the frame. It should be what it's here for."

Mama Baga's face stayed on the approaching blob.

"Should."

Pathwell followed her gaze.

Shade had gone very still.

Elizabeth saw him understand something and hated that she could not tell whether he was right.

"Shade," she said.

He looked at the blob.

Then at Pathwell.

Then at the loose pale threads in the dirt.

"At the bar," Shade said.

Pathwell's face tightened.

"Don't."

"It went for you."

"Because our signatures overlap."

"That's your theory."

"It's a good theory."

"You have had a difficult morning with those."

There was almost humor in it.

Almost.

The blob reached the edge of Camp.

It slowed.

Not hesitation.

Assessment.

Its surface pulled subtly toward Pathwell.

Then toward Shade.

Then toward the pale spillage.

Three related signals occupying too little ground.

Shade looked at his right hand.

It remained pointed at Pathwell.

"What happens if I move away from you?"

Pathwell shook his head.

"I don't know."

Shade looked at him.

The answer seemed to satisfy him more than certainty would have.

"Good."

Elizabeth moved toward Shade.

Her shoulder objected and she ignored it.

"Good?"

"Accurate."

"What are you thinking?"

Shade looked at the blob again.

"I think it's been trying to decide between us because it can't read the difference cleanly."

"Think."

"Yes."

"And?"

He nodded toward the spillage.

"That is new. I'm not."

Pathwell's face changed.

"No."

Shade looked at him.

"Interesting word."

"Don't do this."

"I'm not doing anything yet."

"I know what you're considering."

Shade's mouth moved at one corner.

"No, you don't."

Pathwell flinched as though struck somewhere the fire had missed.

Elizabeth looked between them.

"Shade."

He turned to her.

The green jacket was soot-streaked now.

One sleeve still half an inch too short.

Too many pockets.

The queen of spades was probably still in one of them.

A person could accumulate a surprising amount of life in a morning.

"You don't know what it will do," Elizabeth said.

"No."

"It could just take the fresh spill."

"Yes."

"It could go for Pathwell again."

"Yes."

"It could hurt you."

Shade looked at the blob.

"That seems likely."

"Then don't make this sound like a plan."

He looked back at her.

"It isn't."

That was worse.

"Then what is it?"

Shade thought about the question.

The blob advanced another few feet.

People moved back from it because Mama Baga asked them to.

Not because it hunted them.

Because being between a cleanup creature and a mess was a bad position to occupy.

Shade said, "A way to learn which problem it thinks is biggest."

Elizabeth stared at him.

"By volunteering?"

"By separating the signals."

"With your body."

"Unfortunately attached."

Pathwell dragged himself another foot upright.

"Shade. Stay where you are."

Shade looked at him.

"You really have learned nothing in the last six minutes."

Pathwell closed his mouth.

Shade waited.

Pathwell tried again.

"Will you stay?"

The question arrived broken and late.

Shade's face changed.

Not forgiveness.

Not surprise exactly.

Recognition of effort too small for the moment and still real.

"No," he said.

Pathwell nodded once.

He did not tell him again.

Shade turned toward the open side of the clearing.

Away from Pathwell.

Away from the fresh spillage.

Away from Elizabeth and Milo and the burning archive.

He walked.

The draw fought the direction immediately.

His right arm remained stretched backward toward Pathwell while the rest of him moved forward.

It made his gait ugly.

He kept going.

Ten feet.

Fifteen.

Twenty.

The blob changed course.

Not fast.

Decisively.

Its whole dark mass bent after Shade.

Shade stopped.

Elizabeth stopped breathing.

Pathwell whispered something she did not hear.

Shade looked over his shoulder at the blob following him.

Then down at his own right hand still pointing backward.

"Well," he said.

Elizabeth wanted him to say something better than that.

Something final enough to deserve what was happening.

Shade had never been particularly interested in deserving scenes.

He looked toward the card table.

The old woman had abandoned it when the fire started.

One card remained on the dirt beneath the stool.

A heart.

Not the queen.

Shade looked at Milo, sitting beside the archivist with one bare foot and one sock.

"You still owe me a hand," Milo called.

Nobody had told him not to.

Shade's face changed.

There it was.

His smile.

Small.

Current.

"I was winning," he said.

Milo shook his head violently.

"You were not."

Shade turned back toward the blob.

"Corrupt institution."

Then the dark mass reached him.


It did not bite.

There was no mouth.

It folded around Shade from the legs upward with the patient thoroughness of water finding a low place.

Shade staggered once.

His left hand struck the surface and disappeared to the wrist.

His right arm remained stretched toward Pathwell.

The draw pulled taut.

Elizabeth felt nothing magical herself.

She only saw two men react at once.

Pathwell jerked forward on the ground.

Shade's shoulder twisted backward.

For one terrible second the connection looked visible only because both bodies obeyed it.

Shade looked at Pathwell.

Not for permission.

Not to forgive him.

Just looked.

Then the blob took his chest.

His right hand closed.

Not because Pathwell made it.

Shade closed it himself.

The dark surface passed over his shoulders.

His face.

The green jacket.

Too many pockets.

Then there was no Shade standing in the clearing.

The draw vanished.

Pathwell fell sideways like something that had been holding part of his weight had stopped.

The silence inside him was visible.

Elizabeth hated that too.

The blob did not leave.

That mattered.

It turned back toward the failed frame.

Past Pathwell.

Its surface leaned toward him once.

Paused.

Rechecked.

Pathwell did not move.

The blob passed him and crossed the first loose pale thread.

The spillage vanished into the dark surface.

Not settled.

Not returned to Pathwell.

Removed as unclaimed waste.

The blob continued across the failed frame.

The remaining pale threads disappeared beneath it one by one.

Then all at once there were none.

The wet-rot smell began to thin.

The blob turned toward Pathwell again.

Elizabeth saw his hands tense against the dirt.

Nothing happened.

No attack.

No judgment.

The creature simply searched the immediate ground around him.

Shade was gone.

The fresh spillage was gone.

The ambiguous signal that had followed Pathwell through the night was gone with Shade.

The blob found nothing else it recognized as unclaimed waste.

It withdrew.

Not dramatically.

Back through the trees with the same patient indifference it had arrived with.

The archive continued burning behind them.

The emotional fallout continued breaking loose in fragments.

A wedding dance.

A flooded road.

Somebody's fury about a missing saucepan.

The blob ignored all of it.

Different mess.

Different consequence.


Elizabeth turned back toward the archive.

The front reserve shelf was burning.

Nana's cookbook had caught along the bottom edge.

No glow.

No magical consumption.

Ordinary flame moved through old paper and flour-stained cardboard because that was what fire did.

The support around the spine blackened.

The cover folded inward.

Elizabeth watched until she could not tell the handwriting pages from any other burning pages inside the wagon.

Her diary was closer.

Still in the entrance aisle.

The fire reached it from the runner.

One corner curled.

Then the next.

The cover caught.

Her own ordinary handwriting disappeared without being used for anything.

No spell.

No sacrifice.

No last message.

Just fire.

Elizabeth stood with her right hand empty.

Milo was alive behind her.

She did not regret the order.

That did not make the loss hurt less.


The archive roof sagged.

"Back," Mama Baga said.

Elizabeth moved back because the request made sense.

Stansbury was sitting on an overturned bucket while the healer wrapped his right hand and wrist.

He watched the archive burn without trying to stand again.

The blue road notebook lay on the ground beside the archivist, damp from somebody's bucket and intact.

Two archive boxes sat beside it.

The family letters remained wrapped.

Not everything.

Something.

Pathwell was still in the dirt.

Nobody had gone to him yet.

Not because nobody cared whether he was injured.

Because there were other injuries.

Other work.

Other people.

For once, the world did not reorder itself around the fact that Pathwell had fallen down.

Elizabeth looked toward the place where Shade had been.

Nothing remained there except trampled dirt.

No coat.

No card.

No useful relic.

She thought of the coffee.

The green jacket.

Milo saying he was cheating.

Shade saying he did not know yet what else he liked.

That hand was promising.

The archive roof collapsed inward.

The sound pushed everyone another step back.

Elizabeth kept looking at the empty patch of ground.

Shade had taken a risk because he thought the blob might finally distinguish him from Pathwell.

He had been right about what it chose first.

That did not mean he had known.

It did not mean he had wanted to stop existing.

It did not make his existence a mistake that had finally been corrected.

It meant he had made one last choice with incomplete information.

Like everyone else.

The low-tide smell disappeared into the trees.

The draw did not return.

Shade did not either.
