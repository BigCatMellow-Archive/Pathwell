Chapter Seventeen

By the third morning, Pathwell had learned that restitution contained more rulers than magic.

The new drying rack stood beside the remains of the archive under a patched canvas awning. It was six feet long, four feet high, and—according to Pathwell—structurally superior to the one the archivist had described.

The archivist walked around it once, and then twice. Pathwell waited.

Elizabeth sat nearby with her left arm still in the sling and a mug of Camp coffee balanced on one knee. Milo leaned on his broom-handle crutch and watched with the concentration of someone hoping an adult was about to be corrected publicly.

The archivist pointed at the bottom rail. "Too low."

"By how much?" Pathwell asked.

"Three inches."

"It is stronger there."

"It is also exactly where the wet boxes need to slide underneath."

Pathwell opened his mouth, closed it, and looked at the rack again. "Three inches?"

"Three."

Pathwell picked up the screwdriver.

Milo whispered, "He almost did it."

Elizabeth drank her coffee. "I know."

Pathwell looked over. "I can hear both of you."

"Good," Milo said.

The archivist pointed to the opposite end. "And that brace goes outside."

Pathwell stared at him. "That is objectively worse."

"Outside."

A long silence. Then Pathwell unscrewed it.

Elizabeth looked down at her coffee so he wouldn't see her smile.


The first week was mostly things Pathwell couldn't make interesting.

He was given a list of cabinet dimensions. He found better cabinets and asked before ordering them, and the archivist said no: too deep for the wagon. Pathwell measured. They were too deep for the wagon. He ordered the listed cabinets.

The blue road notebook spent three days beneath blotting paper while the archivist and two Camp women reconstructed copied route pages from it. Pathwell was allowed to help only after the archivist gave him clean hands, a pencil, and exactly one section.

"Copy what it says," the archivist told him.

"I understand transcription."

"Including mistakes."

"Some of these are obviously mistakes."

"Especially the obvious ones."

Pathwell looked down at the page.

DO NOT TRUST THE SOUTH BANK AFTER THREE DAYS OF RAIN, NO MATTER WHAT HESS SAYS.

Below it someone had written:

HESS SAYS FOUR.

Pathwell tapped the pencil against the table. "This is not information. This is a family argument preserved as infrastructure."

The archivist didn't look up. "Copy it."

Pathwell copied it.

Elizabeth came by Camp that afternoon because she wanted to. The distinction had become easier to notice once nobody was carrying her books away from her. Getting there meant asking Pathwell to open the way.

She found him copying HESS SAYS FOUR in exactly the same uneven capitalization as the original.

"You missed a smudge," she said.

Pathwell looked up. "The smudge is not textual information."

The archivist, from the next table, said, "Copy the smudge."

Pathwell looked at Elizabeth. "I have been placed under unreasonable government."

"You set fire to their archive."

"Their government may be proportionate."

He copied the smudge.

Elizabeth stayed for lunch. Afterward the archivist put a stack of finished copies in front of her and the blue notebook beside it, and she checked them one-handed at the next table, line against line. In the third copy she found a place where Pathwell had quietly fixed somebody's spelling of Susquehanna.

She turned the page around and showed him.

He looked at it for a while. Then he took a clean sheet and copied the page again, mistake included.

Nobody asked when she was coming back.


Her apartment took longer to become hers than the archive took to become a construction site. That annoyed her. She had expected catastrophe to make ordinary things feel trivial. Instead ordinary things had remained aggressively themselves.

The electric company still wanted an account number. There were still three boxes marked KITCHEN even though only one contained anything that belonged in a kitchen. Her dry cleaning was permanently gone, because the ticket had disappeared down a storm drain days ago and the shop had required identification, a description, and twenty minutes of suspicious discussion. Her work email contained forty-seven messages and only six that mattered.

Elizabeth answered those six.

She picked Nana's mixing spoon up off the counter, because it was a spoon, and put it in the kitchen drawer. Then she moved it to the crock beside the stove, because the drawer felt wrong. Then she moved it back.

The choice was ridiculous.

It was still hers.

Her right hand still reached, now and then, for the coat pocket where the diary used to ride against her hip. There was nothing there. By the end of the first week it happened less often, though not never.

She didn't measure improvement by it. She unpacked another box instead.


The current curator emailed Elizabeth directly. Pathwell hadn't asked her to come. The museum wanted a witness statement from someone who had been in the room and wasn't Pathwell.

So she sat at a side table in the damaged gallery with a pen and a lined pad, writing it one-handed, while the museum made Pathwell write everything down.

This was Stansbury's favorite part.

The curator had set up a folding table and placed three stacks of paper in front of Pathwell. One was labeled PHYSICAL DAMAGE. One was labeled COLLECTION LOSS. One was labeled INCIDENT ACCOUNT.

Pathwell looked at the third stack. "That appears optimistic about the amount I have to say."

The curator looked at him. "Handwritten."

Stansbury, his burned right hand wrapped in a lighter bandage now, sat against the far wall doing his prescribed finger stretches. "I like her."

"You are not part of this meeting."

"I was injured in the incident this meeting is about. I have standing."

Elizabeth didn't look up from her pad. "You were burned at a different incident."

"It's a series," Stansbury said.

The curator pointed at the first stack. "Cases. Barriers. Flooring. Mounts. Conservation assessment. Climate-control inspection, because your discharge knocked two sensors out of calibration."

Pathwell nodded. "Paid."

"Paid is not completed."

He stopped. "Correct."

She moved to the second stack. "Daniel Vale letters. Household notebook. Medical ledger. Three accession cards by Margaret Bell. One civilian photograph. Two textile labels. All originals. All irrecoverable. You've sent us scans, photographs and old catalog entries since."

"Every copy I could find."

"Useful," the curator said. "Not returned."

"I found a letter packet from the same family." He took it out of his coat. "Henry Vale, 1882 through 1886. Similar provenance. Same family line."

The curator's face didn't change. "No."

"Same family," Pathwell said. "Same period. Genuine originals. It is better than an empty—"

He stopped. He looked at the blank space where Daniel Vale's letters had been, and then at the curator.

"No," he said.

The curator waited. At the side table, Elizabeth's pen had stopped.

Pathwell put the packet back in his coat. "They are not Daniel's."

"Correct."

"May I donate them separately?"

The curator considered him. "After provenance review. Not as replacement."

"Understood."

Stansbury's fingers stopped halfway through a stretch. Pathwell saw. "Do not look impressed."

"I am deciding whether the concussion was permanent."

"Cruel."

"Continue writing," Stansbury said.

Pathwell looked at Stansbury. "I regret bringing you."

"I drove."

"There are taxis."

"You are not allowed to optimize transportation either."

The curator looked between them. "Is this useful?"

"Rarely," Pathwell and Stansbury said together.

Elizabeth finished her statement. She didn't soften the sequence. She wrote that Shade slipped. She wrote that she told Pathwell she was fine. She wrote that she told him not to use the letter. She wrote that he used it anyway.

The curator read the first page and said, "Thank you."

That was all. Nobody asked her to mediate, or to decide what punishment would be enough, or to sit any nearer to Pathwell than the side table she'd been given while he felt bad. Elizabeth liked the museum better immediately.

On her way out she passed the empty Daniel Vale case and remembered a man who had kept every pair of socks. The letters stayed missing.

At the household-notebook display, the original was gone. A reproduction sat where it had been, clearly marked FACSIMILE, and beside it the museum had added a small temporary card:

ORIGINAL DESTROYED IN COLLECTION INCIDENT.

The reproduction still read:

SAM HOME TODAY. THIN AS A FENCE RAIL. ATE THREE EGGS.

The information survived. The page didn't.

Elizabeth stood there until that stopped feeling like something she needed to solve. Then she left the museum and bought lunch because she was hungry.


In the second week he found a supplier offering archival board at half price. He brought the catalog to the archivist and asked before buying it. The archivist said the board was chemically wrong.

Pathwell threw the catalog away.

"That hurt," Elizabeth said.

"Deeply."

"Want me to say you're doing well?"

"No," Pathwell said.

She believed him.

Milo's ankle improved enough that the broom handle went back to being a broom handle. His surviving boot stayed by his cot until Mama Baga produced a mismatched replacement from somewhere in Camp. One was brown and one was black. Milo considered this an upgrade.

Stansbury's hand healed more slowly. The dressing grew smaller and the scar grew clearer: a red uneven band across the back of his right hand that disappeared beneath the wrist. He did the stretches the healer gave him.

Pathwell never mentioned changing it again.


On the twelfth day, Camp needed a replacement archive wagon.

Pathwell found four. The archivist rejected three from photographs. The fourth was structurally sound, dry, ugly, and painted a shade of yellow that made everyone standing beside it look ill.

"Perfect," the archivist said.

Pathwell stared at him. "You cannot mean that."

"I can."

"It resembles a diseased lemon."

"It fits the cabinets."

"Color is also a dimension."

"Not one on my list."

Pathwell looked toward Mama Baga. She shrugged. "His archive."

Pathwell turned back to the wagon. Elizabeth could see an entire better plan forming in him: new paint, better suspension, a reinforced axle, possibly a roof modification no one had requested.

He rubbed both hands over his face. "Do you want it painted?" he asked the archivist.

"No."

"Suspension checked?"

"Yes."

"Axle reinforced?"

"If the wheelwright says it needs it."

"All right," Pathwell said.

The yellow wagon arrived two days later. Nobody repainted it. Milo named it The Lemon. The archivist objected, and the name survived.


Elizabeth spent less time at Camp during the second week. She went back to work. She bought groceries. She learned that one of the plants in her apartment had survived being ignored through all of this and the other had not, and she threw out the dead one.

She took a long route home one evening because the weather was good. Nothing magical happened. She liked the walk anyway.

Adventure had been difficult to separate from being dragged somewhere. A diner at four in the morning could count. A museum before opening could count. Choosing a ditch probably counted too, though she was willing to classify it under poor execution. A quiet street she had never walked before could count if she wanted it to.


At the end of the second week, Milo needed a fourth player again.

Elizabeth had come to Camp for dinner. The older woman in the knitted cap was already seated. The archivist had the deck.

Milo pointed at Pathwell. "You."

Pathwell was carrying a box of blank paper toward The Lemon. He stopped. "Me what?"

"Cards."

"I am working."

The archivist looked at the box. "Put it inside. Then cards."

"This feels coordinated," Pathwell said to Elizabeth.

"You're popular."

"Unlikely."

He put the box exactly where the archivist pointed. Then he sat.

Milo dealt. Pathwell picked up his cards. "Hearts."

"Yes."

"I know Hearts."

Milo narrowed his eyes. "Shade said that."

The table changed.

Pathwell looked at his cards. "Did he."

"He was bad," Milo said.

Elizabeth said, "Very."

Milo pointed at her. "You weren't playing."

"I observed."

"That doesn't count."

Pathwell arranged his hand. "For once, Milo and I agree on evidentiary standards."

Milo dealt the next card harder than necessary. The queen of spades appeared in Pathwell's hand. He looked at it, and then at Milo.

Milo looked innocent enough to convict himself.

Pathwell said, "No."

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You're weird."

"Correct."

They played. Pathwell lost by twenty-six points.

Milo was delighted. "Shade was better."

Pathwell looked at him. Elizabeth waited.

"Then I have work to do," Pathwell said.

Milo gathered the cards. "Again?"

Pathwell looked toward The Lemon, then at Mama Baga. "Am I done for the evening?"

Mama Baga looked offended. "I am not your supervisor."

The archivist said, "You are done."

"Again," Pathwell said.

Elizabeth stayed for the second game because she wanted to see whether he could lose worse.

He could.


Elizabeth went to see the museum reopen the damaged gallery on the eighteenth day. A temporary sign at the entrance said it was reopened, not restored.

Two repaired cases stood where broken ones had been. One remained intentionally empty; the curator had refused to fill every absence. The Daniel Vale display used facsimiles and a photograph of the original letters from before their destruction. The household notebook reproduction remained clearly labeled. Margaret Bell's surviving accession cards had been remounted beside scans of the destroyed ones.

Pathwell had paid for the glass, mounts, conservation work, environmental recalibration, and temporary exhibit printing. He had also spent two full days carrying cases, moving barriers exactly where museum staff had marked the floor, and writing an incident account so detailed that Stansbury claimed it qualified as revenge literature.

The curator didn't thank him for causing the work. She did thank him for completing the assigned work.


On the twenty-first day, Camp held no ceremony. The Lemon simply became the archive.

Blank paper went into one cabinet. Saved originals went into another. The blue road notebook returned to a padded support shelf. The surviving family letters received a new cloth wrap.

The current route copies went into a box that slid under the rack Pathwell had originally braced three inches too low. It slid out cleanly now. The archivist tested it twice.

Pathwell stood nearby holding a box of accession slips. "Satisfied?"

"No."

Pathwell looked at the box under the rack. "What now?"

"Label crooked."

Pathwell stared at the label. It was crooked by perhaps one degree.

He put down the accession slips, peeled the label off, and applied a new one. "Better?"

The archivist inspected it. "Yes."

Pathwell nodded, and that was all.

No debt vanished. The museum still had missing originals. Camp still had shelves that would never be refilled with the same things. Stansbury still had a scar. Elizabeth still had a shoulder that hurt when rain moved in. Shade was still dead. The diary was still ash. Nana's cookbook was still ash.

Pathwell had kept showing up when other people told him what needed doing.

Elizabeth didn't call that redemption. She called it evidence.

She looked toward the far end of Camp, where the road bent between the trees. The thought of going somewhere strange didn't arrive attached to somebody else's hand on the map.

Elizabeth smiled.

Not because she knew where she wanted to go yet.

Because she wanted to go.
