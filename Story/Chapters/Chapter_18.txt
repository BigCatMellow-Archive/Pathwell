Chapter Eighteen

Three weeks and four days after the archive burned, Elizabeth called Pathwell and said, "Take me to the Space Between."

There was a pause.

Not the dangerous kind.

The kind where a man with too many habitual answers was checking whether any of them had actually been requested.

"All right," he said.

Elizabeth waited.

"That's it?"

"You asked me to take you somewhere."

"Yes."

"I can take you there."

"No opinion?"

"Several."

"No better destination?"

"At least four."

"No warning that this is not ideal?"

Pathwell sighed through the phone.

"I am trying very hard to have been house-trained by consequences."

Elizabeth smiled despite herself.

"Seven tonight. My apartment."

"Outside?"

She looked toward the repaired front door.

The landlord had replaced it with something brown, heavy, and aggressively ordinary. The new deadbolt stuck when the weather was humid. Elizabeth liked it more than she expected.

"Outside," she said.

"Seven."

He hung up.

No mission.

No instructions.

No suggestion that she bring a coat, knife, heirloom, charged record, backup plan, or emotional support dumpling.

Elizabeth looked at the phone in her hand.

"Disorienting," she told the empty apartment.

The apartment did not answer.

It had gotten better about that.


Pathwell arrived at seven-oh-three.

Elizabeth made him wait until seven-oh-five because she was tying her shoe.

This was not strategy.

That was also new.

When she came downstairs he was standing beside the building entrance with his hands in his coat pockets and no visible emergency.

His face had mostly healed. The cut at his temple was a pink line now. He still moved carefully when he turned too quickly through the ribs, though he pretended otherwise with less commitment than before.

He looked at her left shoulder.

No sling.

Then looked away from it without asking for a report.

"Hello," he said.

"Hello."

"You have a functioning door."

"I do."

"I am pleased."

"You are not allowed to inspect it."

"I was not going to."

Elizabeth looked at him.

Pathwell looked offended.

"I am capable of growth."

"You tried to redesign an archive wagon nobody asked you to redesign six days ago."

"I asked first."

"And they said no."

"And the wagon remains yellow."

"Diseased lemon."

"Thank you."

They started walking.

Pathwell did not ask why she wanted to go back until they had gone three blocks.

Elizabeth noticed because the old Pathwell would have asked before saying yes and then answered the question himself somewhere between her first and second sentence.

"You're waiting," she said.

"For what?"

"For me to explain."

"I assumed you would if you wanted to."

"That sounds rehearsed."

"It has required practice."

She looked ahead.

Traffic moved through the intersection. A bus sighed at the curb. Someone was carrying flowers badly, stems down, blossoms knocking against their knee.

Ordinary city evening.

"I want to see it when nothing is chasing us," Elizabeth said.

Pathwell nodded.

That was all.

She let the silence continue.

It did not need saving.


The entrance was not the same alley.

Elizabeth had expected it to be.

Pathwell took her through the back corridor of a laundromat instead, past six dryers and a vending machine selling detergent in boxes small enough to qualify as insults.

At the end of the corridor was a utility door with an OUT OF ORDER sign taped across it.

"Is it actually out of order?" Elizabeth asked.

"Profoundly."

"For laundry?"

"For most things."

He took a narrow brass tag from his coat and held it against the center of the sign.

Nothing happened.

Elizabeth waited.

Pathwell rotated the tag.

"Orientation?"

"I resent how much joy that brought you."

Light appeared around the doorframe.

"Still counts," Elizabeth said.

"Unfortunately."

The dryer behind them buzzed.

A woman at the folding table looked up.

Pathwell opened the utility door before she could decide whether the impossible light required concern.

Old paper breathed through the opening.

Dust.

Leather.

Ink.

Binding glue.

Something bitter and herbal.

Elizabeth smiled before she meant to.

Pathwell saw.

He did not make anything of it.

That helped too.

She stepped through first.


The Space Between had not improved its coffee.

Elizabeth learned this within four minutes.

The orange cat found her within two.

The shopkeeper looked up from his ledger when she entered, took in Elizabeth, Pathwell behind her, their complete absence of blood, monsters, stolen property, handcarts, collapsed practitioners, or visibly urgent Camp orders, and frowned.

"What happened?"

Pathwell stopped.

"Nothing."

The shopkeeper's frown deepened.

"I dislike novelty."

Elizabeth walked to the counter.

The orange cat jumped onto it and sat directly on the open ledger.

The shopkeeper moved his pen.

The cat moved six inches to cover the new location.

"Administrative sabotage," Pathwell said.

"He has seniority."

Elizabeth scratched the cat behind one ear.

It allowed this without opening her hand.

"He's mellowed."

"He remembers winning," the shopkeeper said.

Elizabeth looked at the base of her thumb.

The old scratch was long gone.

"Fair."

The shopkeeper looked at Pathwell.

"Your account remains at zero."

Pathwell leaned one elbow on the counter.

"You say that as though waiting for weather."

"Three weeks is not a character trait."

"Cruel."

"Accounting."

Elizabeth laughed.

This time nobody looked surprised.

That was pleasant.


She wandered.

The first time she had been here, the shelves had pressed other people's feeling against her until she could barely tell where her own life ended and the room began.

The pressure was still there.

It had not become weaker for her.

She had become less frightened of not understanding it.

A shelf of rain-swollen journals made her chest ache with somebody else's homesickness.

She stepped away.

A bundle of school notes carried embarrassment so bright she smiled despite having no idea who had been embarrassed or why.

She did not touch them.

A charcoal drawing of three children beside a fence made her want to call someone whose number she did not have.

She kept walking.

None of it meant she was special.

That made the room better.

Everyone had left something somewhere.

Most of it would never matter to her.

Some of it could still be beautiful.

Pathwell stayed near the counter.

Not guarding her.

Not following.

Talking quietly with the shopkeeper about something involving a bridge, a debt the shopkeeper insisted belonged to a man dead since 1912, and whether inheriting someone's toolbox constituted inheriting their late fees.

Elizabeth stopped beside a narrow shelf of recipe books.

Not published cookbooks.

The other kind.

Spiral notebooks swollen with use. Index cards in tins. School copybooks with grease darkening the corners. Pages written by one hand and corrected by another and contradicted by a third.

Her body recognized the category before her mind did.

The empty place opened.

Nana's flour-stained cover.

The torn sugar-cookie page.

Chicken and dumplings.

MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH.

Then fire.

Elizabeth put her right hand in her coat pocket.

Not checking for the diary this time.

Just somewhere to put it.

A book lay flat on the end of the shelf.

Green cloth cover.

Rounded corners.

A piece of masking tape across the front in faded blue ink:

MERRITT FAMILY KITCHEN

1948—

No ending year.

Pathwell had stopped talking.

Elizabeth looked toward the counter.

He was looking at the same book.

Of course he was.

The shopkeeper followed his eyes.

"New acquisition," he said.

Pathwell crossed the aisle.

Elizabeth stayed where she was.

He stopped beside her and looked down at the cookbook without touching it.

"Strong," he said.

"I assumed."

"You can feel it?"

"No. I can see gravy on the cover."

Pathwell looked closer.

"Possibly stew."

"Important distinction."

"Historically."

The shopkeeper came around the counter carrying the ledger under one arm because the cat had claimed the workspace.

"Four generations," he said. "Consigned by the family. They copied the recipes before selling it. Nobody had room for the original and everybody wanted somebody else to store it."

Elizabeth looked at him.

"That seems sad."

"It is also extremely common."

Pathwell reached toward the book.

Stopped before touching it.

"May I?"

The shopkeeper looked at him over his glasses.

"Yes."

Pathwell opened the cover.

The first page had names written one beneath another.

Ruth Merritt.

Joanne Merritt Pike.

Ellen Pike.

Mara Pike Solis.

Different inks.

Different pressure.

Different years.

A recipe for biscuits had three measurements crossed out and a note in the margin:

RUTH LIED ABOUT THE LARD.

Another hand beneath it had added:

SHE DID NOT. YOU USED THE WRONG CUP.

Pathwell smiled.

Elizabeth leaned closer despite herself.

On the next page:

TOMATO SOUP — FOR SNOW DAYS

Under that, years later:

ALSO GOOD WHEN YOU HAVE BEEN FIRED.

Then:

NOT THAT GOOD.

Elizabeth laughed softly.

"They seem healthy."

"By family standards," Pathwell said.

He turned another page.

The smile faded.

Not because the book changed.

Because the old calculation had arrived.

Elizabeth knew the expression now.

Need.

Available thing.

Method.

Price.

Pathwell looked toward the shopkeeper.

"How much?"

The shopkeeper did not answer immediately.

Elizabeth did not either.

Pathwell noticed both silences.

His mouth tightened.

"Camp lost a family cookbook," he said.

Elizabeth looked at him.

"Camp lost Nana's cookbook."

"Yes."

The word came without defense.

He looked back at the green book.

The handwritten arguments.

The grease marks.

The names.

The years that belonged to people none of them knew.

"How much?" he asked again.

"Two hundred forty dollars," the shopkeeper said.

Pathwell searched his coat.

One pocket.

Another.

He produced thirty-two dollars, two coins Elizabeth did not recognize, a folded receipt, and a small brass washer.

The shopkeeper looked at the pile.

"No."

"The washer has history."

"Not useful history."

He pushed the coins back toward Pathwell.

"You can return with money."

Pathwell looked at the book again.

The old solution moved into place almost visibly.

"Future."

The shopkeeper's face became very still.

"Yours?"

Pathwell glanced at Elizabeth.

She said nothing.

He looked back at the ledger.

"Mine."

The shopkeeper turned the ledger toward him.

Pathwell put his hand flat on the open page.

The shop stayed ordinary for one heartbeat.

Then possibility opened around him.

Elizabeth had seen it enough times now to know what she was looking at without pretending to understand it.

Pale branching lines spread from Pathwell's shadow.

Alive.

Still capable of division.

Still his.

They crossed the carpet beneath the recipe shelf and slipped toward the ledger in thin bright forks.

Less broad than the first time she had seen them.

Not gone.

Not closed.

Possible.

The shopkeeper raised two fingers over the ledger.

He had not touched it yet.

Nothing had released.

Elizabeth felt her whole body notice that distinction.

Pathwell looked down at the cookbook.

RUTH LIED ABOUT THE LARD.

SHE DID NOT. YOU USED THE WRONG CUP.

His hand stayed on the ledger.

The branches waited.

The shopkeeper waited.

Elizabeth waited.

Pathwell closed his eyes.

Then lifted his hand.

The pale lines loosened.

For one second they seemed to widen rather than narrow.

Then they withdrew into ordinary shadow.

No knees hitting the floor.

No shaking hand.

No branches folded into the ledger.

Nothing had been spent.

The shopkeeper lowered his fingers.

Pathwell exhaled.

"No," he said.

The shopkeeper closed the ledger.

"No what?"

Pathwell touched the cookbook cover with two fingers.

Not activating it.

Just touching an object.

"It isn't Nana's."

Elizabeth looked at him.

"No."

"And Camp didn't ask for it."

"No."

Pathwell nodded.

He closed the cookbook carefully.

"Then it stays itself."

The shopkeeper took off his glasses.

"I may need to sit down."

"You are sitting spiritually."

"Do not ruin this."

Pathwell took his hand off the book.

The shopkeeper squared it on the shelf.

"I can send Camp the listing," Pathwell said.

The shopkeeper looked at him.

Pathwell corrected himself.

"I can ask whether they want the listing."

The shopkeeper put his glasses back on.

"Disturbing."

Elizabeth smiled.

Pathwell looked at the green cover one last time.

"I kept thinking resemblance was enough," he said.

Elizabeth knew they were no longer talking about cookbooks.

He looked at his empty hand.

"Shade."

She waited.

"It was my fault."

No speech followed.

No explanation of which choice.

The first prune.

The museum.

The frame.

The fire.

The fact that Shade had been forced to spend most of his short life responding to decisions Pathwell had made before asking him.

Elizabeth did not soften it.

"Yes."

Pathwell nodded.

The shopkeeper opened his ledger again.

Not to record the confession.

To continue adding numbers because some things remained none of his business.

The orange cat jumped down from the counter.

Life, apparently, remained aggressively uninterested in timing.


Elizabeth stayed another hour.

Maybe more.

The shop had no windows and too many shelves; time was difficult to track even when nobody was buying the future.

She found a box of postcards written by a woman who had apparently hated every city she visited and continued traveling anyway.

She found a child's illustrated field guide to neighborhood birds in which every bird had been assigned an inaccurate personality.

She found a stack of letters tied in blue string and chose not to learn why they were sad.

She drank half a cup of terrible coffee because she wanted to know whether it was still as bad.

It was.

Pathwell refused his.

"Coward," Elizabeth said.

"Evidence-based decision making."

"You've changed."

"Do not spread that around."

At some point she realized she had stopped waiting for something to happen.

The shop was enough.

Being there because she had asked to be there was enough.

Eventually she returned to the counter.

"How do I get out?"

The shopkeeper looked up.

"Where?"

Elizabeth considered the question.

The first time, she had been offered the sidewalk or a forest she had not chosen until the choice was finally put in front of her.

Now home was available.

Camp was available.

The museum was available through the proper route, though she had no desire to visit it after hours again.

Her apartment would still be there tomorrow.

Work would still contain email.

The plant that survived would probably continue requiring water in a manner she considered manipulative.

Elizabeth looked at Pathwell.

"What routes can you actually get us back from?"

Pathwell blinked.

"That is a surprisingly responsible version of the question."

"I contain multitudes."

He considered.

"From here? Four without making the Shopkeeper invent anything unpleasant."

"Five," the shopkeeper said.

Pathwell looked at him.

"The fifth is seasonal."

"Season began yesterday."

Pathwell frowned.

"I hate when you know things about your own shop."

"Occupationally."

Elizabeth leaned against the counter.

"Give me the five."

Pathwell did.

She listened.

The first sounded useful.

The second sounded beautiful and crowded.

The third involved a ferry schedule and therefore too much planning.

The fourth Pathwell described in great detail, which made Elizabeth suspicious.

The fifth he described badly.

"What's wrong with that one?" she asked.

"Nothing."

"You made a face."

"There are geese."

"Wild geese?"

"Technically."

"What does technically mean?"

"They have learned commerce."

Elizabeth looked at the shopkeeper.

He kept writing.

"Is that true?"

"They steal buttons."

Elizabeth looked back at Pathwell.

"That one."

"Of course."

"Problem?"

Pathwell's old answer almost arrived.

She saw it.

Then he smiled.

"No."

Elizabeth turned to the shopkeeper.

"Can we use it?"

"Yes."

"Both of us?"

"If he is coming."

Elizabeth looked at Pathwell.

She had asked him to bring her here.

That did not make the next step his.

"Do you want to come?" she asked.

Pathwell looked surprised.

Not offended.

Not amused.

Surprised.

He glanced toward the recipe shelf.

Toward the ledger.

Toward the door that had not appeared yet.

Then back at her.

"Yes."

Freely.

No debt.

No Camp order.

No blob.

No one waiting at the other end who needed him to fix anything.

At least as far as either of them knew.

Elizabeth nodded.

"Then come with me."

The shopkeeper closed the ledger.

A narrow door appeared beside the shelf of journals.

Brass handle.

Old wood.

A thin line of cold evening air underneath.

Something honked on the other side.

Pathwell's face tightened.

"They remember me."

Elizabeth put her hand on the brass handle.

"Good."

She looked back at him.

He had already stepped away from the counter.

Already chosen to follow.

Not leading.

Not being dragged.

Coming.

Pathwell stopped beside her.

"Are you ready?"

"No."

"Perfect."
