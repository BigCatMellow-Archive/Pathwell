Chapter Four

She had assumed, without quite thinking about it, that the word camp would mean something she could leave.

Camp Cunnan announced itself in woodsmoke before it showed up.

Then lantern light through the trees.

Then the sound of it — low conversation, a fiddle somewhere, the creak and settle of wagons that had been sitting long enough to start feeling permanent even if they were not.

Pathwell came down the last stretch of dirt road pulling the Space Between's handcart behind him with the offended posture of a man who had only recently learned the handcart existed.

Elizabeth walked beside it.

Nana's cookbook and her diary rode on top of the Camp books.

The shopkeeper had offered to send her back to the sidewalk. She had put them there herself instead.

That fact still felt strange enough to keep checking.

The camp was a loose ring of wagons around a central fire, with smaller fires branching off where people had decided to stay. Everything was used and kept — patched canvas, polished ironwork, rope dark with old rain. Nothing matched and almost everything had been repaired by someone who believed replacement was a personal failure.

A woman read a man's palm with the focused disinterest of someone who had done it ten thousand times and still found people mildly surprising. Someone else was mending a coat beneath a lantern. Near the fire, two children crouched over a beetle.

"It's dead," one said.

"It's practicing," said the other.

"For what?"

The second child considered this.

"Being dead."

The fiddle stopped being tuned and became music by accident.

Pathwell pulled the cart into the ring like a delivery driver who had been inconvenienced by geography.

Nobody looked surprised to see him.

That was the first alarming thing.

The second was that nobody looked particularly impressed.

A boy carrying a basket of turnips stepped around the cart without slowing. One sock had surrendered halfway down his calf. He hooked it back up with his heel, failed, and continued anyway.

An older woman lifted a folded blanket off a stool and nodded for Elizabeth to sit.

Someone handed her a tin cup.

The cup held tea, or something tea had become after a prolonged argument with bark.

"Drink," the woman said.

Elizabeth did.

It was terrible.

She looked toward Pathwell.

He had already crossed the ring toward the largest wagon, leaving the handcart in the middle of the camp as though deliveries completed themselves once he had arrived near them.

Three people immediately began unloading it without being asked.

The boy with the turnips put down his basket and joined them.

One of the men lifted a bundle of blank archival paper from the cart and made a sound of genuine relief.

"We were down to six sheets."

Pathwell turned around, walking backward now. "You see?"

The man ignored him.

An older woman in a red shawl came down the steps of the largest wagon.

She was broad and settled and moved at the pace of someone who had learned that most things were not improved by arriving at them five seconds earlier.

She looked at Pathwell the way people look at a problem they are fond of.

"Mama Baga," Pathwell said.

"You look terrible."

"I always look terrible."

"You look worse than that."

She glanced at the cart.

Then at the blank paper being carried away.

Then back at Pathwell.

"We didn't ask for this."

"You needed it."

"We did."

Pathwell smiled.

Mama Baga did not.

"You could have written first."

Pathwell looked toward the man carrying the last bundle of blank paper.

"On what?"

Mama Baga closed her eyes for one patient second.

Then she looked at Elizabeth.

"And you must be the one he's been dragging around."

"Elizabeth."

"Are you being dragged?"

Pathwell answered for her.

"No."

Mama Baga continued looking at Elizabeth.

Elizabeth looked at Pathwell.

Then at the handcart.

"Less than yesterday."

Mama Baga's mouth moved at one corner.

"I'll take it."

Pathwell looked wounded.

"You look tired," Mama Baga told Elizabeth. "Come sit somewhere that isn't a crate of commercial goods."

Elizabeth looked down.

She was still standing.

"Right."

She followed.

Pathwell did not.

That was fine.

For the first time in several hours, fine was enough.


Mama Baga's wagon was close and warm, hung with bundles of dried plants and small jars labeled in a system Elizabeth had no category for — names, numbers, marks that might have been measurements or warnings. Rolled papers leaned in corners. Quilts occupied more space than some pieces of furniture.

It smelled like tea, lanolin, smoke, and something that might once have been sage before spending thirty years inside a wagon.

"Sit before you fall over politely," Mama Baga said.

"I'm fine."

"That is not a posture."

Elizabeth sat on a wooden crate beside the wall.

It had onions in it.

The onions accepted her without comment.

Mama Baga poured more bark tea into a chipped cup and put it in Elizabeth's hands.

Her fingers were warm and dry.

She did not ask why Elizabeth's coat smelled faintly of low tide. She did not ask about the apartment or the missing front door or what kind of person follows a man like Pathwell through a wall after midnight.

She tucked the corner of a blanket away from Elizabeth's shoe.

An absurdly small kindness.

Elizabeth nearly cried again and resented the blanket for trying.

The wagon door opened behind them.

The boy with the turnips came in backward carrying Nana's cookbook in both hands.

Her diary was balanced on top.

"Where?" he asked.

"Those are mine," Elizabeth said, faster than she meant to.

The boy froze.

Mama Baga looked at her.

Elizabeth set down the tea.

"I mean — yes. Mine."

She stood and took both books.

The diary went under her arm.

The cookbook stayed in her hands.

Its cover was still flour-stained. Three rubber bands still held the spine together. One page was gone.

Everything about it looked exactly as it had ten minutes ago.

That helped.

"Thank you," she told the boy.

He nodded once and immediately forgot the interaction.

"Archivist wants the rest of the cart," he told Mama Baga.

"He has the rest of the cart."

"He says Pathwell has organized it."

Mama Baga looked toward the door.

"That is different."

The boy nodded solemnly, as though this distinction had saved lives before, and disappeared.

Elizabeth sat again with the cookbook on her knees.

Her thumb found the torn edge of the missing sugar-cookie page.

Across the wagon, someone breathed in sharply.

Elizabeth looked up.

She had not noticed the cot at the back.

A young woman lay beneath two blankets, maybe twenty-five, maybe younger. Her skin had the washed-out color of someone whose body had spent too long deciding which systems were still essential. One arm rested on top of the blankets. The fingers were curled and still.

Beside her sat a slight woman with her sleeves rolled past the elbows.

Camp healer, Elizabeth guessed before anyone said it.

The healer pressed two fingers to the woman's wrist, waited, then moved a hand against her ribs.

The woman on the cot breathed as though breathing were the only task she had agreed to perform.

"What happened?" Elizabeth asked.

"Wagon slipped on the north grade," Mama Baga said. "She was between it and a tree."

Elizabeth looked at the young woman again.

"That sounds worse than she looks."

"It was."

The healer sat back.

"I can keep her where she is," she said. "I don't have enough here to move her forward."

Mama Baga nodded.

Nobody looked at Elizabeth.

That somehow made the cookbook heavier.

The healer noticed it only when Elizabeth shifted her hands.

Her eyes moved to the cover.

Then to Elizabeth.

"Family?"

Elizabeth looked down.

"Yes."

"Handwritten?"

"Yes."

The healer did not ask for it.

She looked back at the woman on the cot.

Elizabeth waited for Pathwell to appear in the doorway and turn the whole thing into a problem he had already solved.

He did not.

The young woman took one shallow breath.

Then another.

Elizabeth opened the cookbook.

The pages made the soft dry sound of something that had been opened thousands of times.

Nana Jones's handwriting changed depending on what she had been doing when she wrote. Careful at the top of a page. Sloped harder toward the right by the ingredients. Crowded into margins whenever she remembered something after the fact.

"Did she make anything when someone was sick?" the healer asked.

Elizabeth almost said sugar cookies.

The answer hurt before she reached it.

She turned three pages instead.

Chicken and dumplings.

Of course.

There was a grease mark beside the title and a penciled note in Nana's handwriting beneath the broth:

MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH.

Elizabeth laughed once through her nose.

Mama Baga looked at her.

"She believed soup was an argument you could win," Elizabeth said.

"Was she right?"

"Frequently."

The young woman on the cot breathed in.

Carefully.

Out.

Elizabeth ran one finger down the edge of the recipe.

A different memory this time.

Not twelve.

Seventeen, maybe. Sitting at Nana's table with the flu, furious at being sick and more furious that Nana had been right about it. A bowl between both hands. Steam in her face. Nana pretending not to watch until Elizabeth took the first bite.

The page was ordinary paper.

That was the problem with important things. They almost always were.

Elizabeth closed the book.

"No," she said.

Mama Baga did not move.

The healer did not move.

Elizabeth looked at the woman on the cot.

Then opened the book again.

"No," she repeated, quieter. "Sorry. That was for me."

She held the cookbook out toward the healer.

"The whole book."

The healer looked at her.

"We only need the page."

"I know."

Elizabeth's arms remained extended.

"If I keep holding the rest, I'm going to spend the next ten minutes finding a reason this page is the wrong one."

Mama Baga's eyes stayed on her.

Elizabeth swallowed.

"The whole book."

Mama Baga took it before the healer could.

Not quickly.

Both hands under the spine.

Receiving the weight rather than examining it.

For one second Elizabeth nearly pulled it back.

She did not.

Mama Baga passed it to the healer.

The book left Elizabeth's hands.

That was the choice.

Everything afterward was consequence.

The healer laid the cookbook beside the young woman and found the chicken-and-dumplings page.

She read it once.

Then again.

She put one hand flat on the paper and the other lightly against the woman's sternum.

The ink warmed.

No flash.

No burst of gold.

The page simply began to give off the particular heat of a kitchen in winter.

Broth.

Pepper.

Flour dust against the back of a hand.

The dense soft smell of dumplings when the lid first came off.

Elizabeth heard someone behind her breathe in and realized Mama Baga had smelled it too.

The young woman's shoulders loosened.

The healer said something too quiet to catch.

Nana's handwriting darkened on the page.

Then thinned.

Then disappeared one line at a time.

The paper itself followed.

Not burned.

Spent.

The woman on the cot took a breath deep enough to move the blankets.

Then another.

Her fingers uncurled.

The healer kept her hand in place for several seconds after the page was gone.

Nobody spoke.

Finally she sat back.

"She'll sleep."

Mama Baga nodded.

"And then?" Elizabeth asked.

The healer looked at the woman.

"Then she gets to be annoyed about being alive for a while."

Elizabeth let out a breath.

"That seems fair."

The healer closed Nana's cookbook.

A clean gap remained where the page had been.

Nothing glittered in the air.

Nothing clung to the blankets or steps.

The room smelled like chicken and dumplings for another few seconds and then became a wagon again.

Mama Baga put a hand briefly on Elizabeth's shoulder.

Not gratitude.

Not consolation.

Just contact.

Elizabeth stared at the cookbook.

She expected the healer to hand it back.

The expectation arrived automatically, the way you hold out your hand for change after paying.

Then Elizabeth remembered what she had said.

The whole book.

The healer looked at Mama Baga.

"Archive?"

Mama Baga looked at Elizabeth.

Elizabeth's hand was still halfway raised.

She lowered it.

"Archive," she said.

The word felt different in her mouth than it had in the Space Between.


Outside, Camp had continued having an evening.

This felt rude and was probably healthy.

The fiddle had acquired a second fiddle. Somebody had started cooking onions near the central fire, which meant the entire camp smelled briefly like a better decision than bark tea.

Pathwell was crouched beside the handcart, examining one of its wheels.

Elizabeth stopped beside him.

"She'll be fine," he said without looking up.

"I know."

"That was—"

"I know."

He nodded.

"This wheel is new."

Elizabeth looked at it.

"Newer," Pathwell amended. "Relatively."

She sat on the edge of the cart.

For a while they looked at the wheel because it was available and did not require either of them to say anything useful.

Then the boy with the turnips appeared again.

He was carrying Nana's cookbook now, wrapped loosely in a square of clean muslin.

Elizabeth stood before she meant to.

The boy stopped.

"Archive," he said.

"I know."

He waited.

Elizabeth looked at the outline of the cookbook beneath the cloth.

One cracked spine.

Two missing pages now.

Hundreds left.

Still Nana's.

No longer hers.

She could not decide whether that distinction made the loss smaller or worse.

"I'm going that way," she said.

The boy shrugged, which apparently counted as permission.

He turned.

Elizabeth followed him.

Pathwell stayed by the wheel.

For once, he did not decide what the moment needed.


The archive wagon sat just inside the outer ring of Camp, close enough to the central fire that people could reach it without thinking and far enough away that nobody had to sleep beside several generations of other people's feelings.

It was larger than Mama Baga's wagon and considerably less decorative.

The door stood open.

Inside, shallow drawers climbed both walls. Shelves held wrapped books, boxes, bundles of letters, ledgers, loose paper between boards, recipe notebooks, notebooks that had once been ordinary school notebooks, and a child's drawing of a horse whose legs suggested the artist had been working from rumor.

Nothing glowed.

Nothing announced itself as important.

The boy stepped inside.

"New one," he said.

A man at a narrow desk did not look up.

"Everything is new when you are twelve."

"I'm eleven."

"That explains the filing."

The boy looked at Elizabeth.

"Archivist," he said, as though this explained the man.

The archivist finished the line he was writing before looking up.

He was somewhere past sixty and had the posture of a person who had spent most of those years leaning over other people's handwriting.

His eyes went to the wrapped cookbook.

"Donated?"

The boy pointed at Elizabeth.

The archivist looked at her.

Elizabeth nodded.

"Elizabeth."

"Family name?"

She gave it.

He wrote it down.

"Original writer?"

"My grandmother. Nana Jones."

"Jones first name?"

Elizabeth opened her mouth.

Stopped.

She had called her Nana for so long that for one terrible second the actual name did not come.

Then it did.

She told him.

The archivist wrote that down too.

"Used tonight?"

"One page."

"Before tonight?"

Elizabeth looked at the boy.

He was losing interest rapidly.

"One page."

The archivist nodded and added two small marks in the margin.

Then he unwrapped the book.

His hands were clean.

That mattered to Elizabeth more than it should have.

He opened the cover, looked at the spine, checked the two missing places, then wrapped the book again in fresh paper that did not quite cover the grease stains.

"Reserve shelf," he told the boy.

The boy took the book back.

Elizabeth watched him carry it three steps into the wagon and put it on a waist-high shelf near the front.

Not hidden.

Not displayed.

Available.

A woman appeared at the archive door holding a narrow blue notebook.

"Tell Olan he was wrong," she said.

The archivist did not look up. "About which thing?"

"The creek crossing."

"That narrows it."

She set the notebook on his desk and opened it to a page marked by a strip of cloth.

The handwriting changed three times down the page.

Dates. Rainfall. Notes about a bridge. A line written sideways in the margin: DO NOT TRUST THE SOUTH BANK AFTER THREE DAYS OF RAIN, NO MATTER WHAT HESS SAYS.

The woman tapped it.

"Three days."

The archivist read the line.

"Hess has been dead twenty-two years."

"He'd still argue."

"That is true."

He turned the notebook around.

"Take it back to Olan. Then bring it back here."

The woman tucked it under her arm.

"I always bring it back."

The archivist looked pointedly at the boy.

The boy had found a loose button on the floor and was testing whether it rolled straight.

"It wasn't me," he said.

"No one accused you."

"You were going to."

The archivist sighed.

The woman left with the blue road book.

Elizabeth looked at the shelves again.

Not a vault.

Not a shrine.

People came in because they needed to know where the creek flooded. Because someone had written it down. Because someone else had kept the writing after the person was gone.

The archivist was already filling out another line.

"What happens to it?" Elizabeth asked.

He looked up.

She nodded toward Nana's cookbook.

"Whatever happens to any record people still need."

"That is not very specific."

"Specificity is expensive."

Elizabeth stared at him.

The corner of his mouth moved.

"You keep the original," he said. "Someone may copy the recipes for information. The copy won't be the same thing."

"I know."

He nodded once, accepting that she did.

"If a page is needed badly enough and fits the work, a healer may request it. If it survives long enough, someone may spill tea on it. Someone may argue about a measurement. Someone may cook from it. Someone may not touch it for thirty years."

Elizabeth looked at the reserve shelf.

"So that's it."

"No."

The archivist dipped his pen.

"That is generally the opposite of an archive."

The boy laughed.

Elizabeth did too.

Quietly.

The archivist went back to his ledger.

The moment was over because apparently archives also had opinions about sentimentality.

Elizabeth stepped outside.

The fiddle had changed songs.

The boy ran past her toward the central fire, one sock still down, the loose button forgotten.

Behind her, Nana's cookbook stayed on the shelf.

For the first time since the apartment, Elizabeth walked away from it on purpose.


Pathwell was no longer by the handcart.

She found him at the edge of Camp looking into the trees.

Not theatrically.

Still.

The same kind of still he had gone in her apartment hallway before declaring the place marked.

Mama Baga stood beside him with her arms folded.

"It was already at the door before you used the cookbook?" she asked.

"Yes."

"And when the working broke?"

"It changed targets. Fresh waste. That part made sense."

"The first part didn't."

"No."

Pathwell looked toward the trees again.

Mama Baga watched him for a moment.

"Stansbury."

Pathwell sighed.

"I know."

"You should have gone to him before this."

"I had a delivery."

"You appointed yourself a delivery."

"They were down to six sheets."

"That does not make you less annoying."

"It makes me correct."

Mama Baga reached up and straightened Pathwell's collar as though he were twelve and badly assembled.

"There it is."

Elizabeth came closer.

"Who's Stansbury?"

Pathwell looked at Mama Baga.

Mama Baga looked at Pathwell.

"My brother," Pathwell said.

Elizabeth waited.

"That's all I get?"

"For now."

"Older or younger?"

Pathwell's expression soured.

Mama Baga smiled.

"Older."

"Traitor."

"Go see your brother."

Pathwell looked once more into the trees.

The faint unease under his joking did not leave.

"Fine."

He turned to Elizabeth.

"You can stay here."

The offer surprised both of them.

Elizabeth looked back toward the central fire.

Someone had found the boy's turnips and was peeling them badly. The fiddle kept going. The woman with the blue road book was arguing with a man near a wagon, waving the notebook at him like legal evidence.

The archive door stood open.

Nana's cookbook was inside.

For one minute the idea of staying felt possible.

Then Elizabeth looked at Pathwell.

Not because he had her things.

He didn't.

Not because something was chasing her.

Nothing was.

She wanted to know why something impossible had come through her door before the magic went wrong.

She wanted to know why Pathwell had gone still when he thought about it.

And, against better judgment, she wanted to meet the brother whose existence could make him look personally offended.

"I'm coming."

Pathwell nodded as if he had expected that.

Elizabeth pointed at him.

"Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Look pleased with yourself."

"I wasn't."

"You were about to."

Mama Baga laughed behind them.

Pathwell started down the path.

Elizabeth followed.

Behind her, Camp Cunnan kept being a place without asking permission.
