Chapter Four

She had assumed, without quite thinking about it, that the word camp would mean something she could leave.

Camp Cunnan announced itself in woodsmoke before it showed up.

Then lantern light through the trees.

Pathwell came down the last stretch of dirt road pulling the Space Between's handcart behind him with the offended posture of a man who had only recently learned the handcart existed.

Elizabeth walked beside it.

Nana's cookbook and her diary rode on top of the Camp books.

She had put them there herself, after the shopkeeper had offered to send her back to the sidewalk.

That fact still felt strange enough to keep checking.

Where the road gave out, a path went on into the trees, and Pathwell went with it without slowing down, the cart bucking over roots. Elizabeth kept one hand on her books until something sharp worked its way into her shoe. Her shoes had been bought for an office. She stopped to shake it out.

Pathwell didn't look back. By the time she'd got the shoe back on, the cart's rattle had gone on ahead of her, the lantern light had dropped out of sight behind the slope, and the path had split around a tree. She picked a side. Her books were on that cart.

"Pathwell!"

Nothing. She grumbled under her breath. "Darn it, where are you?" Then, louder: "Pathwell!"

The sounds of the forest were the only response.

She made her way around a large oak, and her foot caught in the undergrowth. She wiggled it free and took one step forward, just enough for her foot to meet a tree root, followed by another greeting with the ground. The taste of dirt kissed her lips.

She lay there for a moment, frustrated. Annoyed.

A hand took her arm and pulled her up onto her knees.

"Now, you really do have to be careful, young lady. These trees are very spiteful."

A kind-faced old woman with a lantern was looking down at her, a red shawl over a white linen shirt and a scarf over her head. Her skin was sun-cured and a little wrinkled. "Where are you off to in such a hurry?"

"I was looking for my friend," Elizabeth said, as something between a confession and a complaint.

"Oh, you're Pathwell's friend?" The old woman sounded surprised. "He is waiting for you."

Elizabeth got the rest of the way to her feet, brushing off her clothes. "Where is he?"

"He said you would say that." The old woman smiled gently.

"Where are we?"

"He said you would say that too." She laughed lightly. "This way, if you please. Just follow me."

She led Elizabeth through the trees, around stumps, over fallen logs and between rocks, and she never slowed down. Where a trunk lay across the path as tall as Elizabeth, the old woman hopped up and over it as if it were a curb.

A short hike brought them to a clearing of circled wagons, and they came into the ring between two of them. A fiddle was playing somewhere, and smoke rose from the center of the ring. The smell of something roasting. The sound of wood being chopped.

Through the gaps between the wagons Elizabeth caught the camp a piece at a time. A man in an old blazer with no shirt underneath, playing a fiddle that was missing a string. A boar turning on a spit over the coals, a small boy leaning his whole weight into the crank. Children playing tag between the tents. A woman reading a man's palm with the focused disinterest of someone who had done it ten thousand times and still found people mildly surprising. People around a table, laughing at something.

The camp was a loose ring of wagons around a central fire, with smaller fires branching off where people had decided to stay. Everything was used and kept: patched canvas, polished ironwork, rope dark with old rain. Nothing matched, and almost everything had been repaired by someone who believed replacement was a personal failure.

Near the fire, two children crouched over a beetle.

"It's dead," one said.

"It's practicing," said the other.

"For what?"

The second child considered this. "Being dead."

Pathwell had beaten her there. The handcart stood in the middle of the ring, and three people were already unloading it without being asked. A boy carrying a basket of turnips stepped around it without slowing. One sock had surrendered halfway down his calf. He hooked it back up with his heel, failed, and carried on anyway. Then he put the turnips down and joined in.

The old woman set her lantern down, went to the cart and got her shoulder under the crate tied with twine, the one Pathwell had loaded with both hands and a great deal of complaining. She stood up with it. There was no effort in it, no sound of strain. She simply picked it up and was on her way.

One of the men lifted a bundle of blank archival paper and made a sound of genuine relief. "We were down to six sheets."

Pathwell turned around, walking backward. "You see?"

The man ignored him.

The old woman came back without the crate and looked Pathwell over properly, now that there was firelight. "You look terrible."

"I always look terrible."

"You look worse than that."

She glanced at the cart, then at the blank paper being carried away, then back at Pathwell.

"We did not ask for this."

"You needed it."

"We did."

Pathwell smiled. The old woman didn't.

"You could have written first."

Pathwell looked toward the man carrying the last bundle of blank paper. "On what?"

The old woman closed her eyes for one patient second.

Elizabeth had reached him by then. "You just left me in the woods," she said through her teeth.

"I did no such thing." He put an arm around her shoulders and turned her to face the old woman, as if presenting them to each other. "I sent someone to get you. She found you, didn't she? Thank you, Mama Baga. Elizabeth here would be figuratively, and quite literally, lost without you. She's just a little nervous after the bear."

"There was a bear!?"

"Not if you didn't see her." He swept his free arm out at the camp. "Welcome, Elizabeth, to Camp Cunnan, home of the Cunning Folk. Dwelling of charmers, blessers, wizards and witches. Abode of the spaewives and the gwyllion. Refuge for all manner of oracles, tarot readers, bone casters and druids. Haven to the mambos. Did I say runemasters?"

"You did not."

"Runemasters! Dwelling for all manner of fortune-tellers, prophets, mystics and diviners. Sanctuary, alcove, holdfast and haunt to many of the more spiritually attuned."

"And the occasional no-good thief," Mama Baga said.

Pathwell turned back to her, smiling. "Oh, please. I was only ever no good at being a thief."

Elizabeth stepped out from under his arm. "This is your home?"

He turned his palm up. "Home is such a permanent term. I prefer… layover."

"Humph," said Mama Baga. Then she looked at Elizabeth properly. "And you must be the one he has been dragging around. Are you being dragged?"

Pathwell answered for her. "No."

Mama Baga kept looking at Elizabeth.

Elizabeth looked at Pathwell, then at the handcart. "Less than I was."

Mama Baga's mouth moved at one corner. "I will take it." She nodded toward her wagon. "You look tired. Come with me."

Elizabeth followed. Pathwell didn't.


Mama Baga's wagon was close and warm, hung with bundles of dried plants and small jars labeled in a system Elizabeth had no category for: names, numbers, marks that might have been measurements or warnings. Rolled papers leaned in corners. Quilts took up more space than some of the furniture, and a curtain was half drawn across the back.

It smelled like tea, lanolin, smoke, and something that might once have been sage before spending thirty years inside a wagon.

"Sit before you fall over politely," Mama Baga said.

"I'm fine."

"That is not a posture."

Elizabeth sat on a wooden crate beside the wall. It had onions in it. The onions accepted her without comment.

Mama Baga poured something into a chipped cup and put it in Elizabeth's hands. It was tea, or something tea had become after a long argument with bark.

"Drink."

Elizabeth did. It was terrible.

Mama Baga didn't ask why Elizabeth's coat smelled faintly of low tide. She didn't ask about the apartment, or the missing front door, or what kind of person follows a man like Pathwell through a wall after midnight.

She tucked the corner of a blanket away from Elizabeth's shoe.

Elizabeth nearly cried again and resented the blanket for trying.

The wagon door opened behind them. The boy with the turnips came in backward, carrying Nana's cookbook in both hands, her diary balanced on top.

"Where?" he asked.

"Those are mine," Elizabeth said, faster than she meant to.

The boy froze. Mama Baga looked at her.

Elizabeth set down the tea. "I mean — yes. Mine."

She stood and took both books. The diary went under her arm. The cookbook stayed in her hands. Its cover was still flour-stained. Three rubber bands still held the spine together. One page was gone. Everything about it looked exactly as it had ten minutes ago, and that helped.

"Thank you," she told the boy.

He nodded once and immediately forgot the interaction. "Archivist wants the rest of the cart," he told Mama Baga.

"The archivist has the rest of the cart."

"He says Pathwell has organized it."

Mama Baga looked toward the door. "That is different."

The boy nodded solemnly, as though this distinction had saved lives before, and disappeared.

Elizabeth sat again with the cookbook on her knees. Her thumb found the torn edge of the missing sugar-cookie page.

Across the wagon, someone breathed in sharply.

Elizabeth looked up. Behind the curtain was a cot she hadn't noticed.

A young woman lay beneath two blankets, maybe twenty-five, maybe younger. Her skin had the washed-out color of someone whose body had spent too long deciding which systems were still essential. One arm rested on top of the blankets. The fingers were curled and still.

Beside her sat a slight woman with her sleeves rolled past the elbows. Camp healer, Elizabeth guessed before anyone said it.

The healer pressed two fingers to the woman's wrist, waited, then moved a hand against her ribs. The woman on the cot breathed as though breathing were the only task she had agreed to perform.

"What happened?" Elizabeth asked.

"Wagon slipped on the north grade," Mama Baga said. "She was between it and a tree."

Elizabeth looked at the young woman again. "That sounds worse than she looks."

"It was."

The healer sat back. "I can keep her where she is. What we had that would help, I've already used. I don't have enough here to move her forward."

Mama Baga nodded.

Nobody looked at Elizabeth. Somehow that made the cookbook heavier.

The healer noticed it only when Elizabeth shifted her hands. Her eyes moved to the cover, then to Elizabeth.

"Family?"

Elizabeth looked down. "Yes."

"Handwritten?"

"Yes."

The healer didn't ask for it. She looked back at the woman on the cot.

Elizabeth waited for Pathwell to appear in the doorway and turn the whole thing into a problem he had already solved.

He didn't.

The young woman took one shallow breath. Then another.

Elizabeth opened the cookbook. The pages made the soft, dry sound of something that had been opened thousands of times.

Nana Jones's handwriting changed depending on what she'd been doing when she wrote. Careful at the top of a page. Sloped harder toward the right by the ingredients. Crowded into the margins whenever she remembered something after the fact.

"Did she make anything when someone was sick?" the healer asked.

Elizabeth almost said sugar cookies. The answer hurt before she reached it.

She turned three pages instead.

Chicken and dumplings. Of course.

There was a grease mark beside the title and a penciled note in Nana's handwriting beneath the broth:

MORE IF THEY'RE SICK. THEY WON'T EAT ENOUGH.

Elizabeth laughed once through her nose.

Mama Baga looked at her.

"She believed soup was an argument you could win," Elizabeth said.

"Was she right?"

"Frequently."

The young woman on the cot breathed in, carefully, and out.

Elizabeth ran one finger down the edge of the recipe.

A different memory this time. Not twelve. Seventeen, maybe, sitting at Nana's table with the flu, furious at being sick and more furious that Nana had been right about it. A bowl between both hands. Steam in her face. Nana pretending not to watch until Elizabeth took the first bite.

The page was ordinary paper.

Elizabeth closed the book. "No," she said.

Mama Baga didn't move. The healer didn't move.

Elizabeth looked at the woman on the cot. Then she opened the book again.

"No," she repeated, quieter. "Sorry. That was for me."

She held the cookbook out toward the healer.

"The whole book."

The healer looked at her. "We only need the page."

"I know." Elizabeth's arms stayed out. "If I keep holding the rest, I'm going to spend the next ten minutes finding a reason this page is the wrong one."

Mama Baga's eyes stayed on her.

Elizabeth swallowed. "The whole book."

Mama Baga took it before the healer could. Not quickly. Both hands under the spine, receiving the weight rather than examining it.

For one second Elizabeth nearly pulled it back.

She didn't.

Mama Baga passed it to the healer, and the book left Elizabeth's hands.

That was the choice.

Everything afterward was consequence.

The healer laid the cookbook beside the young woman and found the chicken-and-dumplings page. She read it once. Then again. She put one hand flat on the paper and the other lightly against the woman's sternum.

The ink warmed.

No flash. No burst of gold. The page simply began to give off the particular heat of a kitchen in winter.

Broth. Pepper. Flour dust against the back of a hand. The dense, soft smell of dumplings when the lid first came off.

Elizabeth heard Mama Baga breathe in behind her. She'd smelled it too.

The young woman's shoulders loosened. The healer said something too quiet to catch.

Nana's handwriting darkened on the page. Then thinned. Then disappeared one line at a time, and the paper itself followed.

Not burned. Spent.

The woman on the cot took a breath deep enough to move the blankets. Then another. Her fingers uncurled.

The healer kept her hand in place for several seconds after the page was gone. Nobody spoke.

Finally she sat back. "She'll sleep."

Mama Baga nodded.

"And then?" Elizabeth asked.

The healer looked at the woman. "Then she wakes up sore and hungry."

Elizabeth let out a breath. "That seems fair."

The healer closed Nana's cookbook. A clean gap remained where the page had been. Nothing glittered in the air. Nothing clung to the blankets or the steps. The room smelled like chicken and dumplings for a few more seconds, and then it was a wagon again.

Mama Baga put a hand on Elizabeth's shoulder and left it there a moment.

Elizabeth stared at the cookbook. She expected the healer to hand it back. The expectation arrived automatically, the way you hold out your hand for change after paying.

Then she remembered what she'd said.

The whole book.

The healer looked at Mama Baga. "Archive?"

Mama Baga looked at Elizabeth.

Elizabeth's hand was still halfway raised. She lowered it.

"Archive," she said.

The healer tucked Nana's cookbook under her arm and went out to find someone to take it.

Mama Baga looked at Elizabeth a moment longer. Then at the coat, and at the pajama legs showing beneath it, which Elizabeth had been trying not to think about since the lobby of her building.

She went to a chest under the quilts and came back with an armful of clothes. "Try these. Take what you like."

Elizabeth looked at the pile, then at her.

Mama Baga hung a quilt from a hook to make a corner, and put the clothes into Elizabeth's hands.

When Elizabeth came out from behind the quilt, she was dressed in a mix of pastel linen, a short blazer and a thin gold chain, with a bandana holding her hair back. Her own coat went back on over the top. Her pajamas were balled in one hand.

Mama Baga clapped her hands together once. "Better."

Elizabeth looked down at herself. Nothing matched. It was the most comfortable she'd been all night.

She left the pajamas folded on the onion crate.


Outside, Camp was still up.

The fiddle had acquired a second fiddle. Somebody had started cooking onions near the central fire, which meant the whole camp smelled briefly like a better decision than bark tea.

Pathwell was crouched beside the handcart, examining one of its wheels.

Elizabeth stopped beside him.

Word had gotten there before her. "She'll be fine," he said, eyes on the wheel.

"I know."

"That was—"

"I know."

He nodded. "This wheel is new."

Elizabeth looked at it.

"Newer," Pathwell amended. "Relatively."

She sat on the edge of the cart. For a while they looked at the wheel, because it was there and didn't require either of them to say anything useful.

Then the boy with the turnips appeared again, carrying Nana's cookbook wrapped loosely in a square of clean muslin. "Archive," he said.

"I'm going that way," Elizabeth said.

The boy shrugged, which apparently counted as permission, and turned. Elizabeth followed him, the diary still under her arm.

Pathwell stayed by the wheel.


The archive was one of the nearest wagons on the ring, close enough to the central fire that people could reach it without thinking and far enough away that nobody had to sleep beside several generations of other people's feelings. It was larger than Mama Baga's wagon and considerably less decorative.

The door stood open. Inside, shallow drawers climbed both walls. Shelves held wrapped books, boxes, bundles of letters, ledgers, loose paper between boards, recipe notebooks, notebooks that had once been ordinary school notebooks, and a child's drawing of a horse whose legs suggested the artist had been working from rumor.

Nothing in it glowed.

The boy stepped inside. "New one," he said.

A man at a narrow desk didn't look up. "Everything is new when you are twelve."

"I'm eleven."

"That explains the filing."

The boy looked at Elizabeth. "Archivist," he said, as though this explained the man.

The archivist finished the line he was writing before he looked up. He was somewhere past sixty and had the stoop of a person who'd spent most of those years leaning over other people's handwriting. His eyes went to the wrapped cookbook.

"Donated?"

The boy pointed at Elizabeth. The archivist looked at her, and she nodded.

"Elizabeth."

"Family name?"

She gave it. He wrote it down.

"Original writer?"

"My grandmother. Nana Jones."

"Jones first name?"

Elizabeth opened her mouth and stopped. She'd called her Nana for so long that for one terrible second the actual name didn't come.

Then it did. She told him, and the archivist wrote that down too.

"Pages used?"

"One tonight."

"Before that?"

Elizabeth looked at the boy, who was losing interest rapidly. "One. Also tonight."

The archivist nodded and added two small marks in the margin. Then he unwrapped the book. His hands were clean. That mattered to Elizabeth more than it should have.

He opened the cover, looked at the spine, checked the two missing places, then wrapped the book again in fresh paper that didn't quite cover the grease stains.

"Reserve shelf," he told the boy.

The boy took the book back. Elizabeth watched him carry it three steps into the wagon and put it on a waist-high shelf near the front.

Not hidden. Not displayed. Available.

A woman appeared at the archive door holding a narrow blue notebook. "Tell Olan he was wrong."

The archivist didn't look up. "About which thing?"

"The creek crossing."

"That narrows it."

She set the notebook on his desk and opened it to a page marked with a strip of cloth. The handwriting changed three times down the page. Dates. Rainfall. Notes about a bridge. A line written sideways in the margin: DO NOT TRUST THE SOUTH BANK AFTER THREE DAYS OF RAIN, NO MATTER WHAT HESS SAYS.

The woman tapped it. "Three days."

The archivist read the line. "Hess has been dead twenty-two years."

"He'd still argue."

"That is true." He turned the notebook around. "Take it back to Olan. Then bring it back here."

The woman tucked it under her arm. "I always bring it back."

The archivist looked pointedly at the boy, who had found a loose button on the floor and was testing whether it rolled straight.

"It wasn't me," the boy said.

"No one accused you."

"You were going to."

The archivist sighed. The woman left with the blue road book.

Elizabeth looked at the shelves again. People came in because they needed to know where the creek flooded. Because someone had written it down. Because someone else had kept the writing after the person was gone.

The archivist was already filling in another line.

"What happens to it?" Elizabeth asked.

He looked up. She nodded toward Nana's cookbook.

"Whatever happens to any record people still need."

"That's not very specific."

"Specificity is expensive."

Elizabeth stared at him. He almost smiled.

"We keep the original," he said. "Someone may copy the recipes for information. The copy won't be the same thing."

"I know."

He nodded once, accepting that she did. "If a page is needed badly enough and fits the work, a healer may request it. If it survives long enough, someone may spill tea on it. Someone may argue about a measurement. Someone may cook from it. Someone may not touch it for thirty years."

Elizabeth looked at the reserve shelf. "So that's it."

"No." The archivist dipped his pen. "That is generally the opposite of an archive."

The boy laughed. Elizabeth did too, quietly.

The archivist returned to his ledger, and Elizabeth stepped outside. The fiddle had changed songs. The boy ran past her toward the central fire, one sock still down, the loose button forgotten.

Behind her, Nana's cookbook stayed on the shelf, and Elizabeth didn't go back for it.


Pathwell was no longer by the handcart.

She found him at the edge of Camp, looking into the trees. Not theatrically. He'd gone still, the way he had in her apartment hallway before declaring the place marked.

Mama Baga stood beside him with her arms folded, watching it too.

"It was already at the door before you used the cookbook?" she asked.

"Yes."

"And when the working broke?"

"It changed targets. Fresh waste. That part made sense."

"The first part did not."

"No."

Pathwell looked toward the trees again. Mama Baga watched him for a moment.

"Stansbury."

Pathwell sighed. "I know."

"You should have gone to him before this."

"I had a delivery."

"You appointed yourself a delivery."

"They were down to six sheets."

"That does not make you less annoying."

"It makes me correct."

Mama Baga reached up and straightened Pathwell's collar as though he were twelve and badly assembled. "There it is."

Elizabeth came closer. "Who's Stansbury?"

Pathwell looked at Mama Baga. Mama Baga looked at Pathwell.

"My brother," Pathwell said.

Elizabeth waited. "That's all I get?"

"For now."

"Older or younger?"

Pathwell's expression soured. Mama Baga smiled.

"Older," she said.

"Traitor."

"Go see your brother."

Pathwell looked once more into the trees. The unease under his joking didn't leave. "Fine."

He turned to Elizabeth. "You can stay here."

The offer surprised both of them.

Elizabeth looked back toward the central fire. Someone had found the boy's turnips and was peeling them badly. The fiddle kept going. The woman with the blue road book was arguing with a man near a wagon, waving the notebook at him like legal evidence. The archive door stood open, and Nana's cookbook was inside.

For one minute the idea of staying felt possible. Back in the city, her meeting was still at nine. She noticed she hadn't thought about it in an hour.

Then Elizabeth looked at Pathwell.

Not because he had her things. He didn't.

She wanted to know why something impossible had come through her door before the magic went wrong. And, against her better judgment, she wanted to meet the brother whose existence could make him look personally offended.

"I'm coming."

Pathwell nodded as if he'd expected that.

Elizabeth pointed at him. "Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Look pleased with yourself."

"I wasn't."

"You were about to."

Mama Baga laughed behind them.

Pathwell started down the path. Elizabeth followed.

Behind her, Camp Cunnan kept being a place without asking permission.
