Chapter Four

She had assumed, without quite thinking about it, that the word camp would mean something she could leave.

Camp Cunnan announced itself in woodsmoke before it showed up. Then lantern light through the trees, and then the sound of it — low conversation, a fiddle somewhere, the creak and settle of wagons that had been sitting long enough to start feeling permanent.

Pathwell walked in like he owned the place, which seemed to be how he walked everywhere. Elizabeth followed with the cookbook held against her ribs.

The camp was a loose ring of wagons around a central fire, with smaller fires branching off where people had decided to stay. Everything was used and kept — patched canvas, polished ironwork, rope dark with old rain. The people around the fires were the same way. A woman read a man's palm with the focused disinterest of someone who had done it ten thousand times and still found it vaguely interesting. A man wrote something at speed, his whole body bent over the page.

From the far side of the ring, two people were arguing with the particular cadence of people who had been at this specific argument for years and had no intention of stopping. Neither of them looked up.

Nobody looked up when Pathwell walked through.

That was the first alarming thing about it.

The second was that nobody looked surprised by Elizabeth either.

A boy carrying a basket of turnips moved around her without slowing. An older woman lifted the corner of a blanket off a stool and nodded at it as if Elizabeth had ordered a seat in advance. Someone near the fire passed her a tin cup. The cup held tea, or something tea had become after a long argument with bark.

"Drink," the older woman said.

Elizabeth took it because refusing seemed harder than drinking bark.

Pathwell had already crossed the ring, his coat swinging behind him, his attention on the largest wagon. He did not check whether she followed. He did not need to. He had the awful confidence of a man who believed roads continued because he had started walking on them.

Elizabeth sat on the stool. The fire was close enough that one side of her face was warm. She did not move closer.

For one minute, possibly two, no one needed anything from her.

The cookbook rested on her knees. Her thumb found the torn edge of the missing page without being asked. Sugar cookies. Nana Jones's looping S, the little dash under "do not overmix" that meant Nana had learned this from experience and expected better from everyone after her.

Across the fire, a woman shook out a shirt and held it up to the light. Two children argued over whether a beetle was dead or practicing. The fiddle gave up on being tuned and became music by accident.

It was almost a place.

Which was rude of it, under the circumstances.

"Mama Baga," a girl said, ducking out of a wagon, "he's here."


Mama Baga came out of the largest wagon and down three wooden steps with the deliberate pace of someone who has learned that most things are not worth the rush. She was broad and settled and she looked at Pathwell the way people look at a problem they are fond of.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I always look terrible," Pathwell said.

"You look worse than that." She turned to Elizabeth. "And you must be the one he's been dragging around."

"Elizabeth."

"You look tired." She said it without apology. "Come in."


Inside the wagon it was close and warm, hung with bundles of dried things and small jars with labels written in a system she didn't have a category for — plant names, numbers, marks that might have been measurements — and papers in rolls that leaned against every surface. It smelled like tea and lanolin and something that was probably sage if sage had been sitting there for thirty years.

Elizabeth had to turn sideways to get past a stack of folded quilts. Mama Baga moved one jar, then another, then set the second one back exactly where it had been, apparently because the first jar had been the point.

"Sit before you fall over politely," Mama Baga said.

"I'm fine."

"That is not a posture. Sit."

Elizabeth sat on a wooden crate beside the cot. It had onions in it. The onions accepted her without comment.

Mama Baga poured more bark tea into a chipped cup and put it in Elizabeth's hands. Her fingers were warm and dry. She did not ask about the cookbook. She did not ask why Elizabeth's coat smelled faintly of rot and city rain and something burned. She only tucked the blanket corner away from Elizabeth's shoe, an absurd small kindness in a wagon full of crisis.

Mama Baga moved to the back where someone else was already sitting — one of the camp healers, a slight woman who looked up and shook her head slightly at Pathwell when he came in.

Behind the healer, on the cot pushed against the far wall, a woman lay with the stillness of someone who has made a private agreement with their own body to stay very quiet and take up as little room as possible. She was younger than Elizabeth had expected. Her hands were at her sides. She was breathing like breathing was the thing she was concentrating on.

"She needs something with charge," the healer said. "Real charge. I've used everything I have."

Pathwell looked at the woman on the cot. Then at the cookbook.

Elizabeth's hands shifted on the spine.

"No," Elizabeth said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You were about to."

The woman on the cot took one careful breath. Then another.

Her thumb found the missing page before the rest of her caught up.

Sugar cookies.

The drawer to the left of the stove. Her grandmother's handwriting in the margin. Twelve years old, flour on her sleeve, being told to stop licking the spoon and doing it anyway.

Gone once already.

Not gone enough, apparently.

The book was heavier than it had been outside. Not physically. Physically it was a cookbook, which meant it weighed one cracked spine, several hundred recipes, and the particular kind of flour that never came out of old paper. The other weight was worse.

Nana Jones had written substitutions in the margins in three colors of ink because she never trusted one pen to last a full recipe. On the back cover there was a shopping list from a summer Elizabeth could not remember except by its evidence: peaches, vinegar, birthday candles, batteries, ant traps. A whole life of small, practical things. The kind nobody thinks to save until saving is no longer available.

She became aware that her hands were gripping the spine. Not hard. Just — present. The book was warm from being held. She had been holding it since the street outside her apartment and had not once set it down.

The woman on the cot breathed in. Carefully. Out.

Elizabeth looked at her hands.

"The cookbook," Elizabeth said.

Pathwell did not move.

"The whole book."

She held it out.

Mama Baga's hand closed around it before Pathwell could move. She held it a moment with both hands under the spine — not examining it, just taking the weight of it. Then she looked at Elizabeth. In that look was something that had no comfortable name: not gratitude, not apology, not the softened expression of someone who feels sorry for you. The face of a woman who had been given things before and understood the terms.

She turned to the cot.

The healer opened the book.

Pathwell slipped out the door. Elizabeth stayed.

She put her hands in her lap. There was a shape at her ribs where the book had rested — a warm pressure that was gone now. She looked at her palms, then looked away.

The wagon warmed by degrees. Not fire-warm. Kitchen-warm. Butter softening on a plate, sugar under a fingernail, the oven door opening against her shins.

For a moment the wagon was not a wagon. It was too narrow to be Nana's kitchen, too crowded, too full of strangers holding their breath, but the smell knew where it was going. It found the good bowl with the blue crack. It found the yellow stool Elizabeth had stood on because the counter was too high. It found Nana's hand guiding hers away from the mixer.

"You'll toughen them," Nana said, somewhere that was not the wagon.

Elizabeth's fingers tightened around nothing.

Mama Baga's hand found her wrist, not stopping her. Just there.

Mama Baga breathed in.

The page edges browned.

Then it was done. The cookbook on the cot was a cookbook again: stained pages, cracked spine, a gap where a page had been.

Mama Baga opened her eyes. She looked at Elizabeth first, not at the healer.

Elizabeth nodded.


Outside, she found Pathwell crouched by the wagon wheel, examining a knot in the wood as if it had personally disappointed him.

"She'll be fine," he said.

"I know."

"That was — "

"I know."

"This wheel is new."

She looked at him.

"Newer," he said. "Relatively."

She didn't have anything to say about the wheel either.

Behind him, the air had changed.

Whatever the healing had left behind was plain enough now. The wagon steps glowed faintly where the healer had carried the cookbook out to cool, a gold dust in the seams of the wood. It clung to the blanket Mama Baga had touched. It sat in the mud by Elizabeth's boot like pollen after a storm.

A little girl reached toward it.

One of the camp women caught her wrist.

Nobody shouted. That made it worse.

Pathwell stopped studying the wheel.

The gold did not fade. It settled. It marked the steps, the mud, Elizabeth's sleeve where Mama Baga had held her. Warm sugar and old paper, spread too thin over too much air.

"That seems noticeable," Elizabeth said.

"Only to things with no manners," Pathwell said.

"Blobs?"

"Among others."

The camp had gone very still in the particular way a room goes still when a glass breaks in another room and everyone knows who owns the glass.

Pathwell brushed his hands on his trousers and failed to remove any mud. "It will draw. Not immediately. Usually not immediately. But enough."

"Enough for what?"

He looked toward the trees beyond the last wagon. For once, he did not look like he owned the place. He looked like a man who had walked into his mother's kitchen wearing muddy boots and only just noticed the floor.

"For Stansbury."

The diary was still in her bag, a small hard edge against her hip. The cookbook was gone now, doing something useful in a stranger's wagon.

The diary stayed where it was.

Pathwell was where she'd left him.

"Stansbury," he said, when she got close enough.

"Stansbury," she agreed.

They left as the fiddle started up again.
