Chapter Two

"Will you slow down?"

The stranger did not.

Elizabeth followed him down three flights of stairs because he still had her grandmother's cookbook under one arm, her diary under the other, and because somewhere above them something wet was trying doors in the hallway.

"What about all my stuff?"

"Replaceable."

"My stuff is not replaceable."

"Some of it almost certainly is."

"That is not better."

They crossed the lobby at a pace just short of running. The night clerk looked up from his phone, took in Elizabeth's coat over pajamas, the barefoot-looking urgency of her shoes, and the man carrying two books that clearly belonged to someone else.

He decided, with admirable speed, not to become involved.

The street air hit cold.

Elizabeth stopped just outside the building and looked up.

Her apartment light was still on.

Three weeks. She had lived there for three weeks. Twenty-one days of boxes she kept promising herself she would unpack over the weekend, as though weekends were a kind of moral reset. The curtains still had fold lines. Half the kitchen was in cardboard. Her grandmother's mixing spoon—the only thing she had bothered putting where it belonged—was still sitting beside the stove.

The front door to her apartment was gone.

Whatever had come through it was not.

Something passed across the light in her window.

She took one step toward the building.

"Wouldn't," the stranger said.

She turned. He was already halfway to the corner.

"You cannot just say 'wouldn't.'"

"I could say considerably more. Most of it would amount to 'wouldn't.'"

She caught up because he had her things. She hated that this was currently the most sensible reason available.

"You okay?" he asked.

"No."

"That's fair."

"Where are you taking me?"

"There's a Chinese place around the corner."

She stared at him.

"I have a meeting at nine tomorrow."

"The dumplings are excellent."

"I said I have a meeting."

"And I said the dumplings are excellent. We are both contributing."

Elizabeth stopped walking.

"My landlord is going to kill me. I have no front door. I have to pick up my dry cleaning before work. I have nothing to wear. I don't know if I should call the police, because I don't know what I would tell them. I don't know if that thing is still in my apartment. I don't know why you were in my apartment. I don't know why you have my diary. I don't know what you did to Nana's cookbook, and I have a meeting in"—she checked a watch she was not wearing—"some number of hours I am currently too angry to calculate."

The stranger listened with his head tilted toward the streetlight above them.

Elizabeth followed his eyes.

"What are you looking at?"

"Nothing."

"You are staring at a streetlight."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He looked back at her. "It seemed rude to interrupt."

She closed her eyes.

"This is a dream."

He pinched her arm.

She slapped his hand away.

"Why would you do that?"

"You asked."

"I did not ask you to pinch me."

"You said it was a dream."

"That is not a request."

"Useful distinction."

He started walking again.

She stood there rubbing her arm until she noticed he was taking her diary with him.

"Hey."

He turned around without stopping.

"What?"

"Give those back."

"Soon."

"That is not yours to decide."

"No," he said, and for once he sounded as though he meant it. "It isn't."

He kept walking anyway.

Elizabeth followed.

The Chinese place really was around the corner.

It was barely a restaurant—more a kitchen with one wall missing and four plastic tables claiming a section of sidewalk. A red neon dragon blinked above the counter with one eye permanently dead. Steam rolled from a metal window. Someone inside was chopping something at a speed that made the knife sound mechanical.

The stranger stepped up to the counter.

"Two orders of fried dumplings."

"I'm not hungry," Elizabeth said.

He looked back at her. "Those aren't for you."

She stared.

"I don't share."

"Of course you don't."

Elizabeth sat at the nearest plastic table because her legs had begun making decisions without consulting her.

The cold forced her hands into her coat pockets.

Coins. A hair tie. A receipt she did not recognize.

Then paper.

She froze.

The laundry ticket.

Bright yellow, folded twice.

She pulled it out and flattened it on the table.

For one stupid, enormous moment the whole night reorganized itself around that rectangle of paper.

Dry cleaning at seven-thirty.

Home by eight.

Shower.

Coffee.

Meeting at nine.

She could borrow a blouse from the woman downstairs if she had to. Explain the door later. Explain nothing else. Go to work. Sit at the conference table. Nod at appropriate intervals. Let someone complain about quarterly numbers until all of this became something that had happened to a different person.

She could still make it.

A delivery truck went by too fast.

The wind caught the ticket.

Elizabeth slapped at it and missed.

It skipped once across the table, once across the sidewalk, caught against the curb, then flipped neatly through a storm drain.

She stared at the drain.

"No."

The word came out small.

She stood and looked down into it as though the city might reconsider.

"Okay. Fine. I can just—"

Her breath stopped halfway in.

The smell was still in her coat.

Wet rot. Low tide. Not strong anymore. Just enough.

Her stomach turned.

She put both hands on the table.

"I can just…"

Nothing came after it.

Three weeks of bare walls.

Boxes she had not opened because opening them would mean deciding where things belonged.

A funeral she had stood through dry-eyed while everyone kept telling her how strong she was, as though grief were an exam she had accidentally done well on.

Garbage bags in the back of a borrowed car.

A new apartment because staying had felt impossible and leaving had not felt like anything at all.

And now her front door was gone and Nana's sugar-cookie page was gone and her laundry ticket was in a sewer.

Elizabeth sat down.

She put her face in her hands.

She was not sure when she started crying.

The stranger returned carrying a paper tray piled with dumplings. He sat down across from her with the anticipatory expression of someone arriving at a private holiday.

Then he noticed her.

His face fell.

"Oh, come on."

Elizabeth looked up through her fingers.

He sighed and pushed one dumpling onto a napkin between them.

"Fine. You can have one."

She stared at it.

The neon dragon flickered red across the table.

Traffic moved at the end of the block. Somewhere a siren climbed through three notes and faded. Two people passed laughing about something that had clearly happened in a much more manageable evening.

The city had continued without asking whether she was ready.

The stranger ate a dumpling.

Then another.

He eyed the one on her napkin.

"You going to eat that?"

"No."

"Good."

His fork moved toward it.

Elizabeth put her hand over the dumpling.

He stopped.

She looked at him.

He slowly withdrew the fork.

"Boundary recognized."

She picked up the menu laminated beneath the napkin.

The stranger waited.

Elizabeth read it twice even though she had taken in none of it the first time.

Then she looked toward the counter.

"Lo mein."

"What?"

"I want lo mein."

His eyebrows went up.

"You said the choice was—" She stopped. "You didn't say anything. I want lo mein."

He looked offended on behalf of the dumplings.

"Your loss."

"It is objectively not."

He raised a finger toward the counter. "One lo mein."

Elizabeth wiped her face with the heel of her hand.

It was an absurdly small decision.

It was still hers.

The stranger returned with a white paper carton and a plastic fork and set both in front of her.

She took one bite.

It was fine.

Not perfect. Not transformative. Not worth destroying an evening over.

Fine.

She took another bite.

The man across from her looked personally betrayed.

"Well?"

"It tastes like lo mein."

"That is devastatingly uncurious."

"I've had a difficult night."

"So have the dumplings."

Elizabeth almost laughed.

She hated him a little for getting that close.

They ate in silence for a minute.

Then she pointed her fork at him.

"What is your name?"

He looked up.

"You broke into my apartment. I feel like this is information I should have."

"Pathwell."

She waited.

He kept eating.

"That's it?"

"That's generally been enough."

"First name?"

"Pathwell."

"Last name?"

"Also Pathwell, if the situation becomes formal."

She stared at him.

He ate another dumpling.

"Fine. Pathwell. Give me my books."

He glanced at the cookbook and diary, which he had set beside his chair but within easy reach.

"We should probably not linger."

"That was not the question."

"No, but it may become the answer."

She put down her fork.

"You keep doing that."

"Doing what?"

"Answering some nearby question and acting like it counts."

Pathwell considered this.

"That is a fair criticism."

"Give me my things."

He picked up the cookbook and diary.

For one hopeful second she thought he was going to hand them over.

Instead he tucked them under his arm.

"Somewhere safer."

Elizabeth laughed once, without humor.

"You do hear yourself, right?"

"Intermittently."

He stood and brushed crumbs from his coat.

"So," he said. "Where to next?"

"Home."

Pathwell looked back toward her apartment building, now hidden around the corner.

"I would advise against that."

"I did not ask what you advised."

"No." He nodded. "You didn't."

He shifted the books under his arm.

"The choice is yours, Lizzy."

The words were sincere.

That was almost the worst part.

He turned and started down the sidewalk carrying her diary and Nana's cookbook with him.

Elizabeth stayed at the table.

Her lo mein was half gone. The dumpling she had defended sat untouched on the napkin. Somewhere under the street her laundry ticket was making its way toward the river or a filtration plant or wherever small administrative hopes went when the universe had finished with them.

She could go back.

She could call the police.

She could sit here until morning.

She could let him disappear with the last things she had brought from one life into the next.

Pathwell reached the corner.

He did not look back.

Elizabeth stood.

Not because he had told her to.

Not because she trusted him.

Because he still had her things.

And because, underneath the anger and the fear and the ridiculousness of the lo mein, she wanted to know what the hell had happened in her apartment.

She picked up the dumpling, ate it in two bites, and followed him.
