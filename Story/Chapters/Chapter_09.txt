Chapter Nine

The radio glowed blue through the dark. Shade drove the way a person drives when they've been doing it since before cars existed — automatic and somewhere else. Elizabeth sat in the back and the mile markers came and went.

Somewhere past the second town, he exhaled through his teeth and tilted his head slightly to the left — the way you do when something calls your name from a distance and you're not sure you heard it. His hands stayed on the wheel. He didn't turn.

The song was one from childhood — she hadn't thought about it in years. She listened to the words in her head and her humming got away from her. When she went quiet again:

"You're welcome to sit up front," he said.

"Pull over and I'll get out."

He laughed, which didn't help. "We're almost there."

The dark fields slid past. The road fell away behind them. The word stayed.

*Were.*

He'd used it once since the road started and she'd filed it away and the car kept moving.


The diner sat on the far side of a gravel lot, lit yellow against the dark by a sign that said DINER 24/7. She hadn't remembered falling asleep, but there was gravel under the tires and then Shade was already inside.

The door had bells on it. The waitress looked up from behind the counter and registered her with the economy of a woman who had opened at four a.m. "With him?" She tilted her head at Shade's booth. "Order'll be out in a minute."

Shade was on his second cup of coffee. She slid into the opposite side of the booth, close to the edge.

"I didn't know how tired you were," he said. "Figured I'd let you sleep."

"You kidnapped me."

"That's one word for it." He smiled, which was what Pathwell's smile would look like if Pathwell had run it for too many years. He was all restraint and old weather. "Don't let the water sit. It's fine."

She let the water sit.

The waitress dropped a cup in front of her and filled it without breaking stride. Elizabeth drank it out of spite and regretted it immediately — burnt grounds and the ghost of a pot that had been going since before this conversation. She set it down. The waitress looked at her with the expression of a woman who had seen that face before and found it endearing.

"Thank you," Elizabeth said.

Shade tipped his cup to her across the table. She ignored him.

"What is it you wanted from this?" he said.

Coffee rings marked the table between them.

"You've had every chance to leave. You could walk over to that woman right now and tell her everything." He settled back into the booth. "You sat down instead. Same as you've always done." He turned his cup in both hands. "You were ready to go when I found you. Boxes packed. You never went back. You loved every minute of it. Every single one."

"Why do you keep doing that?"

He looked at her.

"The past tense. *You were.* *You loved.* Why do you keep talking about me like I'm not here?"

Shade smiled and took a sip and didn't answer.

The food arrived with a tap and a clank — the plate placed in front of her with the casual indifference of someone who had better things to do at this hour.

Cheesy grits. Bacon, half-burnt, exactly how she'd have asked for it. Two eggs, sunny-side, yolks intact. Black coffee with the creamers on the side.

Shade raised an eyebrow.

"Call it a lucky guess."

She ate. The bacon was perfect in the way that food you didn't cook yourself is sometimes perfect — not better than she could do, just free of the effort. He was looking at the road through the window.

"He thinks you're a collectible," Shade said.

She put her fork down.

"Pathwell." He said it to the window, not to her — and when he turned his cup in his hands it was the same gesture. A different table. A different week. A version of Pathwell's face worn past its warranty. "He keeps things that interest him. Books. Receipts. People, if they hold still long enough." He looked at her then. "You're in the collection."

The diner was quiet. The waitress was at the far end of the counter. The coffee maker clicked.

Something in her had been relieved to be interesting.

That was the ugly part. Not the kidnapping, not the diner, not the man wearing Pathwell's face badly enough to make her skin object. The ugly part was the small, embarrassing place inside her that had taken Pathwell's attention and warmed its hands over it. She had thought there must be a reason. Some hidden importance. Some explanation for why, after all the rooms where she had been furniture, someone had looked straight at her and kept looking.

Collectible was a reason.

It was just a worse one.

She looked at the eggs. The yolks were still intact, perfect in their particular way, not broken. She thought about what she'd given up in the shop and what she'd given up at camp and what she was doing in a diner booth at whatever hour this was, and she did not have an answer that was better than the last one she'd tried.

The fork went back in her hand.

She kept eating.

Shade's attention shifted, briefly, toward the window. His hand tightened around the cup. For a second his face went blank.

"No," he said. Not to her. Not to the room. To whatever had hooked itself behind his ribs and pulled. The word came out quiet and flat, the way you tell a door it is not going to open, not this time.

He let go of the cup.

He came back to the table. "You should go back to him."

"That's not what I expected you to say."

"I know." He picked up his cup. "But I'm not what he owes you, and I can't give you what he took. He knows what he did. Ask him."

"Ask him what?"

"The thing he keeps walking around." Shade looked at his reflection in the window. "Start there."

He left a few bills on the table — more than enough, unhurriedly precise. He walked out and didn't look back. She sat with the empty plate for a moment.

The waitress came by with a rag and paused at the edge of the table. She looked at the plate, the bills, Elizabeth's face, and made the professional decision not to put any of those things together out loud.

"You need anything else?"

Elizabeth almost said no because no was easy and required no inventory.

Her hand found the edge of the table. Chipped laminate under her thumb. Something solid. Something cheap. Something that had survived hundreds of people leaning too hard on it.

"No," she said. Then, because the first no had been habit and the second one felt more like hers: "Thank you."

The waitress nodded. "Careful out there."

Elizabeth got up and went outside.

The gravel lot was empty. Somewhere down the road a truck's headlights were already moving away. The morning hadn't started yet but it was trying.

The cold got into her sleeves.

Then she started walking.
