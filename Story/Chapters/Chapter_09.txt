Chapter Nine

The radio glowed blue through the dark.

Elizabeth sat in the back seat because she had chosen the back seat, and the distinction felt more important the farther they got from the wreck.

The road ran through fields and small towns that seemed to have closed hours ago. A gas station with one light still on. A church sign announcing last week's fish fry. A motel vacancy sign missing the V.

Shade drove with both hands on the wheel. Almost. Every few miles his right hand loosened and opened a little toward the driver's-side window, palm turning as if something outside had asked a question only his wrist could hear. Each time, he caught it. Sometimes he closed it against the wheel. Once he shoved it under his thigh.

"That him?" Elizabeth asked.

Shade looked at her in the mirror. "What?"

"Whatever your hand keeps doing."

His eyes went back to the road. "Yes."

"And right now?"

His right hand flexed once on the wheel. "Behind us."

Elizabeth looked through the rear window. Nothing but dark road.

"Is he following us?"

"No idea."

"Do you know what he's thinking?"

Shade glanced at her again. "No."

"Good."

That nearly got a smile out of him. "You ask efficient questions."

"I've had an inefficient night."

He nodded as if that made sense.

A song came on the radio, an old one from when she was a kid, and Elizabeth knew the words without remembering when she'd learned them. She sang them in her head until her humming gave her away. Shade was tapping the wheel along with the beat, singing under his breath, and when he lost the words in the second verse he heard her and stopped. He didn't say anything about it.

After another mile he said, "You don't have to sit back there. Front seat's still empty."

"So is the seat I'm in. Mostly."

"Fair."

He left it there. He checked his mirrors often, and tapped the wheel twice with one finger before every turn. The closer she watched him, the less the resemblance explained.


The diner sat on the far side of a gravel lot under a yellow sign that said DINER 24/7. Shade pulled in and turned off the engine.

"Coffee," he said.

"You really meant that."

"I usually do."

Elizabeth looked at the building: warm windows, three cars, a waitress moving behind the counter. Public. Ordinary. She could stay in the sedan. She could walk to the road. She could go inside.

Shade was already halfway to the door. He didn't look back to see which one she chose.

She got out.

The bells over the door made a small, exhausted noise when she came in. The waitress looked up from the counter. She wore a blue-and-white uniform with her wild hair tucked under a little white cap, and her eyes went over Elizabeth's coat, the pastel linen under it and the gold chain at her throat, and decided not to ask.

"Sit anywhere."

Shade had taken a booth by the window. Elizabeth took the seat across from him, close enough to the aisle that standing up would need no negotiation.

The waitress came over with two menus and a pot of coffee. "Coffee?"

Shade turned his cup upright. Elizabeth hesitated, then turned hers over too.

It came out black and steaming. She took one drink. Burnt grounds that had been used once already, an old pot, the faint mineral taste of water that had been through machinery older than she was. Her face couldn't hide it. The waitress smiled, unsurprised, and went back to the counter.

It was terrible. Something in Elizabeth's shoulders came down anyway.

Shade noticed. "Bad?"

"Impressively."

"Good."

"That is not the normal response."

"You looked less tired after you drank it."

Elizabeth stared at him.

Shade lifted both hands slightly. "Observation. Not memory."

She believed him, mostly because he'd bothered to make the distinction.

The waitress came back with a pencil behind one ear. "What can I get you?"

Shade ordered eggs, toast, and the coffee he already had. Elizabeth opened the menu and ordered cheesy grits with extra pepper, bacon, and two eggs sunny side up. The waitress wrote it down and left.

Elizabeth folded the menu shut. "So."

Shade looked out the window. "So."

"You don't know me."

"I met you on the road twenty-five minutes ago."

"Good. Keep remembering that."

"I'll make a note."

Pathwell would have smiled after saying it. Shade didn't. Elizabeth couldn't decide which was more irritating.

"What do you remember?"

Shade turned the coffee cup between his hands. The gesture was Pathwell's. The restraint around it wasn't.

"Fragments."

"Try some nouns."

Shade looked down at the coffee. "A room in Prague I have never entered. A woman named Celia who hated pears. Three ways to cheat at a card game I have never played. The smell of a stable before rain. A staircase with a loose fourth step."

Elizabeth waited. "Useful things?"

"Sometimes."

"A life?"

Shade's hand stopped on the cup. "No."

He looked toward the window again. "There are too many pieces and not enough between them. I know what he knew in places. I remember what he remembered in pieces. I don't remember being him. We used to—"

He stopped. His jaw tightened.

"He used to," he corrected.

That was the thing she had been trying to name since the road. Not Pathwell done badly, or Pathwell in another coat. A person sorting his grammar around memories that didn't belong to his life.

"Do you want to remember being him?" Elizabeth asked.

Shade's eyes came back to hers. "No."

He didn't add anything to make it easier to hear, and Elizabeth believed that too.

The waitress brought their food, the plates landing with a tap and a clank. Elizabeth went for the bacon first. It was burnt at one end and nearly right at the other, and she chewed, and chewed, and closed her eyes for a second. It was exactly how she'd have made it.

Shade salted his eggs before he tasted them. Pathwell would have done that. Probably.

"The blobs," Elizabeth said.

Shade looked up. "What about them?"

"One came through my apartment door while he was standing in my living room. Another one swallowed Pathwell in a parking lot."

Shade's fork stopped. "Swallowed him?"

"Yes."

"And he got out?"

"I cut him out."

Shade looked at the foam dagger where it lay beside her coat in the booth, then back at her. "Good."

Elizabeth frowned. "That's all?"

"Would you prefer applause?"

"No."

"Then good."

He went back to his eggs. Elizabeth let him have two bites.

"You drove toward him because your hand told you to."

Shade's jaw tightened. "I drove because it wouldn't stop."

His right hand opened against the tabletop, palm turning toward the window and the road behind them. He closed it into a fist.

"No," he said quietly.

It wasn't for Elizabeth, and it wasn't exactly for Pathwell either. It was for the pull itself. He kept the fist closed until it passed.

"You can resist it," she said.

"Yes."

"Does it hurt?"

"Eventually."

"Then why not go back?"

Shade looked at her as if the answer were obvious. "Because I don't want to."

Elizabeth looked down at her grits. She had deliberately crashed a Cadillac less than an hour ago because two men wouldn't let her into a conversation about her own night.

"Why did you come toward Pathwell in the first place?"

"I told you. The pull."

"Nothing about me?"

Shade's expression changed, closer to irritation that the question was necessary than to sympathy. "I did not know you existed."

Elizabeth sat very still.

Pathwell saying marked. The second blob crossing the parking lot without looking at her. Shade driving toward Pathwell without knowing her name.

Something small and embarrassing came loose inside her: a theory dying. She had never said it out loud, but somewhere in the night she'd let all that impossible attention arrange itself around a flattering explanation. Maybe there was something about her. Maybe the universe had picked her out, for once.

Shade took another drink of bad coffee. "You were never the point," he said.

Elizabeth looked at him.

His eyes narrowed slightly. "Of whatever followed him," he added. "Don't make that sentence larger than it is."

Elizabeth leaned back against the booth. "So what was?"

"I don't know."

"You?"

Shade looked at his closed right hand. "Maybe."

"Pathwell?"

"Maybe."

"Both?"

"That would explain why the cleanup keeps making bad decisions."

"Cleanup?"

"That's Stansbury's word."

"Of course Stansbury has the sensible noun." Then it caught up with her. "You know Stansbury."

"I remember him." Shade corrected himself at once. "Fragments of him. Before me. Not now."

"Does he know you?"

"He met me on the road twenty-five minutes ago."

Elizabeth pointed her fork at him. "Do not start doing that."

For the first time Shade smiled without looking like Pathwell. It was smaller, and meaner around the edges, and his own. It didn't last.

Elizabeth put the fork down. "What did Pathwell do?"

"I don't know all of it."

"What do you know?"

"That I started with pieces of him already inside me."

"Because of a prune."

"Probably."

"Probably?"

Shade looked down at the table. "I remember the edge of one. The shape of work. His certainty. A problem he had decided belonged to him. Then the pieces stop lining up."

"What was he certain about?"

"I don't know."

"What did he choose?"

Shade looked up. "That's his answer."

"Convenient."

"Accurate." His voice had gone flatter. "The part I remember clearly is the feeling before it."

"What feeling?"

"Certainty."

Elizabeth stopped.

"Not that he was right," Shade said, to the table. "That he had reached the point where being uncertain no longer mattered to him."

The diner went on around them. The coffee maker clicked. A spoon hit the floor behind the counter, and someone in the kitchen laughed.

Elizabeth thought about Pathwell in her apartment saying marked. Pathwell at Camp, arriving with books nobody had asked him to buy. Pathwell in the car saying Drive. All of it could look generous from one angle. That was what made it hard.

"Why are you telling me?"

Shade's fingers tightened around the cup. "Because he'll listen to you longer than he'll listen to me."

Elizabeth let out a short laugh. "That is an extremely low bar."

"Still a bar."

"So I'm leverage."

Shade didn't insult her by pretending otherwise. "Partly."

"And the other part?"

He looked toward the road. "I'm tired of carrying pieces of an answer that belong to a question I never asked."

Elizabeth looked at him for a long moment. "You want something from him."

"Yes."

"What?"

Shade's right hand moved once against the table. He flattened it. "For him to stop deciding what I am before I get to."

Elizabeth looked down at her own hands. That one she understood more than she wanted to.

"And what am I supposed to ask?"

Shade met her eyes. "Don't ask me for his confession."

"Then what?"

"Ask him what he chose."

Elizabeth waited.

Shade looked at the window, at his own dim reflection laid over the parking-lot lights. "He knows what he did. Ask him."

"And if he says he doesn't?"

"Then ask what he decided he was allowed to do."

Elizabeth sat with that.

The waitress came by with the pot. "Warm you up?"

"No, thank you," Elizabeth said.

Shade held his cup out, and the waitress filled it.

Elizabeth looked at him. "I'm going back."

Shade nodded. "All right."

"I'm not doing it for you."

"I know."

"You don't get to know that yet."

Shade considered the correction. "Fair."

Elizabeth slid out of the booth. "I assume you're paying for this."

Shade already had his wallet out. "I was going to pay for mine."

"I don't have any money. All I have to my name right now is my diary and a foam dagger."

Shade looked at the dagger on the seat, then at her plate. He put down bills for both of them, then counted out the tip with irritating precision.

Elizabeth asked the waitress the way to the bar back toward the fields, the one with a fish over the register.

"That'd be the one out on the county road." The waitress gave her the road number and three turns, then repeated the second one because Elizabeth's face apparently asked her to. She wrote them on the back of the receipt in blocky capitals, underlined the second left after the water tower twice, and handed it over.

Shade watched, and didn't offer to drive her.

Elizabeth tucked the foam dagger into the back of her waistband and went out.

Outside, it was still full night, and the cold got into her sleeves. She stood under the yellow sign with the receipt in her hand. She didn't know where Pathwell was. She did know where the night had started.

Behind the glass, Shade sat alone with his coffee. His right hand opened on the table, and he closed it again.

Elizabeth started walking toward the road. Nobody had sent her, and nobody had called her back. She had a question now, and this time she intended to make him answer it.
