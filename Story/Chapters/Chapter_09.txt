Chapter Nine

The radio glowed blue through the dark.

Shade drove without talking.

Elizabeth sat in the back seat because she had chosen the back seat, and the distinction felt increasingly important the farther they got from the wreck.

The road moved through fields and small towns that seemed to have closed hours ago. A gas station with one light still on. A church sign announcing a fish fry from last week. A motel vacancy sign missing the V.

Shade kept both hands on the wheel.

Almost.

Every few miles his right hand loosened.

Not casually.

It opened a little toward the driver's-side window, palm turning as if something outside had asked a question only his wrist could hear.

Each time Shade caught it.

Sometimes he closed the hand against the wheel.

Once he shoved it beneath his thigh.

Elizabeth watched from the back seat.

"That him?"

Shade looked at her in the mirror.

"What?"

"Whatever your hand keeps doing."

His eyes went back to the road.

"Yes."

"Pathwell."

"Yes."

The certainty of the answer bothered her less than the fact that it was apparently the only certainty Shade possessed.

"You said it only tells you which way."

"That's all it tells me."

"And right now?"

Shade's right hand flexed once on the wheel.

"Behind us."

Elizabeth looked through the rear window.

Nothing but dark road.

"How far?"

"No idea."

"Is he following us?"

"No idea."

"Do you know what he's thinking?"

Shade glanced at her again.

"No."

"Good."

That got the smallest movement at one corner of his mouth.

"You ask efficient questions."

"I've had an inefficient night."

He nodded as if that made sense.

A song came on the radio that Elizabeth knew without remembering when she had learned it.

She listened for half a verse before realizing she was humming.

Shade did not comment.

That alone separated him from Pathwell in a way the face had not managed.

After another mile he said, "Front seat's still empty."

"So is the seat I'm in. Mostly."

"Fair."

He left it there.

Elizabeth looked at the back of his head.

Wrong was becoming more specific.

Shade had Pathwell's pauses but not his need to fill them.

He checked mirrors the same way. Tilted his head the same way when something caught his attention. Tapped the steering wheel twice with one finger before a turn.

But Pathwell moved through the world as if every empty space had been left there for him to occupy.

Shade seemed to distrust empty space and leave it alone.

The closer she watched him, the less the resemblance explained.


The diner sat on the far side of a gravel lot under a yellow sign that said DINER 24/7.

Shade pulled in.

He turned off the engine.

"Coffee," he said.

"You really meant that."

"I usually do."

Elizabeth looked toward the building.

Warm windows. Three cars. A waitress moving behind the counter.

Public.

Ordinary.

She could stay in the sedan.

She could walk to the road.

She could go inside.

Shade was already halfway to the door.

He did not look back to see which one she chose.

Elizabeth disliked how much that helped.

She got out.

The bells over the diner door made a small exhausted noise when she entered.

The waitress looked up from the counter.

"Sit anywhere."

Shade had taken a booth near the window.

Elizabeth chose the seat across from him, close enough to the aisle that standing would require no negotiation.

The waitress appeared with two menus and a coffee pot.

"Coffee?"

Shade turned his cup upright.

Elizabeth hesitated.

Then turned hers over too.

The coffee hit the cup black and steaming.

She took one drink.

Burnt grounds. Old pot. The faint mineral taste of water that had passed through machinery older than she was.

It was terrible.

Elizabeth looked at the cup.

The familiarity arrived half a second later.

Space Between coffee.

Not the same coffee.

The same category of failure.

Two in the morning. Highlighters. Papers due. Ordinary exhaustion.

Something in her shoulders lowered despite herself.

Shade noticed.

"Bad?"

"Impressively."

"Good."

"That is not the normal response."

"You looked less tired after you drank it."

Elizabeth stared at him.

Shade lifted both hands slightly.

"Observation. Not memory."

That distinction mattered enough that she believed him.

The waitress returned with a pencil behind one ear.

"What can I get you?"

Shade ordered eggs, toast, and coffee he already had.

Elizabeth opened the menu.

She chose cheesy grits, bacon, and two eggs over easy.

The waitress wrote it down.

No lucky guess.

No dead grandmother's preferences appearing in the mouth of a stranger.

Just breakfast.

When the waitress left, Elizabeth folded the menu shut.

"So."

Shade looked out the window.

"So."

"You know pieces."

"Yes."

"Of Pathwell."

"Yes."

"But not now."

"Correct."

"And not me."

Shade looked at her.

"I met you on the road twenty-five minutes ago."

"Good. Keep remembering that."

"I'll make a note."

There it was.

Pathwell would have smiled after saying it.

Shade did not.

Elizabeth could not decide which was more irritating.

"What do you remember?"

Shade turned the coffee cup between his hands.

The gesture was Pathwell's.

The restraint around it was not.

"Fragments."

"You said that."

"Because it's the accurate word."

"Try some nouns."

Shade looked down at the coffee.

"A room in Prague I have never entered. A woman named Celia who hated pears. Three ways to cheat at a card game I have never played. The smell of a stable before rain. A staircase with a loose fourth step."

Elizabeth waited.

"Useful things?"

"Sometimes."

"A life?"

Shade's hand stopped on the cup.

"No."

The answer landed harder than she expected.

He looked toward the window again.

"There are too many pieces and not enough between them. I know what he knew in places. I remember what he remembered in pieces. I don't remember being him."

"We used to—"

Shade stopped.

His jaw tightened.

"He used to," he corrected.

Elizabeth watched the correction settle.

That was the thing she had been trying to name since the road.

Not Pathwell done badly.

Not Pathwell in another coat.

A person sorting grammar around memories that did not belong to his life.

"Do you want to remember being him?" Elizabeth asked.

Shade's eyes came back to hers.

"No."

Immediate.

No speech after it.

No explanation offered to make the answer more acceptable.

Elizabeth believed that too.

The waitress brought their food.

The plates landed with a tap and a clank.

Elizabeth picked up her fork.

The bacon was too crisp at one end and nearly right at the other.

It was excellent.

Shade salted his eggs before tasting them.

Pathwell would have done that.

Probably.

She hated that she could now imagine both men performing the same tiny action for completely different reasons.

"The blobs," Elizabeth said.

Shade looked up.

"What about them?"

"One came through my apartment door. Another swallowed Pathwell in a parking lot."

Shade's fork stopped.

That was information he did not have.

Good.

"Swallowed him?"

"Yes."

"And he got out?"

"I cut him out."

Shade looked at her foam dagger where it rested beside her coat in the booth.

Then back at her.

"Good."

Elizabeth frowned.

"That's all?"

"Would you prefer applause?"

"No."

"Then good."

He went back to his eggs.

Elizabeth let him take two bites.

"You said on the road that you know enough to know he won't do something cleanly."

Shade chewed.

Swallowed.

"I said I know old versions of his excuses."

"Close enough."

"It isn't."

"Fine. Explain the difference."

Shade put down his fork.

"The fragments I have are old. I don't know who he is today."

"You don't seem optimistic."

"I'm not required to be."

"You also drove toward him because your hand told you to."

Shade's jaw tightened.

"That was not optimism."

His right hand opened against the tabletop.

Palm turning slightly toward the window.

Toward the road behind them.

Shade closed it into a fist.

"No," he said quietly.

The word was not for Elizabeth.

Not for Pathwell either, exactly.

It was for the pull itself.

He kept his fist closed until the tension passed.

Elizabeth watched.

"You can resist it."

"Yes."

"Does it hurt?"

"Eventually."

"Then why not go back?"

Shade looked at her as if the answer were obvious.

"Because I don't want to."

There it was again.

A choice uncomplicated by a speech.

Elizabeth looked down at her grits.

She had deliberately crashed a Cadillac less than an hour ago because two men would not let her into a conversation about her own night.

Then she had climbed into another man's car because he seemed like he might have answers.

Agency, apparently, was not a staircase.

More like a hallway full of doors where some opened backward.

"Why did you come toward Pathwell in the first place?"

"I told you."

"The pull."

"The pull."

"Nothing about me?"

Shade's expression changed.

Not sympathy.

Something closer to irritation that the question was necessary.

"I did not know you existed."

Elizabeth sat very still.

The apartment door.

Pathwell saying marked.

Pathwell pulling her into the hallway because he had already decided the danger belonged to her.

The second blob crossing the parking lot without looking at her.

Shade driving toward Pathwell without knowing her name.

Something small and embarrassing loosened inside her.

Not disappointment exactly.

A theory dying.

She had never said it aloud, but somewhere in the night she had let the impossible attention arrange itself around a flattering explanation.

Maybe there was something about her.

Maybe the universe had selected her for once.

Shade took another drink of bad coffee.

"You were never the point," he said.

Elizabeth looked at him.

His eyes narrowed slightly.

"Of whatever followed him," he added. "Don't make that sentence larger than it is."

That saved it.

Barely.

Elizabeth leaned back against the booth.

"So what was?"

"I don't know."

"You?"

Shade looked toward his closed right hand.

"Maybe."

"Pathwell?"

"Maybe."

"Both?"

"That would explain why the cleanup keeps making bad decisions."

"Cleanup."

Shade nodded.

"That's what he calls them?"

"Stansbury."

"Of course Stansbury uses the sensible noun."

Elizabeth stared.

"You know Stansbury."

"I remember him."

Shade corrected himself immediately.

"Fragments of him. Before me. Not now."

"Does he know you?"

"He met me on the road twenty-five minutes ago."

Elizabeth pointed at him with her fork.

"Do not start doing that."

For the first time Shade smiled without looking like Pathwell.

It was smaller.

Meaner around the edges.

His own.

It disappeared quickly.

Elizabeth put down the fork.

"What did Pathwell do?"

Shade's smile was gone.

"I don't know all of it."

"What do you know?"

"That I started with pieces of him already inside me."

"Because of a prune."

"Probably."

"Probably?"

Shade looked down at the table.

"I remember the edge of one. The shape of work. His certainty. A problem he had decided belonged to him. Then the pieces stop lining up."

"What was he certain about?"

"I don't know."

"What did he choose?"

Shade looked up.

"That's his answer."

Elizabeth stared at him.

"Convenient."

"Accurate."

Shade's voice had gone flatter.

"The part I remember clearly is the feeling before it."

"What feeling?"

"Certainty."

Elizabeth stopped.

Shade looked at the table.

"Not that he was right. That he had reached the point where being uncertain no longer mattered to him."

The diner continued around them.

Coffee maker clicking.

A spoon dropped behind the counter.

Someone in the kitchen laughed.

Elizabeth thought about Pathwell in her apartment saying marked.

Pathwell at Camp arriving with books nobody asked him to buy.

Pathwell in the car saying Drive.

All of those things could be generous from one angle.

That was what made them difficult.

"Why are you telling me?"

Shade's fingers tightened around the coffee cup.

There.

The part he had not volunteered.

"Because he'll listen to you longer than he'll listen to me."

Elizabeth laughed once.

"That is an extremely low bar."

"Still a bar."

"So I'm leverage."

Shade did not insult her by pretending otherwise.

"Partly."

"And the other part?"

He looked toward the road.

"I'm tired of carrying pieces of an answer that belong to a question I never asked."

That was almost enough to make him sympathetic.

Almost.

Elizabeth looked at him for a long moment.

"You want something from him."

"Yes."

"What?"

Shade's right hand moved once against the table.

He flattened it.

"For him to stop deciding what I am before I get to."

Elizabeth looked down at her own hands.

That one she understood more than she wanted to.

"And what am I supposed to ask?"

Shade met her eyes.

"Don't ask me for his confession."

"Then what?"

"Ask him what he chose."

Elizabeth waited.

Shade looked at the window, at his own dim reflection overlaid with the parking-lot lights.

"He knows what he did. Ask him."

"And if he says he doesn't?"

"Then ask what he decided he was allowed to do."

Elizabeth sat with that.

The waitress came by with the coffee pot.

"Warm you up?"

Elizabeth looked at the cup.

"No, thank you."

Shade held his out.

The waitress filled it.

Of course.

Elizabeth looked at Shade.

"I'm going back."

Shade nodded.

No relief.

No victory.

"All right."

"I'm not doing it for you."

"I know."

"You don't get to know that yet."

Shade considered the correction.

"Fair."

Elizabeth slid out of the booth.

Shade reached for his wallet.

"I'll pay for mine," she said.

"I was going to pay for mine."

She stopped.

Shade put exact bills beside his plate, then counted out the tip with irritating precision.

Elizabeth paid at the register herself.

The waitress handed back her change.

"You need directions?"

Elizabeth looked through the window at the road.

"Probably."

Shade spoke from behind her.

"There's a pay phone by the bathrooms. It works."

Elizabeth looked at him.

"You know that how?"

"I asked the waitress while you were ordering."

Ordinary information.

Again.

She almost laughed.

Instead she went to the phone.

The receiver was beige and heavier than she remembered phones being.

She stared at it.

Then at the row of numbers.

There was nobody she could call who could solve the actual problem.

But she did know where Pathwell had been headed before she wrecked the car.

And she knew one other thing now.

The question belonged to him.

Elizabeth put the receiver back without dialing.

When she returned to the front, Shade was still at the booth.

He had not followed.

He had not left either.

"Which way is the bar?" she asked the waitress.

The waitress gave her the road number and three turns, then repeated the second turn because Elizabeth's face apparently requested it.

Elizabeth wrote the directions on the back of her receipt.

Shade watched but did not offer to drive her.

Good.

Outside, the morning had not started yet but was trying.

The cold got into Elizabeth's sleeves.

She stood beneath the yellow diner sign with the receipt in one hand and the foam dagger in the other.

Behind the glass, Shade sat alone with his coffee.

His right hand opened on the table.

He closed it again.

Elizabeth started walking toward the road.

Not because Shade had sent her.

Not because Pathwell had called her back.

Because she had a question now.

And this time she intended to make him answer it.
