Coda

The Space Between smelled the same as it always had: old paper, dried leather, binding glue gone brittle, and beneath all of it the dust that had been settling longer than most things had been alive.

The shopkeeper was at his counter, carrying ones in the ledger. The cat was on the windowsill. Nothing had changed since the last time, or the time before that, or the time before that.

Outside, three weeks had passed. In here, that was the kind of fact that had to bring its own proof.

Pathwell stood in the cooking section.

He had been standing there for approximately ten minutes, which in a shop like this was either no time at all or much longer than anyone should be standing anywhere. He was holding a copy of The Joy of Cooking, a different edition than the one that had come in through the door with Elizabeth in her arms, given away now, in a camp he owed a debt to. This one was newer. The spine was intact. The pages were unworn.

The shopkeeper turned a page in the ledger.

"No," he said.

Pathwell had not asked yet.

"I have excellent credit."

"You have extensive credit," the shopkeeper said. "That is different."

Pathwell looked down at the book. "I only need enough."

"You have had enough several times."

That was rude, which was typical of men who kept ledgers for a living. Pathwell set the cookbook on the counter and reached into his coat.

The paper he found was small. A receipt from somewhere that no longer existed. His own handwriting on the back, younger by several mistakes. It had enough charge for a simple prune. Not elegant. Not large. Just enough to buy a thing that could be bought.

He pressed his thumb to the corner.

Nothing happened.

No branch-light. No pressure. No clean little subtraction from the future. The paper sat under his thumb and remained paper.

Pathwell waited for anger.

It did not come.

The absence of it was so complete that he almost looked around to see where it had gone. What came instead was a loosening in his chest, small and undignified and deeply inconvenient.

The cat jumped down from the windowsill and passed through his ankles.

"I know," Pathwell said.

The shopkeeper put his pen down.

"Do you?"

"No," Pathwell said. "But I'm closer than I was."

The door opened.

Elizabeth came in wearing the same coat she'd had since the street outside her apartment. Her arm was out of the sling, held carefully at her side. She had two books under her arm — a French technique paperback, spine soft from use, and something new. Not the diary.

Pathwell picked up the replacement cookbook, considered it, and put it back on the counter.

"It was my fault," he said.

Elizabeth stopped beside the cat. "What was?"

Pathwell adjusted the book so its spine was square with the edge of the counter. "Most recently?"

The shopkeeper made a small mark in the ledger.

Elizabeth looked at him for a long moment, then at the cookbook.

"That isn't mine," she said.

"No," Pathwell said.

"Good."

The shopkeeper slid a card across the counter. It had an address on it, written in ink that had dried before either of them was born.

"Do this for me," he said, "and you can have the book."

Pathwell looked at the card.

Elizabeth leaned over just enough to read it and made a face.

"That seems unreasonable," Pathwell said.

"It is," the shopkeeper said.

Pathwell put the card in his pocket.

"Are you ready?" she said.

"No," he said.

"Perfect."
