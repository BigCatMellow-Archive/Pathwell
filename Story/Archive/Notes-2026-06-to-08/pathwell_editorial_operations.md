# Pathwell — The Revision Process
### How Discovery Draft Becomes Finished Prose

*Built from manuscript drafts, revision sessions, and documented editorial exchanges.*
*Every "Discovery" excerpt is verbatim from BCMWriter.txt. Every "Revision" excerpt is verbatim from the working manuscript.*
*Purpose: let someone reproduce the process that took Chapters 1–2 from discovery draft to finished prose — and run it on new material.*

---

## How to Use This Document

It has five parts.

**The Method** is how the revision work actually proceeds — the loop you run, not the rules you apply. It's the most important part for someone trying to replicate the process, and it's first for that reason.

**The Principles** are the rules underneath every revision. They govern writing new material and fixing old material alike.

**The Operations** are the specific moves that turn discovery draft into revision. Each shows the same passage before and after, names what changed, and tells you how to spot the problem in new text. These are the principles in action at scene level.

**The Sentence-Level Faults** are the smaller, recurring habits the operations don't cover — the things flagged line by line in every session. These are what you hunt for on a close pass.

**The Voice Reference** lets you hear each character's register so you can reproduce it.

The checklist at the end collapses everything into a one-page diagnostic.

# PART ZERO: THE METHOD

This is how the revision actually happens. The operations and principles tell you *what good looks like*; this tells you *how to get a page there*. Replicating the prose process means replicating this loop, not just knowing the rules.

### The work is iterative, not one-pass.
A page is not fixed in a single sitting. The same problems recur across drafts, and the editor's job is partly just to notice that they're still there: "we flagged this last time — it's still here." Expect three or four passes over the same paragraph. A flagged problem that comes back unfixed is normal; it gets flagged again, the same way, until it's gone. Do not treat a draft as done because it has been revised once.

### Diagnose in a batch, then fix one at a time.
The pattern in every session is the same: read the passage, list *all* the problems at once, then solve them individually. A typical diagnosis looks like "you've got five specific fixes: the disorientation paragraph, the 'lost hers' line, the tears, the duplicated phrase, and the missing lo mein — which do you want to take first?" Listing them together shows the scale of the work; taking them one at a time keeps it from being overwhelming. The writer picks the order. Never try to fix everything in one motion.

### Separate what works from what doesn't, and say so plainly.
Every diagnosis opens by naming what's already landing before touching what isn't. "The structure is sound. The value turns correctly. The ending sequence is clean. — Now, what still needs work." This isn't softening; it's information. The writer needs to know which parts to protect as much as which to change. State the working parts in one or two lines, then move to the problems.

### The rewrite is a teaching example, not a replacement.
When a rewrite is offered, it comes with the underlying technique named so it can be reused, and the writer is expected to write the real version themselves. The disorientation rewrite ends with: "You don't have to use my specific details. The technique is: *expectation, then violation.* What does she think she'll see, and what's there instead?" The example exists to extract a portable rule. The actual page is the writer's to produce — the prose has to be in the writer's voice, not the editor's, or it isn't theirs.

### Name the technique, then make it transferable.
Every fix should leave behind a rule the writer can carry to the next paragraph without help. Not "change this sentence to that" but "disorientation is the gap between expectation and reality — apply that anywhere a character is lost." If a rewrite doesn't produce a reusable principle, it hasn't finished its job.

### One dramatic question at a time, one chapter at a time.
The work doesn't hold the whole book in view. It takes one chapter, one scene, one problem. When the project feels too big, the instruction is always to shrink the frame: not "fix the novel" but "fix the disorientation paragraph in Chapter 2." Hold the smallest unit that still makes sense.

**The loop, distilled:**
1. Read the passage.
2. Name what's working (briefly).
3. List every problem at once.
4. Let the writer choose which to take first.
5. For each: diagnose *why* it fails, then either ask the writer to fix it or demonstrate — and if you demonstrate, name the transferable technique.
6. The writer rewrites in their own voice.
7. Re-read. Anything still broken gets flagged again, the same way.
8. Repeat until the page holds.

# PART ONE: THE PRINCIPLES

These are the rules the revision is built on. They are not about revision specifically — they govern every sentence and every scene, whether you're drafting fresh or editing. The operations in Part Two are these principles applied to actual passages.

### 1. The Body Knows First
Before a character understands something intellectually, the body reacts. Throat tightens before the mind knows it's about to cry. Legs move before the mind decides to run. Goosebumps in the fear section, warmth near love, stomach dropping when a lie surfaces. Write the body's response and the emotion is carried without being named. This is the single most load-bearing rule in the manuscript.

*Example:* "Her body moved back before she did, sending her stumbling into the wall."

### 2. Write the Sequel
The first book explains everything; the sequel assumes you were there. The characters already live in this world — they don't explain gravity and they don't explain magic. The reader catches up by watching, not by being taught. Test: would someone who actually lives here say this, notice this, explain this? If not, cut it.

### 3. Therefore / But — Never And Then
Every scene must cause the next. "Therefore" means the outcome of Scene A forces Scene B. "But" means the outcome of A is complicated by a new problem that creates B. If the only connection you can find between two scenes is "and then," one of them is misplaced or doesn't belong. Rewrite the end of the first scene until a "therefore" or a "but" appears.

### 4. Every Scene Turns a Value
At the top of a scene something is at stake — safety, trust, freedom, knowledge. By the bottom, that value has changed charge: positive to negative, or negative to positive. If the emotional state at the end matches the beginning, nothing happened. Find the turn or cut the scene. (Ch. 2 turns on safety/belonging: she begins managing a crisis and ends having quietly decided to follow.)

### 5. Show the Iceberg's Tip
Build everything; show one-eighth. A locked door they never open. A name dropped and never explained. A coin called a Rai Stone with no gloss. The world feels deep not because you showed it all but because you clearly know it all and chose to show almost none. (See Principle 11 for the version of this that applies per-chapter.)

### 6. Characters Want Things
Every character in every scene wants something, even if it's a glass of water. Conflicting wants make tension. A want the world won't satisfy makes a scene. If a character is only present — watching, following, listening — give them a want or get them off the page. Elizabeth's early want is small and suppressed (to manage, to keep things normal), but it's there.

### 7. Agency Is a Ladder
A passive character can become active, but the climb must be visible. Small choice → bigger choice → choice with consequences → choice that changes the story. Each rung is something the character climbs, not a door someone else opened. Reacting well to pressure is not the same as initiating. No skipping rungs.

### 8. Don't Explain the Metaphor Before Performing It
The demonstration is the explanation. The cookie spell teaches the magic system by happening to Elizabeth — no lecture precedes it. Cut every pre-explanation that sits in front of a dramatic demonstration. (This is the rule the Dewey Decimal lecture violates most badly in the discovery draft.)

### 9. Incomplete Answers Build Worlds
The default answer to a question about the world should be partial, evasive, contradictory, or silence. "That was nothing, just cleaning up a mess" tells you more than a paragraph would, because it implies a world where this is routine. Complete answers close doors; partial answers leave the iceberg intact.

### 10. Subtext Over Text
What a character doesn't say matters more than what they do. The dumpling is not about the dumpling. The laundry ticket is not about laundry. The gap between what is said and what is meant is where the scene actually lives.

### 11. One Unexplained Thing Per Chapter
Leave at least one detail unexplained on purpose. A name, a place, a scar, a coin, a page that smells like lavender. One sentence each, and they imply centuries. If everything in a chapter is accounted for, the world has no depth — add a mystery.

### 12. Breathe Between Punches
Fast scenes need slow scenes after them. If every chapter runs at the same speed, none of them feels fast. Chapter 2 is the deliberate exhale after Chapter 1's blob attack — this is where the reader falls for the characters. Protect the slow beats; they make the fast ones land.

### 13. Read It Aloud
If a sentence sounds wrong in your mouth, it reads wrong on the page. Prose is rhythm. Short sentences punch, long sentences flow, monotony kills either way. The isolated three-word sentence ("The impact sang.") only works because the sentences around it don't. Your ear knows before your brain does.

# PART TWO: THE OPERATIONS

Each operation is one of the principles above, applied to a real passage. The discovery text is what existed; the revision is what it became.

---

## Before You Start: Spotting a Discovery Draft

The discovery draft has consistent tells. Recognizing them is the first step.

**It explains what it's about to show.** An emotion is named before the character feels it. (Violates Principle 1.)

**It describes from outside.** The narrator reports what characters look like rather than what they experience. (Violates Principle 1.)

**It holds the reader's hand.** Dialogue runs two or three exchanges long; endings add a summary after the image already landed. (Violates Principles 10, 13.)

**It visualizes magic.** Spells are light shows — glowing shapes, swirling rings, colors. (Violates Principle 1.)

**It lets Pathwell win every exchange.** He speeches, persuades, explains. (Violates Principles 2, 9.)

These aren't failures of craft. They're features of exploration — the discovery draft is finding the story. The revision makes it legible to a reader who wasn't there for the finding.

---

## Operation 1: Strip the Establishing Shot
### (Principle 2 — Write the Sequel)

**The move:** The discovery draft opens before anything interesting happens. It shows the setup to the setup. Start as late as possible.

**Discovery:**
> The door burst open with panic, slamed shut with desperation. The scuffling of mismathed boots formed a misguided pattern across the floor as hurried hands sorted through scattered piles. "Come on" the hands ruffled through layers, "...come on..." piles flinging to the side, "...damnit, where are you..." towers being knocked over. The noise drew more than a curiosity from the homes occupants. Nudges and urges to investigate came from the other room. More rustling and exclamations paired with more piles being tumbled over. Eventually with enough courage a head poked out from behind the door of the bedroom to investigate, perhpash against their better judgement.

**Revision:**
> It was the crash that woke her.
>
> Stepping out into the living room she found a man tearing through boxes, hurling their contents across the floor.
>
> She watched for a moment before interrupting, "Do you mind?"

**What changed:** Three paragraphs to two sentences plus a line of dialogue. The reader enters at the moment of contact, not the moment before.

**Spot it in new material:** Find the first line of dialogue or action that couldn't be cut without losing something. Start there. Everything before it is staging, and staging is for the writer.

---

## Operation 2: Kill the Narrator's Emotion Labels
### (Principle 1 — The Body Knows First)

**The move:** The discovery draft names emotions. The revision finds the body feeling them. This is the most important operation in the document, because it's the most-violated principle.

**Discovery:**
> Bewilderment had mostly replaced any fear left.

And:
> Emotions boiled over.
> Tears formed at the corners of her eyes, she slumped and let them out.

**This one is worth seeing worked in real time. From the editorial session:**

*What was submitted:*
> The wind kicked up as a truck zipped by, taking the ticket with it.
> Rolling into the street, and catching a ride on a puddle, the ticket, and her last bit of hope disappeared down a drain.
> Emotions boiled over,
> Tears formed at the corners of her eyes, she slumped and let them out.

*The diagnosis (verbatim from the session):*
> "Emotions boiled over" — cut this entirely. It's narration announcing what the next line is about to show. If the tears do their job, you don't need a thesis statement before them.

*The rewrite:*
> The wind kicked up as a truck zipped by. The ticket skipped across the sidewalk, caught the gutter, and was gone.
>
> Her throat tightened. She sat down, put her hands flat on the table, and just… stopped. Everything stopped. The trying, the holding it together, the pretending any of this was something she could manage. She pressed her palms against the plastic and let it come.

**What changed:** "Tears" never appears. "Crying" never appears. A physical sequence — throat, hands, table, the stopping — makes the reader experience the collapse. The ticket gets a clean death: three verbs, no whimsy. The whimsical version ("catching a ride on a puddle") was cut because whimsy undercuts a gut-punch.

**Spot it in new material:**
- Find every sentence that names an emotion directly: fear, bewilderment, sadness, panic, joy.
- Ask what it feels like in the body.
- Replace the label with one or two physical details, then the action that follows.
- If you still need the word "tears," the physical setup isn't doing its job yet.

---

## Operation 3: Compress Dialogue to Its Minimum
### (Principles 10, 13 — Subtext; Read It Aloud)

**The move:** The discovery draft runs dialogue until a point is thoroughly made. The revision stops one exchange before that.

**Discovery (cookbook retrieval):**
> "Wheres your cookbook?" was all the faceless head said.
> "My what?"
> "Your cookbook, surely you have a cookbook."
> "Probably in the kitchen." She pointed sheepishly from behind the man.
> "In the box," she corrected. "marked cookbooks."
> [Long internal narration about Pathwell hurling books]
> "Anything hand written? Old family recipes, index cards, anything!?" His despiration began to boil over into frustration.
> "Im sorry but who are you, and what are you doing in my apartment?" she asked sheepishly.

**Revision:**
> "Wheres your cookbook?" the stranger said without looking up.
> Another box hitting the ground, "The old one."
> "Excuse me?"
> "We need it."
> A thud hit the door. Wet. Heavy.
> "Now"
> "They're in the kitchen?"

**What changed:** Seven exchanges become four fragments and a stage direction. The urgency the discovery draft builds through volume ("His despiration began to boil over") is built instead through compression. Less text reads faster; faster reads as more urgent.

**Spot it in new material — the rule of thumb:** Two rounds. Pathwell wins or deflects in two exchanges. If he's still in the conversation at the third, he's overstaying. Whatever the last thing he says is, ask whether it would be stronger if he said nothing.

---

## Operation 4: Move Magic From Visual to Sensory
### (Principle 1 — The Body Knows First)

**The move:** The discovery draft describes magic as something you watch. The revision describes it as something you feel.

**Discovery:**
> A glowing swirling shape formed between his palm and the page, a ring of circles framed the shape as it spun. She wanted to panic, everything in her body told her to run out of the room, but the warmth, the warmth of golden orange shapes, how they swirled... he pulled back and sidearmed the light, it flung off his hand in a glob, stretching out behind itself, before smacking her in the body. She felt the blob hit her, but it didn't feel like anything but warmth.

**Revision:**
> He placed his hand flat on the page, the letters glowed a soft gold.
>
> [...]
>
> Elizabeth snatched the book, leaving the page behind, "What are you doing!?"
>
> Everything went white, and the scent hit her; buttery and sweet. Then a taste... and something else. A voice she hadn't heard in years.
>
> She was twelve again, baking cookies with her grandmother. Sun shining through an open window above the sink, the fresh smell of rain filled her lungs. Her grandmother smiled at her, and Elizabeth felt something she hadn't felt in years.
>
> Her body moved back before she did, sending her stumbling into the wall.

**What changed:** The visual spectacle — rings, shapes, globs of light — is stripped away. The magic is rendered as smell, taste, sound, and memory, experienced from inside Elizabeth rather than observed from outside. The discovery draft explains what the spell does; the revision makes the reader feel it as Elizabeth does.

**And the fight, same operation:**

*Discovery:*
> A medieval gauntlet of dark purple, adorned with spikes, and gold trim covered to his forearm as he punched the night... a milkyway of gold dust stared the air around it, dragging behind him in swirls of thick air.

*Revision:*
> It lunged for the light in the man's hand, where it was met sharply by a blinding strike.
>
> The impact sang.
>
> A clear bright note that rang through the room.

A visual description becomes a sound. "The impact sang" is three words doing the work of a paragraph.

**Spot it in new material:** When a spell is on the page, close Elizabeth's eyes. What reaches her anyway — smell, taste, sound, temperature? Magic is not a light show; it's an experience.

---

## Operation 5: Give Elizabeth the Wrong Detail
### (Principle 6 — Characters Want Things; characterization through focus)

**The move:** In the discovery draft Elizabeth responds to the most important thing. In the revision she responds to the wrong thing — and the wrongness is her character.

**Discovery:**
> "Why do you have my diary? Wait... where did you get my diary?" [after the blob fight and the destruction of the apartment]

A reasonable response. The problem: reasonable responses make a passive character look competent, which works against the characterization.

**Revision:**
> "What. What just happened? Why are you holding my diary?"

Still the diary — but now foregrounded as the thing she zeroes in on after an impossible event. The stutter ("What. What just happened?") shows her mind catching before it lands on the wrong priority.

**The same instinct, fully developed (Ch. 2):**
> "I have a meeting at nine tomorrow."
> "I swear it's really just around the corner."
> "My landlord is going to kill me. How am I going to explain the door? I have to get my dry cleaning before the meeting. Where am I going to get a new door? How am I going to even get to the meeting? Do you think someone's going to steal my stuff? Should I call the cops?"

**What changed:** She's just escaped a blob attack and her first priority is her nine o'clock meeting. The dry cleaning is the detail that makes it land — so specific, so useless. She dissociates into logistics because the management reflex fires even when management is impossible.

**Spot it in new material:** Any time Elizabeth might respond to the actually important thing, ask what she'd focus on instead. Her priorities under pressure are her characterization.

---

## Operation 6: Let the Body Act Before the Mind
### (Principle 1 — The Body Knows First)

**The move:** The discovery draft has characters do things they decided to do. The revision has their bodies act before they decide — including the decisions that move the whole plot. This is Principle 1 applied to action and to choice, not just to emotion.

**Discovery (entering the bookstore):**
> She edged closer to the frame, looking down to see if there was a drop. The linted red carpet met the floor of the street. She didn't move, lost in a blank thought with her head halfway through the frame. She snapped back to her senses surrounded by shelves organized in a respected chaos.

She considers the entry carefully, then snaps in.

**Revision:**
> She tapped her foot on the carpet through the portal, placing it with calculated weight to lean even slightly through. The room around her spun, and took what footing she did have with it, toppling her into a shelf of books.

She doesn't enter — she falls in. The room takes her. Funnier, and truer to her: she doesn't choose the magical world, she loses her footing into it.

**Discovery (leaving the apartment):**
> Elizabeth ran back to her bedroom, grabbing her sneakers, phone, and purse. She returned to him halfway out the door waving her on.

**Revision:**
> Her stomach dropped as the wave of rot hit her.
>
> A wet thud hit from down the hall.
>
> Her heart skipped and without thinking, she jumped into her shoes, and she threw on a coat.

She doesn't decide to get her shoes; her heart skips and she jumps into them. The wet thud does the deciding.

**Now the high-stakes version — the choice that ends Chapter 2:**

*Discovery (why she follows Pathwell):*
> "You dont have to come with me... Or you really were attacked by a giant suckerfish blob, not from your dimension... You really want to find out the answer to that on your own?"

She follows because he makes a good argument.

*Revision:*
> She sat there for a moment staring at the table, when the glow of the neon dragon flickered off, leaving her in the darkness.
>
> A flash of warmth hit her, she could taste a trace of butter and sugar on her lips.
>
> He still has it.

She follows because she tastes her grandmother's cookies and realizes he has the cookbook. The motivation moves from intellectual (his argument persuades her) to physical (her body remembers what her mind hadn't focused on). This matters structurally: if his argument moves her, the choice is a response to him; if her own sensation moves her, the choice is hers. (This is also Principle 7 — a real rung on the agency ladder has to be hers.)

**Spot it in new material:** Any moment a character makes a decision, ask whether the body could make it first — a stomach drop, a skipped heartbeat, a taste — while the mind is still catching up. Especially watch for Elizabeth deciding things by reasoning; replace the reasoning with sensation.

---

## Operation 7: Remove Pathwell's Explanations
### (Principles 2, 9 — Write the Sequel; Incomplete Answers Build Worlds)

**The move:** The discovery draft Pathwell explains himself, his world, his intentions. The revision Pathwell deflects, qualifies, and moves on.

**Discovery (introduction):**
> "My name is my own, and I go by many of them, some obscure, and some common, a few you may have heard before, but for our interactions today, you may call me Mr. Pathwell." He said sticking his hand out as a formal introduction. "Pleased to meet you."

**Revision:**
> "Right, so…" Tucking the diary under his arm, "We should probably get going."
>
> [Elizabeth protests]
>
> "Understandable. But your house has now been marked, magically speaking. So we should probably be anywhere but here."

He never introduces himself as Pathwell in Chapter 1. The narrative just calls him Pathwell.

**Discovery (after the blob fight):**
> "I needed something to fight back with." Moving the hair from in front of his face, he held the diary up in the air and shook it, "Diaries, come jam packed with all sorts of emotion. Yours happened to come with a bit of angst and depression, which was great in the time that I needed it, but as you can see comes with its own drawbacks."

**Revision:**
> The man glanced around, "That? That was nothing. Just cleaning up a mess."

**What changed:** Eight words instead of forty, and the eight do more — they reveal his register, deflect her question, and characterize a world where blob attacks are routine. (This is Principle 9 directly: the incomplete answer builds the world the complete answer would have flattened.)

**Pathwell's pattern in the revision:**
- States the situation once, briefly
- Qualifies it ("mostly gone," "magically speaking," "the right kind of bad")
- Moves on or walks away
- Never argues twice

## Operation 8: Trust the Ending
### (Principles 1, 10 — The Body Knows First; Subtext)

**The move:** The discovery draft endings say what the image already showed. The revision endings stop at the image.

**Discovery (end of Chapter 2):**
> She sat not looking at anything in particular, just empty space, her mind wandering through the night.
>
> What if this really was all some dream?
>
> Then it wouldnt really matter where she went to bed. Waking up would put her right back in her bed. And things would continue on, like they have been.
>
> What if this really was all some dream?
>
> What if it had been real?
>
> What harm was there in dreaming?
>
> "One lo mein to go!"

**Revision:**
> She sat there for a moment staring at the table, when the glow of the neon dragon flickered off, leaving her in the darkness.
>
> A flash of warmth hit her, she could taste a trace of butter and sugar on her lips.
>
> He still has it.

**What changed:** The discovery draft makes us watch Elizabeth talk herself into a decision. The revision ends with the decision already made — not as thought but as sensation (warmth, taste) and then a single declarative statement. "He still has it" is three words for a complete character turn. The dragon flickering off removes the scene's comfortable warmth; the butter and sugar is the structural echo of the cookie spell. She's not thinking about the cookbook — her body is.

**The pattern:**
Discovery ending: What if → What if → What harm → [action]
Revision ending: image → sensation → three words

**Spot it in new material:** If your ending can be summarized as "character decides to X," it's too long. The ending is the moment *after* the decision, felt in the body, expressed in the world. If a sentence follows the final image, it's probably explaining the image. Cut it.

---

## Operation 9: Show Process, Not Result
### (Principles 5, 11 — Iceberg; One Unexplained Thing)

**The move:** The discovery draft tells you a magical thing happened. The revision makes you watch it happen — and slips in one detail it never explains.

**Discovery (bookstore door):**
> Pulling a folded piece of paper from his pocket, and pressing it against the brick wall behind her. A small square glowed behind it. Expanding into a doorway as he unfolded the sheet, the paper dissolving into the wall.

**Revision:**
> He shifted the diary and cookbook under one arm, pulling a folded piece of paper from his pocket with the other. Then pressing it flat against the concrete wall behind her.
>
> Light bled through the creases, spiderwebbing along the wall. A soft square, no bigger than the paper itself, glowing against the grey. He peeled back a layer and the square doubled. Another layer, and it doubled again. The light stretched past the edges of the paper until a doorway stood where the wall had been, warm and humming faintly.

**What changed:** Discovery draft result — "a small square glowed, expanded, the paper dissolved." Revision process — light bleeds, spiderwebs, a layer peeled, doubles, doubles again, stretches, until. Each step shown. The world is built through accumulation of physical fact, not summary of outcome.

**And the revision adds a detail the discovery draft doesn't have:**
> From somewhere inside, a loose page drifted out, spinning once in the air before settling at her feet. The handwriting was faded, but the ink smelled like lavender.

A page that smells like lavender, never explained. The reader fills in what it means — love letter? old garden diary? The mystery does more than any description. (Principle 11.)

**Spot it in new material:** This is a targeted move, not a default. Use it for threshold-crossing moments — the cookie reveal, the bookstore door, the pruning ceremony. For those, slow down and show every step. (Don't apply it to ordinary action; that's what Operation 1 cuts.)

# PART THREE: SENTENCE-LEVEL FAULTS

The operations work at scene level. These are the smaller habits flagged line by line in the sessions — the ones that recur draft after draft and have to be hunted individually on a close pass. Each has a name (so you can flag it fast), the reason it fails, and the fix.

### The Dangling Modifier
The most persistent habit in the manuscript. An opening phrase attaches grammatically to the wrong subject.
> "Her head spinning around seeing a park at the end of another street."

Grammatically, the park is doing the spinning. Also: "Rolling into the street, and catching a ride on a puddle, the ticket…" — grammatically Elizabeth is rolling.
**Fix:** Make the phrase's subject the same as the sentence's subject, or break it into two clean sentences. *Hunt:* any sentence that opens with an "-ing" phrase. Check what it's actually modifying.

### The Camera Angle (passive narration)
The narrator describes Elizabeth from outside instead of from inside her.
> "She could only watch as the wind snatched it away."
> "Tears formed at the corners of her eyes, she slumped and let them out."

"Could only watch" is a camera position, not an experience. "Tears formed at the corners of her eyes" is clinical — it's filming her face, not inhabiting her. (This is the sentence-level version of Operation 2.)
**Fix:** Drop into the body. What does she feel from the inside before the tears arrive? *Hunt:* "could only," "watched as," "found herself," and any external description of her own face or eyes.

### The Cliché
A pre-made phrase that slides past without making an image.
> "her curiosity getting the better of her" · "her voice left her" · "a smile crept across her face"

These require no thought from writer or reader. "Her voice left her" — does that mean her throat closed, she whispered, she couldn't speak? It's vague because it's borrowed.
**Fix:** Replace with a specific image. "'Uh—' was all she managed" beats "her voice left her." *Hunt:* ask of any emotional phrase, "have I read this exact phrasing before?" If yes, it's probably a cliché.

### The Doubled Phrase
The same words appear twice in one sentence or in adjacent sentences, usually unnoticed.
> "Pathwell popped the lid off the container and flipped it over in front of her, placing a dumpling in front of her."

"In front of her" twice. The repetition is invisible to the writer and grating to the reader.
**Fix:** Cut one. *Hunt:* read sentences aloud (Principle 13) — the ear catches doubling the eye misses.

### The Blocking
Choreography that narrates physical motion not worth the space.
> "Pathwell popped the lid off the container and flipped it over in front of her, placing a dumpling…"

The lid, the flip, the container — none of it matters. The beat is the gesture: he gives her a dumpling without being asked. Everything else is stage direction.
**Fix:** Find the one gesture that carries meaning; cut the choreography around it. *Hunt:* sequences of small physical verbs (popped, flipped, reached, turned). Ask which single action is the actual beat.

### The Tag That Does the Work
A dialogue tag carrying emotion the dialogue itself should carry.
> "'I like the Thai one,' Elizabeth said meekly."

"Meekly" tells us her state because the line doesn't show it. Two "said"s in a row is the related fault.
**Fix:** Put the emotion in the words ("'I like… the Thai one?'"), or in an action, or cut the tag — context usually tells us who's speaking.
*Hunt:* any adverb on a dialogue tag; any two tags in a row.

### The Thesis Statement
A sentence announcing the emotion the next sentence is about to demonstrate.
> "Emotions boiled over,
> Tears formed at the corners of her eyes…"

"Emotions boiled over" is a heading for the line beneath it. If the demonstration works, the heading is redundant. (Sentence-level Principle 8.)
**Fix:** Cut the announcement. Trust the demonstration. *Hunt:* any abstract emotional summary immediately followed by a concrete one — keep the concrete, cut the abstract.

### The Whimsy Undercut
A cute phrase placed in a moment that needs weight.
> "the ticket… catching a ride on a puddle"

Charming, but the laundry ticket is a gut-punch beat. Whimsy drains the punch.
**Fix:** Match register to stakes. Save the wordplay for light beats; let heavy beats land plainly ("caught the gutter, and was gone"). *Hunt:* clever phrasing in scenes that are supposed to hurt.

# PART FOUR: THE VOICE REFERENCE

To write in this voice you have to hear each register. These are tuning forks.

---

## The Narrator

Dry, precise, slightly absurdist. Treats the extraordinary as unremarkable. Doesn't react.

**Sounds like:**
> The neon dragon flickered against the night.

> The chorus sang one more time before the apartment shook before the noise stopped.

> The living room had exploded, covered in bits of rotting gelatinous ooze.

**Doesn't:**
- Express surprise at magical events
- Editorialize about characters' choices
- Add an exclamation about what just happened
- Summarize a scene's emotional significance

**Test:** Read a narrator sentence aloud. If it sounds like you're *telling* someone what happened, it's wrong. If it sounds like you're *looking* at what happened, it's right.

---

## Pathwell

- Formal phrasing that drops into casual mid-sentence
- Qualifies rather than explains ("mostly gone," "the right kind of bad," "magically speaking")
- Deflects by moving to logistics ("Do you have shoes?")
- Never argues twice. States and moves on.
- When wrong-footed, throat-clears ("Right, so…")

**Sounds like:**
> "Understandable. But your house has now been marked, magically speaking. So we should probably be anywhere but here."

> "You can come out now. It's… mostly gone."

> "Oh fine, you can have a dumpling."

> "That? That was nothing. Just cleaning up a mess."

> "It's not a dream. Just a really weird Tuesday for you."

**Tells:**
- "Right, so…" — the throat-clearing transition
- One-word agreement before a deflection: "Understandable. But—"
- Precision about the wrong things: "They're the right kind of crappy, thank you. You have no idea how hard it is to find this kind of quality."
- He never finishes an explanation he started

---

## Elizabeth

- Short, declarative, repeatedly pulled to blunted affect
- Responds to the wrong detail under pressure
- Rises to anger, then drops to flat
- Single-word answers when overwhelmed

**Sounds like:**
> "No."

> "Home."

> "It's Thursday."

> "Do you mind?"

> "What. What just happened? Why are you holding my diary?"

**Escalation:**
Discovery draft — she argues, demands explanations.
Revision — she spirals into logistics, then goes flat.

**Test:** If Elizabeth is making a complete, logical argument, she's been given too many words. Her power is brevity and misdirected focus.

# THE CHECKLIST

Three passes, coarse to fine. Run them in order — there's no point hunting doubled phrases in a paragraph you're about to cut.

**Pass 1 — Structure (the questions from Part One).** Decide what the scene is and whether it earns its place before touching the prose.
- What does the character **want** here? If nothing, give them a want or cut the scene. (P6)
- Does this scene connect to the next by **therefore/but**, not and then? (P3)
- What **value changed** top to bottom? If nothing, it's a non-event. (P4)
- Did anyone **explain** something the story showed or is about to show? (P8)
- Is there **one thing left unexplained**? (P11)

**Pass 2 — Operations (scene-level moves from Part Two).**
1. Starts with setup rather than action? Cut to first contact. → Op 1
2. Names an emotion directly? Find the body feeling it. → Op 2
3. Any dialogue exchange past its second round? Cut the last exchange. → Op 3
4. Magic described visually? Find the non-sight sense. → Op 4
5. Elizabeth responding to the important thing? Give her the wrong detail. → Op 5
6. A character deciding to act? Let the body act first; replace reasoning with sensation. → Op 6
7. Pathwell explaining himself? Cut to one sentence; let him walk away. → Op 7
8. A sentence after the final image? Cut it. → Op 8
9. A threshold moment summarized rather than shown? Show each step, leave one detail unexplained. → Op 9

**Pass 3 — Sentence faults (the close pass, from Part Three).** Read aloud and hunt:
- Opening "-ing" phrases → check the **dangling modifier**
- "could only," "watched as," descriptions of her own face → the **camera angle**
- Familiar emotional phrasing → the **cliché**
- Repeated words in or across sentences → the **doubled phrase**
- Strings of small physical verbs → the **blocking**
- Adverbs on dialogue tags, or two tags in a row → the **tag that does the work**
- An abstract emotion line followed by a concrete one → the **thesis statement**
- Cleverness in a scene meant to hurt → the **whimsy undercut**

And underneath all three passes, the working method (Part Zero): **diagnose in a batch, fix one at a time, name the transferable technique, expect to run the passes again next draft.**

---

# ONE WARNING

The sugar cookie scene is the ceiling. It is the best single piece of prose in the manuscript, and it does almost everything in this document at once — body before mind, magic as sensation, the metaphor performed instead of explained, an emotion delivered without being named. Every other scene is tested against it, not the reverse.

When a scene isn't working and you've run the checklist, read the cookie scene again. What's missing is usually somewhere in those fifteen lines.
