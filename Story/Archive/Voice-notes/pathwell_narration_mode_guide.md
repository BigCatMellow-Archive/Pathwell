# Pathwell: Clinical vs. Oral-Storytelling Narration

*When to use each mode, and what the narrator's irreverence actually means.*

---

## The Two Modes

**Clinical narration** — precise, efficient, no narrator commentary. Sentences do one thing: report action, position, or sensation. Moves fast. Lets the reader feel without being told what to feel.

**Oral-storytelling narration** — the narrator is present, opinionated, slightly irreverent. Sentences interpret what they describe. The narrator talks like a person who noticed something and wants you to understand exactly why it was odd.

Neither is better. The book lives by knowing which one to use.

---

## The Narrator's Voice

When the narrator is present in this book, they speak like someone who:

- Qualifies correctly: "mostly gone," "not yet claimed," "neater than it had any right to be"
- Interprets without naming the theme: "the ghost of a pot that had been going since before this conversation"
- Makes dry observations that are accurate without being unkind: "the practiced patience of someone who had heard everything"
- Notes the wrong detail, like Pathwell does, but from outside: "which was saying something"

The narrator does NOT explain what the reader can already feel. They add what the reader can't feel without them.

**The narrator is slightly irreverent.** That means they are not impressed by the weight of their own material. They will describe something momentous with casual precision and something mundane with inexplicable seriousness. That gap is the register.

---

## When to Use Oral-Storytelling Narration

- **Atmospheric and transitional passages** — entering a new space, driving at night, the texture of a place before anything happens in it
- **Elizabeth's POV during observation** — when she's noticing something and the narrator can add what she can't yet articulate
- **Narrator characterization of recurring figures** — Stansbury's measured patience, Pathwell's centuries-old ease with inconvenience
- **Interpretive moments** — when a sensory detail needs the narrator to say *why* it stuck, not just *that* it was there
- **Register-maintenance** — when the tone is drifting clinical and needs the dry wit to pull it back

---

## When to Use Clinical Narration

- **Shade scenes** — his register IS eerie clinical precision. The narrator matching his flatness amplifies the wrongness. Any interpretive aside during his dialogue breaks the effect.
- **Action sequences** — blob fight, crash, the archive fire. Momentum is the point. Narrator commentary during action is a speed bump.
- **Pathwell's logistics in motion** — he is appetite, trajectory, administration. The narration should match his pace, not comment on it.
- **Dialogue-heavy scenes** — the voices carry the register. Narration steps back.
- **Elizabeth's agency moments** — when she cuts out of the blob, when she turns the wheel, when she spends the diary — the action is the sentence. No narrator overlay. She does the thing. Full stop.

---

## Red Flags

**Clinical where oral-storytelling is needed:**
- Narration reads as stage direction with no voice behind it
- Sensory details listed without any feeling about them
- Transitions feel like a report rather than a person navigating their memory
- The narrator disappears for pages and the prose goes flat

**Oral-storytelling where clinical is needed:**
- Narrator commentary during action slows what needs to move
- Interpreter steps in during Shade dialogue and softens his eerie register
- Narrator is present when Elizabeth is acting — she should carry those moments, not be observed through them
- Comedy or irreverence arrives in a scene that needs to stay tense

---

## Benchmark Examples from the Draft

**Right move — oral-storytelling:**

> "The bar was neater than it had any right to be."
Narrator has an opinion about the bar before the scene starts. Sets the register.

> "the ghost of a pot that had been going since before this conversation"
Narrator interprets the coffee. Adds what Elizabeth feels but doesn't think.

> "She didn't say this."
Four-word narrator aside. Present, dry, does its work and leaves.

> "which was saying something"
Narrator makes a quiet observation about the situation. Oral-storytelling in five words.

**Right move — clinical:**

> "'Stay,' Shade said, and the third gust said it too."
Shade gets no narrator interpretation. His voice plus the action is the whole register.

> "She cut."
Two words. Elizabeth acts. No commentary.

> "She turned."
Two words. The wheel. The chapter ends. No one explains it.

---

## The Test

Before adding narrator voice to a passage, ask: *Is the narrator adding something the action can't say, or explaining what the action already said?*

If explaining: cut it. If adding: keep it.
