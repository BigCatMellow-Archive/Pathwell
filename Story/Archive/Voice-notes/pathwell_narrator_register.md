# Pathwell — Narrator Register Guide

Two registers in the narration: **clinical precision** and **irreverent oral storytelling**. Both are correct. Knowing when to use each is the difference between narration that lands and narration that's just stage direction.

This guide is the Pathwell-specific companion to `cadence-rhythm-euphony-ai-review-guide.md` and `natural-oral-storytelling-prose-ai-guide.md`. Read those first for the general framework. This file applies it to this book.

---

## The Irreverent Narrator

The narrator of this book is slightly irreverent. Not the author breaking the fourth wall — the narrator noticing things with a dry, sideways attitude. Like someone telling you this story who finds it both serious and faintly ridiculous, and lets that show in what they choose to look at.

The narrator's irreverence surfaces in:

- **What the narrator notices** — The darts still in the board while a blob fight happened outside. The wrong detail at the serious moment. This is the narrator's version of Pathwell's wrong-detail response.
- **Narrative self-correction** — "Or it looked empty, which turned out to be a different thing." The narrator noticing the gap between first impression and reality.
- **Dry interpretation** — "Then he stayed there awhile longer anyway." The "anyway" is attitude. The "probably" is attitude. Small words that carry the narrator's view without announcing it.
- **Emotional interpretation without explaining** — "The wheel sat under her hands, steady and real." The narrator gives Elizabeth's anchor without saying it was her anchor.
- **Noting the absence** — "She didn't scream." "Nobody looked up when Pathwell walked through." What didn't happen tells you what was expected. The irreverent narrator uses this.

The narrator sounds like someone who has seen Pathwell do this before and is choosing, deliberately, to report it without comment. That restraint IS the irreverence.

## When to Use Clinical Narration

**During physical action and urgency:**
Blob fights, crashes, magical events — precision serves speed. Short, hard sentences. The narrator gets out of the way. Let the action happen without attitude.

**During Shade scenes:**
Shade's register is clinical precision. The narration should match his energy. When Shade is in frame, the narrator adopts something close to his eerie exactness. The wrong tone is warmth or looseness.

**Magic mechanics and sensory experience:**
Let the sensation carry the magic. The sugar-cookie scene works because the narrator doesn't interpret it. Body before mind, sense before understanding. The narrator's opinion would puncture it.

**The punchline:**
Deliver the wrong detail clean, then stop. "He straightened his jacket." Full stop. Do not follow it with the narrator noticing that he straightened his jacket. The reader sees it. The narrator's job is done.

**Breath moments after action:**
"The dust settled." "Then nothing." Three-word lines that give the reader a beat. No attitude here — pure landing.

## When to Use Oral Storytelling Narration

**Scene-setting and arrival:**
How we enter a location. The narrator has opinions about places — the bar that's "cared for, in the way things get cared for when people have been coming to them for a long time," the diner with its sign that "simply said DINNER 24/7." The narrator's choice of detail tells you how to feel about the place before the characters do anything in it.

**Character observations:**
When Pathwell does something characteristically Pathwell, the narrator can give it one line of interpretation. "He walked in like he owned the place, which seemed to be how he walked everywhere." The "which seemed to be" is the narrator's slightly raised eyebrow.

**Transition and connective tissue:**
The sentences between scenes are where the narrator's voice should be most present. If a transition feels like stage direction (X happened, then Y happened), ask whether the narrator should notice something in that gap.

**What Elizabeth hasn't named yet:**
Elizabeth's POV is limited and body-first — she doesn't always know what she's feeling. The narrator can see slightly more. Not much. A single line. The narrator tells you what Elizabeth's hands are doing and trusts you to read what it means.

**Aftermath:**
After big moments — a fight, a sacrifice, a revelation — the narrator can take a breath and notice one wrong detail in the scene. The camp going on with the fiddle while Elizabeth stands outside the wagon. Pathwell staying in the ditch "awhile longer anyway." The emotional weight stays with the reader; the narrator moves on.

## The Grammar of Narrator Attitude

These are the small words and structures that carry the narrator's irreverence. Use them sparingly; they lose power if overused.

| Technique | Example |
|---|---|
| "probably" | "Pathwell's darts were probably still in the board." |
| "anyway" | "Then he stayed there awhile longer anyway." |
| "which turned out to be" | "Or it looked empty, which turned out to be a different thing." |
| "seemed to be" | "which seemed to be how he walked everywhere" |
| "simply" | "a sign that simply said DINNER 24/7" |
| "of course" | reserved — strongest, use once per chapter maximum |
| "as if it had personally X" | "examining a knot in the wood as if it had personally disappointed him" |
| noting absence | "Nobody looked up when Pathwell walked through." |
| "which was saying something" | careful — can read as cliché if overused |

## The Key Test

After writing any narration line, ask:

1. Is this pure stage direction (X happened, then Y happened)?
2. If yes — does the clinical delivery serve this moment (action, precision, punchline, breath)?
3. If no — where could the narrator have an attitude about what just happened, without explaining what happened?

The narrator is a person in the room, watching with a slightly raised eyebrow. Not commenting out loud. Just noticing the wrong thing at the right moment.

---

## What the Irreverent Narrator Does NOT Do

- Explain the joke or the gap. If the narrator says "which was funny" or "which was characteristic of him" — cut it.
- Tell the reader what to feel. "This was significant." "She felt sad." Cut.
- Comment on theme. The thesis is in the action. The narrator has nothing to add.
- Break the dry register to become warm or sentimental. The narrator cares about these people, but the narrator doesn't say so.
- Add commentary after a punchline. The punchline is the last word. The narrator moves on.

---

## Calibration Lines

Read these when the narrator's voice is drifting. These are what it sounds like when the register is right:

*"Or it looked empty, which turned out to be a different thing."*

*"He walked in like he owned the place, which seemed to be how he walked everywhere."*

*"Then he stayed there awhile longer anyway."*

*"The cannon was still pointed at the front door."*

*"Nobody looked up when Pathwell walked through."*

*"There were fewer than the moment required."*

Each of these is the narrator noticing something with a dry attitude and trusting the reader to catch it. That's the register. Write toward it.
