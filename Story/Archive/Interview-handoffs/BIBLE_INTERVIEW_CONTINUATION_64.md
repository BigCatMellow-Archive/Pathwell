## Audit Question 125 — What practical safety limit should Stansbury's foam weapon design represent?

This question is **UNRESOLVED**. The user has not answered it yet.

Context:
- `character_bible.md` says Stansbury wants to arm people but gives them toys instead of real weapons; this contradiction expresses his over-protective/control flaw.
- `Chapter_05.txt` presents foam swords/daggers that look like toy-aisle weapons but carry genuine magical effects.
- Q106 locks that a finished pre-imbued weapon can be activated through ordinary physical use by a non-practitioner.
- Q122 locks that Stansbury routinely keeps these tools as working inventory and sells them as a source of income.
- Q123 locks that buyers come through an informed magic-aware network, not unsuspecting public retail.
- Q124 locks that the tools are reusable/durable, though not infinitely permanent; wear, damage, time, or magical strain can eventually require repair/re-imbuing.
- No existing support defines whether foam itself limits harm or whether Stansbury's caution lies in the effects he chooses to imbue.

Options:

**A. Foam itself imposes a broad magical safety cap.**
- Even when imbued, foam weapons cannot become conventionally lethal against ordinary people.
- Makes the `toy instead of real weapon` contradiction mechanically literal.
- Downside: invents a universal magical property of foam/material that is not otherwise established.

**B. Foam is mostly symbolic/camouflage.**
- Depending on the effect, an imbued foam weapon could be as dangerous as a conventional weapon.
- Preserves maximum magical flexibility.
- Downside: weakens the idea that Stansbury is actually constraining other people's risk; the `toy` choice becomes mostly visual.

**C. No universal foam-safety rule; Stansbury deliberately designs his normal inventory around narrow defensive/disruptive effects while the mundane foam body remains physically toy-like.**
- Effects can repel, stagger, expose, contain, disrupt, or cut anomalous magical material.
- Individual/custom tools can still be dangerous, but his standard line is intentionally safety-biased rather than straightforwardly lethal.
- Preserves Elizabeth's dagger cutting a blob without declaring foam universally harmless to magic.
- Makes Stansbury's caution a **design philosophy**, not a new law of materials.

**D. Leave the safety distinction undefined.**
- The weapons simply work when needed.
- Downside: now that the weapons are recurring world infrastructure/business, the character-bible `gives them toys` contradiction becomes partly cosmetic.

**Audit lean: C.**
Reason: C preserves Stansbury's control/caution flaw without adding a universal foam rule. He genuinely arms people, but chooses effects that constrain how dangerous the tools he gives them are. A over-systematizes the material; B makes the toy form mostly aesthetic; D leaves a visible gap.
