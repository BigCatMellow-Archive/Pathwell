# Pathwell — When to Use Oral-Storytelling vs. Clinical Prose

*Decision guide for agents editing Pathwell chapters. Read alongside `natural-oral-storytelling-prose-ai-guide.md` and `pathwell_tone_register.md`.*

---

## The Core Principle

**Oral-storytelling prose** = narrator is present, slightly irreverent, interpreting events as a person who was there.

**Clinical/precise prose** = narrator steps back, counts the cost, lets the weight land without commentary.

In Pathwell both modes are correct — but they are correct in different places. The mistake is applying either one uniformly. The narrator should modulate.

---

## The Pathwell Narrator's Specific Flavor of Oral

This narrator is NOT chatty or warm. The irreverence is dry. Pratchett-adjacent: ideas smuggled into observations, the extraordinary noted with mild exasperation rather than wonder. The narrator does not editorialize loudly. It editorializes in a single word, or not at all.

**Oral in this book sounds like:**

> *The shop held opinions about itself.*

> *Mama Baga came out of the largest wagon and down three wooden steps with the deliberate pace of someone who has learned that most things are not worth the rush.*

> *Nobody looked up when Pathwell walked through.*

> *A woman read a man's palm with the focused disinterest of someone who had done it ten thousand times and still found it vaguely interesting.*

> *It smelled like tea and lanolin and something that was probably sage if sage had been sitting there for thirty years.*

What these have in common: the narrator is making a quiet judgment call. They are noticing not just what happened but what kind of thing it was. They allow themselves a small opinion. None of them announce that opinion.

**This narrator does NOT say:** "Which was strange." "She couldn't believe it." "That was odd." — If the narrator notices, the observation IS the interpretation. No label needed.

---

## When Oral Mode Is Right

Use oral-storytelling narration when:

### 1. Describing a place the narrator has stood in and assessed
Camp Cunnan, the bar, the shop interior — these are places with character. The narrator can have a take on them.

> *The bar was neater than it had any right to be.*

The narrator isn't reporting. They're telling you what they made of it.

### 2. Introducing a character through the narrator's impression
Not "a woman of sixty, broad and grey" but what she was like — how she registered.

> *She was broad and settled and she looked at Pathwell the way people look at a problem they are fond of.*

The narrator is describing the impression before the facts. Oral order.

### 3. Noting what the extraordinary doesn't do to ordinary people
The shopkeeper doing accounting. The camp not looking up. These are moments where the narrator's dry noticing IS the joke.

### 4. Transitions and connective tissue between scenes
Between action beats, the narrator can be present with a qualifying observation. This is where clinical narration most often goes flat and becomes pure stage direction.

### 5. Elizabeth's body-first processing
Her body acts, the narrator catches up alongside her — both arriving slightly behind the event. The narrator's slight irreverence is calibrated to Elizabeth's own dry detachment.

---

## When Clinical/Precise Mode Is Right

Use clean, precise, hard-edged narration when:

### 1. The cost is landing
A prune. A sacrifice. A turn of the wheel. When something permanent happens, the prose counts it without commentary. The narrator does not interpret the cost — the cost interprets itself.

> *Then it was over.*

> *Pathwell slipped out the door. Elizabeth stayed.*

> *She held it out.*

Three words. Two words. Four words. No narrator editorializing. The precision IS the weight.

### 2. Magic in transaction
The Space Between barter, the tear, the archive igniting — these are mechanical events with specific consequences. Clinical language makes the magic real. Explaining or interpreting it makes it smaller.

### 3. Shade's register
Shade is eerie precision. The narration around him should match. He doesn't have wrong-detail responses — he has too-right ones. The narrator near Shade becomes quieter and more exact, not warmer.

### 4. Action sequences under pressure
The blob fight, the crash, the archive fire — short declaratives, specific verbs, no narrator commentary. The narrator is trying to keep up, not observe. Clinical urgency.

### 5. The agency-ladder rungs
Elizabeth's choices must be stated plainly. The narrator does not interpret them for the reader — the choice is the point. If the narrator says "and she chose," that's enough. Any commentary softens the rung.

---

## The Decision Test

Before every paragraph of narration, ask:

**Is this a moment of consequence, or a moment of observation?**

- Consequence → clinical. Step back. Count it. Let it land.
- Observation → oral. Lean in. Give the narrator a quiet take.

**Is the narrator in the middle of something happening, or has it just happened?**

- Middle of happening → clinical (action, sequence, body-first)
- After it happened, moving on → oral (interpretation, the narrator's light remark on what that was)

**Would the narrator pausing to remark here slow down something that needs to land?**

- Yes → cut the narrator's voice, keep the fact.
- No → let the narrator have the observation.

## The Narrator's Register

Slightly irreverent means: the narrator has seen enough to have a take, but doesn't announce it.

The narrator:
- Chooses words that carry a small judgment
- Notices what the scene reveals about character without naming the revelation
- Treats the extraordinary as socially understood (Pratchett's move)
- Has opinions but delivers them as observations

The narrator does NOT:
- Comment on the theme
- Name Elizabeth's feelings after the image already showed them
- Warm up to the reader (no "you might think" or "as you can imagine")
- Signal that a wrong response is wrong
- Explain the magic

## What to Watch For

**Oral mode misapplied (narrator speaks when it should be silent):**
- Narrator interprets a choice Elizabeth makes (softens the rung)
- Narrator remarks on the magic (should be sensation, not observation)
- Narrator signals that a wrong response is wrong (kills the gap)
- The chapter's most costly beat has a narrator's aside (deflects weight)

**Clinical mode misapplied (narrator absent when it should be present):**
- Pure stage direction between scenes ("She walked to the door. He was there.")
- Environment described as inventory without any narrator's take
- Characters introduced by facts alone, not by the impression they make
- Transitions that simply move bodies without any observation

---

## Special Case: The Gap

The tone register is built on the gap: real situation + wrong response. The narrator must be oral enough to set up the situation fully — and then must go clinical for the wrong response. The narrator never signals the gap is funny. It presents both sides with equal seriousness.

> *Pathwell was crouched by the wagon wheel, examining a knot in the wood as if it had personally disappointed him.* ← oral (narrator's wry setup)
>
> *"This wheel is new."* ← clinical (wrong response presented straight)
>
> *She looked at him.* ← clinical (Elizabeth's reaction, no narrator gloss)
>
> *"Newer," he said. "Relatively."* ← clinical (the joke lands straight)

The narrator's irreverence appeared in the setup ("personally disappointed him") and then vanished. The gap runs on its own.
