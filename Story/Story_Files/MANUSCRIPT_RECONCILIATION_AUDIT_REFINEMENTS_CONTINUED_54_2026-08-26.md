## Existing-story check: Stansbury's scar is intended to be permanent, but current canon does not yet explain why it stays

Current `Chapter_12b.txt` has Stansbury enter the archive fire after Elizabeth, emerge with a serious burn, and refuse Pathwell's immediate attempt to intervene: `It's fine.` / `Let it be.` The burn is wrapped and remains part of the aftermath.

Current `character_bible.md` gives the burn a larger function: Stansbury carries a **permanent scar Pathwell cannot undo**, described as the first cost Pathwell faces with no tool to fix it.

Earlier `Chapter_03.txt` also establishes a narrower limitation in Pathwell's own healing repertoire: after treating a small injury he says, `Unfortunately, scars are outside my wheelhouse.` That supports Pathwell personally being unable to erase an established scar, but it does not establish a universal rule that no practitioner or healer could reduce or remove one.

A repo search found no later support explaining whether Stansbury's permanent scar is:

- mechanically impossible to heal;
- outside Pathwell's abilities but potentially treatable by someone else;
- or deliberately left in place because Stansbury refuses extraordinary repair.

Because the story's larger theme centers on respecting choices and not treating every consequence as a problem Pathwell is entitled to solve, the reason the scar remains matters.

## Audit Question 117 — Why should Stansbury's archive-fire scar remain permanent?

### A. Stansbury accepts ordinary/necessary treatment but refuses extraordinary magical repair intended to erase the scar; Pathwell respects that boundary.

`Let it be` becomes more than an immediate line about the burn. Stansbury allows the injury to be stabilized and treated, but does not consent to Pathwell or another practitioner trying to remove the lasting mark simply because it can be treated as a problem to solve.

Pathwell's earlier `scars are outside my wheelhouse` line remains true, but the story does not need to establish that scars are universally unhealable. The scar remains because Stansbury chooses not to pursue extraordinary erasure and Pathwell, unlike earlier in the book, does not override that choice.

This makes the permanence both physical consequence and relational test: Pathwell cannot make the cost disappear by finding the right tool because the person carrying it has said to leave it alone.

### B. Establish a universal rule that magical healing cannot remove established scars.

This mechanically guarantees permanence, but adds a broad world rule primarily to protect one symbolic beat. It also weakens the importance of Stansbury's `Let it be` as a boundary.

### C. Keep only the existing narrower rule: Pathwell personally cannot heal scars, although another specialist theoretically could; never address why Stansbury does not seek one.

This preserves current lore with minimal additions, but makes `permanent` feel contingent rather than earned. The reader could reasonably wonder why Camp's healers or another specialist cannot eventually repair it.

### D. Let the scar eventually be healed/removed.

This removes the permanence problem but discards the character-bible payoff that Pathwell must live beside a consequence he cannot simply undo.

### Audit lean: **A**

A avoids inventing an absolute healing limitation and makes the scar's permanence serve the same boundary/agency logic as the rest of the ending. Stansbury is treated because treatment is necessary; erasing the lasting consequence is a different question, and he says no. Pathwell's growth is partly visible in leaving it alone. B over-systematizes healing. C leaves an obvious loophole. D removes the intended consequence.
