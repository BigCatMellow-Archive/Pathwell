## Existing-story check: Camp restitution is now concrete, but `you owe her for the rest` still risks turning Elizabeth's harm into another payable account

Q100 locks that Pathwell spends the three-week gap doing ordinary Camp-directed restitution: he stays, repairs/rebuilds what can be repaired, replaces recoverable practical losses, and does not use pruning or another shortcut. Irreplaceable archive records remain lost.

Chapter 12b also gives Stansbury a second line: `And Elizabeth. You owe her for the rest.`

That second obligation cannot automatically work like the Camp debt. Pathwell's core flaw is ledger-minded control: if something is lost or damaged, he decides what counts as an equivalent fix, pays the cost himself, and treats the balanced account as justification. Q99 deliberately preserves that residue in the coda: he considers another family's handwritten cookbook as a replacement for Nana's, and Elizabeth's `That isn't mine.` rejects the substitution.

A repo search did not locate an existing support lock defining a literal material payment, formal forgiveness, or other settlement of Pathwell's personal debt to Elizabeth. The story therefore needs to decide whether `you owe her for the rest` is actually payable, or whether part of Pathwell's growth is accepting that some interpersonal harm cannot be balanced by an equivalent transaction.

## Audit Question 101 — What does `you owe her for the rest` ultimately mean?

### A. It is a real moral obligation, but not a debt Pathwell can settle with an equivalent payment.

Pathwell cannot make Elizabeth whole by replacing the cookbook, paying for injuries, or finding a sufficiently costly object. What he owes is accountability expressed through changed behavior: respecting her choices, not deciding what repair should mean for her, and accepting that some consequences remain. The coda leaves this `account` intentionally unbalanced. His attempted cookbook substitution shows that he initially still thinks transactionally; `That isn't mine.` is the correction.

### B. Pathwell makes concrete material restitution to Elizabeth as well as to Camp.

He pays for practical damage, medical costs, lost property, or another measurable consequence. This could coexist with deeper accountability, but it risks making the story feel as though sufficiently complete compensation can settle the relational harm — exactly the logic the cookbook beat is challenging.

### C. Elizabeth explicitly forgives him and releases the debt.

This would make the relationship repair unmistakable, but it turns Elizabeth into the person who certifies Pathwell's redemption and creates a clean emotional settlement the current ending has intentionally avoided.

### D. Remove Stansbury's `And Elizabeth. You owe her for the rest.` line and keep only the Camp debt.

This avoids transactional language around Elizabeth entirely, but loses a useful contrast between damage that can be partly repaired materially and damage that must instead be lived with and answered through future behavior.

### Audit lean: **A**

A makes the two halves of Stansbury's line deliberately different. **Camp can demand concrete restitution because some burned structures and practical losses can be repaired. Elizabeth cannot be reduced to an account Pathwell can balance.** Pathwell may still try to treat her side that way when he considers the substitute cookbook, but the coda corrects that instinct without requiring Elizabeth to forgive him or declare the debt settled. B risks reproducing the ledger logic; C makes forgiveness structurally necessary; D removes a contrast that can clarify the theme rather than confuse it.
