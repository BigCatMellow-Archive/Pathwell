## Audit Question 108 — What did Camp actually ask Pathwell to do before the Chapter 3 transaction?

### A. Camp asked Pathwell to acquire needed supplies, but did not ask him to prune; Pathwell independently chose pruning as the quickest/easiest payment method.

Camp's need is legitimate. Pathwell's help is genuinely useful. The flaw lies in the method he selects and normalizes: `you need something; I can solve it by spending myself; therefore I will.` Camp does not authorize or require the pruning cost.

This also creates a low-stakes structural rhyme with Shade's creation: in both cases someone genuinely needs/asks for help, but Pathwell decides that pruning is the appropriate mechanism without being asked to use it. Chapter 3 shows the pattern when it works cleanly; Shade shows the same pattern when its consequences become catastrophic.

### B. Camp explicitly asked Pathwell to prune in order to obtain the supplies.

This would make the transaction fully authorized by the people benefiting from it, but it weakens Pathwell's responsibility for habitual pruning and conflicts with Mama Baga's established concern about the practice.

### C. Nobody asked Pathwell for supplies; he independently decided Camp needed them and took it upon himself to provide them.

This makes his self-appointed savior tendency even stronger, but may overstate Camp's dependence on him and make the family/community relationship less reciprocal than current material suggests.

### D. Camp asked him to get supplies and knew he would probably prune, but deliberately did not ask how he paid.

This adds a more compromised Camp culture around Pathwell's self-destruction. It is possible, but makes the community partially complicit in the very habit later material generally treats as Pathwell's problem.

### Audit lean: **A**

A keeps the need real and Pathwell's help meaningful while preserving responsibility for the dangerous shortcut. It also establishes the story's core pattern in an ordinary setting before the Shade reveal: Pathwell does not need malicious intent or selfish motive to violate boundaries around method and cost. B diffuses responsibility; C may make him too self-appointed; D creates a Camp-complicity thread the current book does not otherwise develop.
