# Pathwell — Prose Voice Reference

*Sourced from Chapters 1–2 (the benchmark) and established revision work. Every entry is observed from existing text, not invented.*

---

## WHAT THE PROSE IS

Third-person limited, Elizabeth's POV. Conversational. Dry. Slightly absurdist. Treats the extraordinary as socially understood. The prose does not explain what it shows. It trusts the reader to catch up.

The closest single-author reference: Pratchett. The specific Pratchett quality: ideas smuggled inside jokes, catastrophe met with the wrong response, philosophy arriving sideways.

---

## THE BENCHMARK

The sugar cookie scene in Chapter 1. Every other scene is tested against it.

What it does: magic is revealed through sensation, not exposition. Elizabeth doesn't understand what's happening. Her body does. The smell becomes a taste, the taste becomes a voice, the voice becomes a memory, the memory becomes a feeling she hasn't felt in years. Not one word of explanation. The reader understands because they felt it.

**Test:** Can this scene be explained in a sentence without losing what makes it matter? If yes, the prose isn't doing its job. The sugar cookie scene cannot be summarized — you have to be inside it.

---

## THE SIGNATURE MOVES

### 1. Body-First Interiority
The body acts before the mind understands. Elizabeth stumbles into the wall before she knows why. Her feet move before she decides. Her hand goes to the dagger before she's thought about the smell.

**Right:** *Her body moved back before she did, sending her stumbling into the wall.*

**Wrong:** *She felt scared, so she stepped back.*

The body is the tell. The understanding is always slightly behind.

---

### 2. Sensory Synesthesia
Sensation crosses sense categories. Smell becomes taste becomes sound becomes memory. Magic is felt as the wrong kind of thing — warmth where there should be none, a sound where there should be silence.

**Right:** *Everything went white, and the scent hit her; buttery and sweet. Then a taste... and something else. A voice she hadn't heard in years.*

The synesthesia earns its magic by moving through categories. It doesn't describe the magic — it enacts it.

---

### 3. Fragment as Percussion
Short sentences and fragments mark emphasis. They land hard and stop. They do not explain themselves.

**Right:**
*The impact sang.*

*Then nothing.*

*"Ready?" / "No." / "Perfect."*

Used sparingly. One fragment does more work than three. When in doubt, cut to the fragment and stop.

---

### 4. Isolation for Emphasis
Single-line paragraphs separate a moment from the noise around it. Used for beats that need to breathe, not for every beat.

**Right:**
*She wasn't sure when the tears started, only that she couldn't stop them.*

[paragraph break]

*Pathwell sat down brimming with anticipation before noticing Elizabeth.*

The break makes the pivot. Without it, the joke doesn't land — it's just the next sentence.

---

### 5. The "Mostly" Qualifier
Understatement through qualification. Things are not gone — they're "mostly gone." Not fine — "mostly fine." The qualifier does two things: it's funny, and it's honest. The situation is not resolved. Something remains.

Deploy when the dramatic situation is worse than the character is willing to admit.

---

### 6. The Wrong-Detail Response
A character notices or responds to the wrong thing. Pathwell, confronted with Elizabeth crying, offers a dumpling. Confronted with a destroyed apartment, he asks about shoes. Confronted with his arm shaking, he says "Surprise me."

The wrong detail is never random — it's precisely wrong. It's the thing the character would notice because of who they are. Pathwell notices appetite and logistics. Stansbury notices precision. The wrong detail is the character.

---

### 7. Ambient Atmosphere Without Commentary
The physical environment is noted but not interpreted. The neon dragon flickers. It doesn't mean anything. The city is in motion. That's all. The reader does the work.

**Right:** *The neon dragon flickered against the night.*

**Wrong:** *The neon dragon flickered against the night, a reminder that the city moved on regardless.*

Cut the "a reminder." The image is the reminder. Trust it.

---

### 8. Dialogue That Doesn't Explain Itself
Pathwell's dialogue is never expository. He doesn't explain his reasoning. He responds to the wrong thing, deflects, or accelerates. He says "mostly" and moves on. He offers dumplings.

The test: if you removed a line of Pathwell dialogue and the scene still made logical sense, the line is probably wrong. His dialogue should feel like it skipped a step.

## SENTENCE-LEVEL FAULTS

These are the specific weaknesses that appear in the discovery draft. Check for them at the sentence pass.

**Dangling modifier** — The modifier's subject is absent from the clause it's modifying. Easy to miss, kills clarity.

**Camera angle confusion** — The narration briefly describes something Elizabeth can't see from where she is. The POV slips.

**Cliché** — Any phrase that arrives pre-built. "Heart skipped a beat." "Eyes went wide." Replace with the specific.

**Doubled phrase** — Two phrases saying the same thing in sequence. "Quiet and still." "Pressed and trapped." Cut one.

**Stacked body actions** — Three or more physical actions in sequence during an exit or transition. "She looked at him, then at the door, then reached for her coat." Cut to one.

**Tag that does the work** — "she said angrily." The tag should be "said" or cut. If the line needs an adverb, rewrite the line.

**Thesis statement as prose fault** — A sentence that states the theme directly. Characters think it, narration notices it. Cut it. The action is the thesis.

**Catalog instinct under uncertainty** — When the scene's purpose is unclear, the prose adds more: more detail, more objects, more observations. Adding volume is not the same as adding clarity. When uncertain, cut — don't catalog.

## THE PROSE PASS CHECKLIST

Run this after structural revision is done. Not before.

1. Find every place Elizabeth understands something. Ask: does her body react first?
2. Find every sensory detail. Is it specific? Can you swap it for any other detail without losing something? If yes, find the specific.
3. Find every fragment. Is it doing percussion work, or is it just a short sentence? If just short, make it a full sentence or cut it.
4. Find every character response. Is it the right response or the wrong one? If right, ask whether wrong would be truer to who they are.
5. Find every qualifier. Is "mostly" doing its work, or is it hedging?
6. Read every line of Pathwell dialogue aloud. Does it sound like someone who has been alive for centuries and finds most things less urgent than you do? If not, cut or rewrite.
7. Find every place the prose explains an image it just showed. Cut the explanation.
8. Run the sentence-level faults check against every paragraph in the problem chapters (11, 8, 4, 12 — in that order).

---

## THE SINGLE TEST

Before finalizing any passage, ask:

*Is the prose experiencing this, or describing it?*

Description is what it looks like from outside. Experience is what it feels like from inside, arriving slightly before understanding, through the body first.

If it's describing, rewrite until it's experiencing.
