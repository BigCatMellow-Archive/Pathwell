## Audit Question 99 — Why is Pathwell considering buying the other family's handwritten cookbook?

### A. He is trying, in his old ledger-minded way, to replace Nana's lost cookbook for Elizabeth.

Pathwell still feels he owes Elizabeth and sees another old charged family cookbook as something he might be able to put back into the empty space. The impulse is not presented as noble restitution; it is a residue of his core flaw — `something was lost, therefore I should acquire an equivalent and fix the balance.` He has not asked Elizabeth whether she wants that. The difference from earlier Pathwell is that he does not force the transaction through, and when Elizabeth says `That isn't mine`, he can simply answer `No.` rather than arguing that the substitute is close enough or that he paid fairly.

### B. He wants the cookbook for himself as useful charged magical material.

This fits his scavenger habits, but makes Elizabeth's `That isn't mine.` oddly irrelevant: if Pathwell is buying it as his own resource, no one has claimed it is hers or a replacement for Nana's.

### C. He is considering it as an ordinary cookbook for practical/personal use.

This avoids another control beat, but disconnects the object from the central loss and gives the coda's `That isn't mine.` exchange little reason to exist.

### D. The shopkeeper deliberately puts the cookbook in front of Pathwell as a test.

This can explain the setup, but turns the custodian into a moral examiner and makes Pathwell's final growth depend on being staged/tested by someone else rather than arising from his own habitual impulse.

### Audit lean: **A**

A makes the cookbook beat do character work without pretending Pathwell is fully cured. His first instinct is still transactional replacement: a loss has created a debt, so he looks for an equivalent object and a price he can pay. What has changed is his response to uncertainty and to Elizabeth's boundary. He leaves the loss unresolved rather than insisting that a balanced ledger makes the substitute acceptable. B weakens the `That isn't mine` exchange; C makes the object dramatically arbitrary; D gives too much authorial/moral control to the shopkeeper.
