# Pathwell — Oral vs. Precise Register: When to Use Which

*Working notes for the editing pass. Not a general guide — specific to this manuscript.*

---

## What "Oral Narrator" Means Here

Pathwell is third-person limited (Elizabeth's POV). The narrator doesn't say "I." But the narrator has a voice — dry, slightly absurdist, slightly irreverent — and that voice is either present or absent in any given passage.

**Present narrator (oral mode):** The narration has opinions. It interprets what it describes. It notices the absurd angle. It sounds like someone is telling you the story and finds it slightly funny that this is what happened.

**Absent narrator (precise mode):** The narration is transparent. It shows what happens through Elizabeth's body and senses. It doesn't editorialize. It trusts the image to carry the weight.

Both are right. Neither is always right.

---

## The Key Test

**Oral mode:** Can you hear a slightly wry voice behind the sentence?

> "He noticed most things, unfortunately, and then ordered another round."

The "unfortunately" is the narrator leaning in. That's oral.

**Precise mode:** Is the sentence doing its work through image and sensation alone?

> "The draw went quiet. Not violently. The way a bell goes quiet when you put your hand on it."

No narrator here. The image carries everything. Adding narrator voice here would kill it.

---

## When to Use Oral Mode

### Character observation from outside
When the narrator is watching a character do something and the absurdity or precision of that action is the point. Pathwell especially. His behavior is always slightly wrong for the situation — the narrator noticing that is the joke.

> "He looked at Pathwell the way people look at a problem they are fond of."

> "She had the practiced patience of someone who had heard everything."

The narrator is interpreting, not just describing. These are oral.

### Establishing shots and setting descriptions
The bar, the camp, the town drive — places where the narrator sets the scene and has room to notice something slightly off or funny about the environment. These can carry the narrator's wry eye without breaking Elizabeth's POV, because she's observing too.

> "a bar that had been there long enough to stop trying"

> "lots that had given up pretending to be lawns"

Both are narrator interpretations of setting. The places are "feeling" something, which is the narrator speaking through the description.

### Transitions between scenes
The narrator can briefly editorialize at the seams. When the chapter is moving from one beat to the next, the narrator can offer a small interpretation of what just happened or what's about to happen.

### Comic register moments / the gap
The "gap" (serious situation + wrong response) always has the narrator present — the narrator is the one choosing to frame Pathwell's wrong detail as the thing worth noting. The narrator's choice of what to notice IS the irreverence.

---

## When to Use Precise Mode

### Elizabeth's interiority (body-first moments)
When Elizabeth experiences something through her body before her mind catches up, the narrator pulls back. Body-first interiority works by removing the interpretive voice. The reader IS the experience.

> "The pain went bright and then thin. Her arm settled back into itself with a sensation like a drawer closing correctly after months of sticking."

No narrator here. This is sensation. Adding the wry voice would distance us from the moment.

### Magic in operation
The benchmark is the sugar-cookie scene in Ch1. Magic is felt through sensation, synesthesia, sensory cascade. The narrator doesn't comment on this — it moves through Elizabeth. Any narrator editorial in a magic moment breaks the spell.

### Climactic emotional beats
The archive fire, the diary burning, Shade's death, the draw going quiet — these need precise mode. The narrator retreats and lets the images do the work. If the narrator is cracking wise at Shade's death, that's the wrong gap.

### Action sequences
The blob confrontation, the overreach at the museum, the tear — these need short declarative sentences and no narrator voice. The narrator's presence slows action. Get out of the way.

### Shade's passages
His register is eerie precision, not comic. The narrator being slightly wry about Shade doesn't work — he's too unsettling for that. When Shade is in the room, the prose is precise.

## The Slightly Irreverent Narrator Voice

What "slightly irreverent" means in this manuscript:

1. **Noticing the wrong detail.** The narrator chooses to tell you Pathwell is examining a wagon wheel, not grieving. The choice IS the comment.

2. **Interpreting behavior through comparison.** "The way people look at a problem they are fond of." "The serious focus of someone paying off a debt to themselves." These similes contain the narrator's wry read on the character.

3. **"Unfortunately."** Small editorial asides that break the third-person veil just slightly. Sparingly. One per scene at most.

4. **Treating the impossible as logistical.** The narrator describes magic, blobs, and centuries-old practitioners in the same register as parking lots and foam daggers. The irreverence is in the refusal to be impressed.

5. **Self-correction in description.** "Not clean — clean was a different aspiration." The narrator correcting its own first word is an oral move.

---

## What to Watch For

- **Narrator absent where it should be present:** Flat transitions that just move bodies from A to B without any voice. Pure stage direction where a small interpretive note would land.
- **Narrator present where it should be absent:** Editorializing inside Elizabeth's body-first moments. Any comic aside during a climactic beat.
- **The narrator going full oral (bad):** Self-correction every two sentences, "you know" constructions, memory-logic hedging in the middle of action. The narrator should feel *present*, not chatty.
- **Stansbury especially:** He's the straight man who delivers absurd lines by meaning them completely. The narrator can observe him with a slightly fond absurdism. His fish order is the model.

## Quick Map

| Scene type | Register |
|---|---|
| Chapter opening / establishing shot | Oral — narrator present, sets tone |
| Camp, bar, town descriptions | Oral — narrator notices the wrong/absurd angle |
| Pathwell doing something wrong | Oral — narrator chooses what to notice |
| Stansbury being precise | Oral — narrator amused by the precision |
| Elizabeth in motion (non-magical) | Lightly oral — narrator can interpret one beat |
| Transitions between scenes | Lightly oral — narrator can editorialize briefly |
| Elizabeth's body-first interiority | Precise — narrator out |
| Magic in operation | Precise — narrator out |
| Any climactic moment | Precise — narrator out |
| Shade on the page | Precise — narrator out |
| Action sequences | Precise — narrator out |
| The draw going quiet | Precise — this is sacred, do not touch |
