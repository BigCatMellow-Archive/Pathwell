# Natural Oral-Storytelling Prose Guide for AI Writing Agents

## Purpose

This guide teaches an AI writing agent how to write and review prose that feels like a person is telling a story from memory.

The goal is not to write in a clinical, perfectly grammatical, essay-like way. The goal is to create prose that feels natural, smooth, human, and spoken, while still being readable.

This style can be called:

- oral-storytelling prose
- spoken-memory narration
- controlled informal narration
- natural narrative voice

The core principle:

> Do not write exactly like people talk. Write like people think they talk when they are telling a good story.

Real speech is messy. It includes filler, dead ends, repeated starts, unfinished thoughts, and clutter. Good written speech removes most of that clutter but keeps the rhythm, emphasis, and emotional logic of speech.

---

# 1. Core Difference: Clinical Prose vs Spoken-Memory Prose

## 1.1 Clinical Prose

Clinical prose sounds formal, complete, and report-like.

Example:

> I entered the house and immediately noticed that the lights were off. The silence suggested that no one was home, but I still felt uneasy because the front door had been left unlocked.

This is clear, but it feels distant. It sounds like someone writing a statement after the fact.

Traits:

- grammatically complete
- orderly
- explanatory
- emotionally controlled
- efficient but sometimes flat
- focused on facts before feeling

---

## 1.2 Spoken-Memory Prose

Spoken-memory prose sounds like someone telling the story as they remember it.

Example:

> So I go in, right, and the first thing I notice is the lights are off. Not dim. Not low. Off. And the house is quiet in that weird way where you know it’s empty, but you still feel like something is listening. Then I see the front door wasn’t even locked.

This version feels more human because it includes:

- conversational entry
- fragments
- emphasis
- interpretation
- delayed realization
- emotional texture

Traits:

- sounds speakable
- uses contractions
- allows fragments
- follows memory order
- includes self-correction
- gives emotional interpretation
- uses specific remembered details
- feels shaped but not stiff

---

# 2. Main Rule: Write the Order of Remembering

Clinical writing usually follows this order:

```text
fact -> explanation -> conclusion
```

Spoken-memory prose often follows this order:

```text
hook -> detail -> correction -> feeling -> realization
```

## Example

Clinical/report order:

> The man was standing beside my car when I came outside, which made me nervous.

Spoken-memory order:

> I come outside, and there’s this guy standing by my car. Just standing there. Not doing anything, which somehow made it worse.

Why the second version works:

1. It starts with the action.
2. It lets the reader notice the man with the narrator.
3. It pauses on the weirdness.
4. It interprets the emotional effect.
5. It sounds like memory being reconstructed.

The key instruction for an AI agent:

> Do not only organize the facts logically. Organize the experience the way a person might remember it.

---

# 3. Use Fragments Purposefully

People do not always speak in complete sentences. They speak in chunks.

Fragments can create:

- emphasis
- hesitation
- realization
- emotional pressure
- pacing
- intimacy

## Effective fragment use

> And then the noise stopped. Just stopped. Like somebody had reached over and turned the whole world down.

Why it works:

- **Just stopped** repeats and sharpens the point.
- The fragment slows the moment.
- The final sentence gives the emotional comparison.

## Ineffective fragment use

> I went there. Very strange. The room. Cold. Dark. Weird.

Why it fails:

- too choppy
- too artificial
- no clear movement of thought
- sounds like style pasted onto the sentence

## Better version

> I went in, and the room was cold. Not “forgot to turn the heat on” cold. More like nobody had lived there for a long time.

Why it works:

- the fragment clarifies the kind of cold
- the comparison adds voice
- the rhythm feels conversational but controlled

Agent rule:

> Use fragments for emphasis, correction, reaction, or emotional pressure. Do not use fragments randomly.

---

# 4. Use Self-Correction

Memory is not perfectly linear. People adjust as they speak.

Self-correction makes prose feel human because the narrator appears to be searching for the right word or meaning.

Examples:

> It was late — no, not late, exactly. More like that dead part of the afternoon where everything feels used up.

> He looked angry at first. Actually, no. Not angry. Tired. Like he’d already had the argument in his head and lost.

Useful self-correction phrases:

- no, not exactly
- actually
- or maybe
- I guess
- that’s not the right word
- what I mean is
- not because... but because...
- maybe that sounds wrong, but
- I don’t know how else to say it

Warning:

> Use self-correction sparingly. Too much makes the narrator sound unsure, rambling, or artificially casual.

---

# 5. Use Conversational Connectors

Formal prose often avoids loose connectors. Spoken narration uses them naturally.

Useful connectors:

- so
- and
- but
- anyway
- then
- because
- which is the thing
- that’s when
- the weird part was
- here’s what I remember
- the thing is
- at first
- after that
- and then
- by the time

Example:

> So we’re sitting there, and nobody wants to say it, but everybody knows. That’s the thing I remember most. Not what he said. The silence before he said it.

Why it works:

- **So** opens like spoken narration.
- **but everybody knows** creates shared tension.
- **That’s the thing I remember most** gives memory logic.
- **Not what he said. The silence before he said it.** creates emphasis and rhythm.

Agent rule:

> Use conversational connectors to guide the listener through the memory. Do not overuse them as filler.

---

# 6. Let the Narrator Interpret Events

Clinical prose often reports what happened.

Spoken-memory prose reports what happened and what it felt like while it was happening.

## Flat version

> The room was messy.

## Spoken-memory version

> The room was a mess, but not normal mess. Not clothes-on-the-floor mess. More like someone had been looking for something and stopped caring what they broke.

Why it works:

- the narrator qualifies the detail
- the description becomes interpretive
- the reader learns how the narrator read the room
- the prose feels like someone making sense of the memory

Agent rule:

> Do not only describe objects or events. Explain why they felt strange, funny, frightening, sad, or important.

---

# 7. Use Emphasis Like Speech

People emphasize by repeating, contrasting, narrowing, and escalating.

## 7.1 Repetition

> He was calm. Too calm.

Effect:

- sharpens the observation
- adds suspicion
- feels spoken

## 7.2 Contrast

> It wasn’t loud. That was the problem. It was careful.

Effect:

- creates unease
- redirects the listener’s expectation

## 7.3 Narrowing

> I remember his hands. Not his face. His hands.

Effect:

- mimics selective memory
- focuses attention
- makes the detail feel important

## 7.4 Escalation

> First it was weird. Then it was uncomfortable. Then it was obvious we needed to leave.

Effect:

- creates progression
- builds tension
- feels like a person walking the listener through their realization

Agent rule:

> Use emphasis to reveal what mattered to the narrator, not just to decorate the prose.

---

# 8. Make Grammar Flexible, Not Careless

Natural prose can bend grammar rules, but it should remain clear.

## Good spoken looseness

> And maybe that sounds dramatic, but that’s how it felt.

This works because:

- it sounds natural
- the meaning is clear
- the informality supports the voice

## Bad looseness

> And like it was just the thing where I guess maybe he kind of was there but not in that way.

This fails because:

- too many filler words
- unclear meaning
- no clean rhythm
- sounds like unedited speech

Agent rule:

> Break grammar for rhythm, emphasis, or voice. Do not break grammar because the sentence is unfinished or unclear.

---

# 9. Use Memory Anchors

A memory anchor is a specific detail that makes a story feel remembered rather than invented or summarized.

Good memory anchors include:

- a smell
- a sound
- an object
- a repeated gesture
- a strange phrase someone said
- the weather
- a physical sensation
- a small detail that emotionally sticks
- the position of an object
- the quality of light
- a background noise

## Weak version

> The restaurant was uncomfortable.

## Stronger spoken-memory version

> I remember the table was sticky. That’s the part that stayed with me. Not what she said first. The table. My sleeve kept catching on it.

Why it works:

- specific physical detail
- selective memory
- emotional discomfort shown through the body
- sounds like someone remembering what actually stuck

Another example:

> I don’t remember what time it was. I remember the vending machine humming. That stupid bright blue light in the corner.

Why it works:

- admits memory limits
- focuses on a strange sensory detail
- gives the scene texture

Agent rule:

> Prefer concrete remembered details over abstract summary.

---

# 10. Use Delayed Explanation

People often tell stories by making the listener wait for the realization.

## Too direct

> I was scared because I realized someone had been inside the house.

## Better spoken-memory version

> At first I thought I was being stupid. You know, old house, bad wiring, whatever. But then I saw the chair. It had been moved. Not a lot. Just enough.

Why it works:

- starts with doubt
- gives ordinary explanations first
- introduces one specific detail
- delays the conclusion
- lets the reader experience the realization

Useful structure:

```text
setup -> small detail -> doubt -> second detail -> realization
```

Agent rule:

> Do not always explain the meaning immediately. Let the reader arrive at the realization with the narrator.

---

# 11. Control Filler Words

A small amount of filler creates voice. Too much creates noise.

Useful filler in small amounts:

- like
- you know
- I mean
- sort of
- kind of
- right
- anyway
- whatever
- I guess

## Effective use

> I mean, I knew he was lying. I just didn’t know why yet.

Why it works:

- **I mean** creates a spoken turn
- the sentence is still clear
- the emotional point is direct

## Ineffective use

> Like, I mean, I kind of knew, you know, that he was sort of lying or whatever.

Why it fails:

- bloated
- weak
- evasive
- rhythm becomes mushy

Agent rule:

> Use filler for voice, hesitation, or attitude. Remove filler that weakens meaning.

---

# 12. Dialogue vs Spoken Narration

This style can apply to both dialogue and narration, but they are not the same.

## 12.1 Dialogue

Dialogue is what characters say out loud.

Example:

> “I thought you left,” she said.  
> “I did.”  
> “Then why are you still here?”

Dialogue usually needs to be even more compressed than real speech. It should sound spoken, but it should still move the scene.

## 12.2 Spoken Narration

Spoken narration is the storyteller’s voice outside quotation marks.

Example:

> I thought he left. That was the whole point. He was supposed to be gone. But when I came downstairs, there he was, sitting at the kitchen table like he’d been waiting all night.

This is not dialogue, but it has conversational rhythm.

Agent rule:

> Dialogue is spoken by characters. Spoken narration is the story voice sounding conversational.

# 13. The Spoken-Memory Formula

Use this structure when writing or revising in this style:

```text
1. Start with the remembered moment.
2. Add one concrete detail.
3. Correct or qualify the memory.
4. Give the emotional meaning.
5. Move to the next action.
6. Delay the explanation until the reader wants it.
7. Land on the strongest image or realization.
```

## Example

> I remember the hallway first. That’s strange, probably, because nothing happened there. But it was so quiet. The kind of quiet where your own breathing starts to feel rude. I stood there with my hand on the wall, listening, and that’s when I heard the floorboard upstairs.

Why it works:

- begins with memory
- admits uncertainty
- focuses on quiet
- gives bodily experience
- delays the reveal
- ends on a concrete sound

---

# 14. Before and After Example

## Clinical Version

> When I arrived at my father’s apartment, I noticed several unusual things. The door was unlocked, the television was on, and there was a glass of water on the floor. These details made me suspect something was wrong.

## Spoken-Memory Version

> When I got to my dad’s apartment, the door was unlocked. That was the first thing. Not open. Not broken. Just unlocked, which somehow felt worse.
>
> Then I heard the TV going inside. Some game show, I think. People clapping at nothing.
>
> And there was a glass of water on the floor by the couch. Full glass. Just sitting there.
>
> That’s when I knew something was wrong.

## Why the revision works

- **That was the first thing** sounds like memory organizing itself.
- **Not open. Not broken.** creates spoken emphasis.
- **Some game show, I think** adds human uncertainty.
- **People clapping at nothing** gives the narrator’s perception.
- **Full glass. Just sitting there.** slows the reader down.
- **That’s when I knew something was wrong** gives the emotional landing.

# 15. Measuring Naturalness

Use this checklist to decide whether prose feels naturally spoken.

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Read-aloud test | Can someone say this without sounding like they are reading an essay? |
| Memory-order test | Does the passage follow how someone might remember it, not just how a report would organize it? |
| Filler test | Are filler words used for voice, not clutter? |
| Fragment test | Do fragments create emphasis rather than confusion? |
| Detail test | Are there specific remembered details? |
| Emotion test | Does the narrator interpret why the detail mattered? |
| Compression test | Is the speech cleaned up enough to be readable? |
| Landing test | Does the paragraph end on a strong image, realization, or turn? |

# 16. AI-Agent Review Rubric

Score each category from 1 to 5.

| Score | Meaning |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Serious problem; style feels unnatural or unreadable |
| 2 | Noticeable weakness; needs revision |
| 3 | Functional; acceptable but not strongly voiced |
| 4 | Strong; natural and controlled |
| 5 | Excellent; feels human, smooth, and intentionally shaped |

## 16.1 Natural Voice

Question:

> Does the prose sound speakable?

| Score | Description |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Sounds stiff, fake, or unreadable aloud |
| 2 | Some conversational elements, but mostly artificial |
| 3 | Speakable but plain |
| 4 | Clearly natural and human-sounding |
| 5 | Strong spoken voice with excellent control |

| Score | Description |

|---:|---|

# 18. Writing Instructions for AI Agents

Use these instructions when asking an AI agent to write in this style.

```text
Write in a natural oral-storytelling style.

Do not make the prose clinical, overly grammatical, or essay-like.

Use contractions, sentence fragments, conversational transitions, self-corrections, and selective repetition where they improve voice.

Make the narration feel like someone is remembering the event while telling it.

Prefer concrete remembered details over abstract summary.

Let the narrator interpret what details felt like, not just what happened.

Use delayed explanation when it would create stronger memory logic or tension.

Avoid excessive filler such as “like,” “you know,” “kind of,” and “I mean.”

Do not write a literal transcript of speech. Cut dead air, circular phrasing, and meaningless repetition.

The goal is controlled informality: readable prose that feels spoken.
```

# 19. Common Problems and Fixes

## Problem: Too clinical

Clinical:

> I realized that the situation was dangerous because the door had been left open.

More natural:

> The door was open. That was when I knew this wasn’t just weird anymore.

Why:

- starts with the concrete detail
- lets the realization follow
- sounds more like remembered experience

---

## Problem: Too much filler

Overfilled:

> Like, I mean, I kind of knew something was wrong, you know?

Cleaner:

> I knew something was wrong. I just didn’t know what yet.

Why:

- removes filler
- keeps uncertainty
- lands more strongly

---

## Problem: Fake casualness

Fake casual:

> So yeah, basically, it was super weird and stuff.

Better:

> It was weird, but not in a way I could explain yet.

Why:

- more precise
- still natural
- avoids empty casual phrasing

---

## Problem: Too much summary

Summary:

> The house seemed strange and unsettling.

Better:

> The house was too quiet. Even the fridge had stopped humming.

Why:

- gives a sensory detail
- lets the reader feel the strangeness

---

## Problem: No emotional interpretation

Flat:

> He smiled at me.

Better:

> He smiled at me, but it was the kind of smile people use when they already know how the conversation ends.

Why:

- interprets the smile
- gives emotional meaning
- deepens the narrator’s perception

---

## Problem: Weak landing

Weak:

> I felt uncomfortable because of everything that had happened.

Stronger:

> After that, I couldn’t make myself sit down.

Why:

- shows the discomfort physically
- ends on an action
- feels less abstract

---

# 20. Final Principle

The best question is not:

> Is this grammatically perfect?

The better question is:

> Would a real person say something close to this while telling the story well?

The answer does not need to be exact. It needs to feel close.

Natural prose is not raw speech. It is speech shaped into story.
