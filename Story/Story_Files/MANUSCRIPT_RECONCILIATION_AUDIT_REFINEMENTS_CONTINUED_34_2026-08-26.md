## Audit Question 97 — Who should actually take ownership of the next errand before the final exchange?

### A. The shopkeeper presents the card/alternative price without assigning it to Pathwell; Elizabeth takes the card and chooses the errand.

The shopkeeper can still explain that completing the job earns the handwritten cookbook, but he does not frame Pathwell as the already-committed actor. Elizabeth reads the card, makes the choice to take it/claim the errand, and then turns to Pathwell: `Are you ready?` Pathwell's `No.` becomes a genuine response to **her** initiative.

### B. The shopkeeper offers the errand to both of them, and Elizabeth is the first to accept.

This preserves a joint-adventure frame while still giving Elizabeth priority. It is slightly less sharp as a role reversal because the external invitation is explicitly collective.

### C. The shopkeeper still offers the errand to Pathwell, but Pathwell does not accept; Elizabeth takes the card from the counter and decides they are going.

This makes the reversal very legible, but risks becoming slightly performative if Elizabeth effectively appropriates a bargain whose reward is a book Pathwell was considering.

### D. Keep the current staging: Pathwell pockets the card first, then Elizabeth asks `Are you ready?`

This preserves the manuscript but weakens the stated endpoint that Elizabeth initiates what comes next; Pathwell has already chosen the quest before she speaks.

### Audit lean: **A**

A preserves the shopkeeper as the source of the external opportunity while making Elizabeth the source of the **decision to go**. She does not need magical authority, a special destiny, or ownership of the reward; she simply chooses the next direction and asks whether Pathwell is coming. B works but diffuses the reversal. C is stronger visually but can make the bargain ownership awkward. D leaves the final exchange rhetorically reversed rather than structurally reversed.
