## Existing-story check: the foam/toy form is character-significant, but its practical safety meaning is undefined

Current `character_bible.md` explicitly defines Stansbury's Nerf/foam weapons as part of his contradiction: he wants to arm people, but gives them toys instead of real weapons. This is tied to his over-protective/control flaw — he equips people while still trying to limit the danger/risk they can take on.

Current `Chapter_05.txt` supports that presentation: the weapons look like ordinary toy-aisle foam swords/daggers, but Stansbury insists `These are not toys` and demonstrates a magical effect by striking a tree. Q106 establishes that a non-practitioner can trigger the finished tool's prepared effect through ordinary physical use. Q122-Q124 now further establish that these are durable, reusable products sold through an informed magic-aware network.

What remains undefined is whether the foam construction itself mechanically limits harm, whether Stansbury's chosen imbued effects are merely designed to be defensive/nonlethal, or whether the foam appearance is mostly symbolic/camouflage while the magic can make the tool as dangerous as any conventional weapon.

A repo search found no later support defining a universal safety rule for foam construction or Stansbury's normal effect design.

This matters because the character-bible contradiction works best if `giving people toys` reflects a real attempt to constrain risk rather than only an aesthetic choice. At the same time, making foam a universal magical safety material would over-systematize the world and could conflict with the dagger's ability to cut a blob.

## Audit Question 125 — What practical safety limit should Stansbury's foam weapon design represent?

### A. Foam construction itself imposes a broad magical safety cap: the tools cannot become conventionally lethal against ordinary people even when imbued.

This makes the `toy instead of real weapon` contradiction mechanically literal. However, it adds a broad material rule to imbuing and raises questions about why foam is uniquely capable of limiting magic.

### B. The foam form is mostly symbolic/camouflage; once imbued, the tool can be as dangerous as a conventional weapon depending on the effect.

This preserves maximum magical flexibility, but weakens the character-bible idea that Stansbury is actually limiting the risks he lets other people take. He would be giving them toys only visually.

### C. No universal foam-safety law; Stansbury deliberately designs his normal inventory around narrow defensive/disruptive effects, while the mundane foam body remains physically toy-like.

The foam itself does not magically guarantee safety. Instead, Stansbury's caution appears in what he chooses to imbue into it: effects intended to repel, disrupt, contain, expose, stagger, cut through anomalous magical material, or otherwise protect without simply reproducing a lethal steel weapon. Individual effects can still be dangerous, and unusual/custom tools could differ, but his standard line is deliberately safety-biased.

This preserves the dagger's ability to cut the blob without claiming that foam has a universal anti-harm property. It also makes Stansbury's flaw a design philosophy rather than a new fundamental law of magic.

### D. Leave the safety distinction undefined; the story only needs the weapons to work when used.

This preserves iceberg lore but leaves the character-bible `gives them toys instead of real weapons` contradiction partly cosmetic now that the weapons are established as a real business/product line.

### Audit lean: **C**

C keeps the existing character logic without inventing a universal foam rule. Stansbury's control/caution shows up in the effects he is willing to sell and hand people: he gives them functioning tools, but deliberately biases those tools toward protection and disruption rather than straightforward lethality. A over-systematizes the material; B makes the toy choice mostly aesthetic; D leaves an increasingly visible gap now that the weapons have become recurring world infrastructure.

## Q125 — Stansbury's standard foam inventory is safety-biased by design, not by a universal foam rule — LOCKED C

- Foam itself does **not** impose a universal magical safety cap.
- Stansbury deliberately designs his ordinary inventory around defensive, disruptive, containing, exposing, staggering, or otherwise risk-limiting effects rather than simply reproducing conventional lethal weapons.
- The mundane foam body remains physically toy-like; the practical safety bias comes from Stansbury's chosen imbuements and product philosophy.
- Individual effects can still be dangerous, and unusual/custom tools may differ when the story needs them.
- Elizabeth's foam dagger can therefore cut anomalous blob material without establishing that foam is universally harmless or universally anti-lethal.
- This makes the `gives them toys instead of real weapons` contradiction a manifestation of Stansbury's over-protective control rather than a fundamental law of magic.
