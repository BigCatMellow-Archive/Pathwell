## Existing-story check: turning the imbued weapons into merchandise makes the customer base matter

Q106 already locks that a finished pre-imbued weapon can be wielded non-magically: Stansbury performs the magical preparation in advance, and the finished tool's narrow effect can trigger through ordinary physical use. The wielder does not need to be a practitioner.

Q122 now adds that Stansbury routinely maintains these weapons as inventory because making/supplying them is a source of income.

Current world rules also say magic is secret but visible. There is no magical veil preventing ordinary people from seeing supernatural effects.

That combination creates a distribution question. If a foam sword that can produce a genuine magical effect is sold as an ordinary toy to an unsuspecting member of the public, the customer does not need magical training to activate it. General retail would therefore create repeated safety and secrecy problems.

A repo search found no existing lock defining Stansbury's customers, sales network, or whether buyers know what the tools actually are.

## Audit Question 123 — Who should normally buy Stansbury's pre-imbued defensive toys?

### A. A quiet practitioner/supernatural-aware network whose buyers know exactly what the tools are.

Stansbury keeps inventory and sells through people/communities already aware of magic: practitioners, Camp-adjacent people, supernatural households, or others who understand the risks. The exact sales channel can remain off-page.

This preserves the income source without requiring magic to be openly retailed to the general public. It also fits Q106: a buyer does not need to be a practitioner to wield the tool, but they should knowingly belong to a context where its magical function is understood.

### B. Mostly ordinary members of the public who think they are buying novelty/toy weapons.

This could make Stansbury's business larger and stranger, but because non-practitioners can activate the tools it creates obvious accidental-use, injury, and secrecy problems.

### C. Primarily Camp Cunnan and a small number of known practitioners/communities.

This is tighter than A and makes his business heavily relationship-based. It reduces exposure risk, but may make `source of income` feel relatively narrow unless these clients buy often enough.

### D. Mostly custom commissions to known clients, with very little standing inventory.

This minimizes distribution risk but conflicts with the newly locked reason that a ready box of assorted weapons is normal working inventory rather than a special order.

### Audit lean: **A**

A keeps the income model broad enough to support standing inventory while respecting the world's secrecy and the fact that finished tools can be activated by non-practitioners. The critical distinction is not `practitioner versus non-practitioner`; it is **informed magical customer versus unsuspecting public buyer**. C is also viable but makes the commercial network considerably smaller. B creates unnecessary safety/secrecy problems. D weakens Q122's inventory logic.
