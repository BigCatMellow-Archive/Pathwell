## What *is* consistently visible

The narrower recurring pattern is **resigned accommodation of Pathwell as a fixed condition**.

Stansbury:

- sees Pathwell's behavior clearly;
- has centuries of evidence that the same pattern repeats;
- warns other people about it;
- contradicts Pathwell tactically;
- limits immediate damage when he can;
- helps people after Pathwell's interventions go wrong;
- often helps Pathwell himself recover afterward;
- uses sarcasm/familiarity as if `Pathwell being Pathwell` is a condition to manage rather than a relationship in which he still expects meaningful change.

This is not the same as endorsing Pathwell, obeying him, or refusing to act. `Enabling` may also be too morally simple if it implies Stansbury approves of the behavior. **Resignation / normalization / accommodation** is the more evidence-based concept.

A useful description of their long-running cycle is:

1. Pathwell appoints himself fixer and acts with more certainty than his evidence or authority justifies.
2. Stansbury recognizes the pattern and frequently says so.
3. Stansbury mitigates the immediate fallout and/or helps Pathwell recover.
4. The brothers continue onward with the larger relational pattern basically intact.

This lets Stansbury be genuinely competent and frequently correct without turning that correctness into proof that he has solved his own relationship to Pathwell.

## Audit Question 128 — How much independent character arc does Stansbury actually need?

### A. Full arc built around resigned accommodation of Pathwell

Make Stansbury's primary flaw that centuries of managing around Pathwell have made him stop expecting meaningful change. He still acts and argues, but he has normalized the cycle: Pathwell intervenes, Stansbury diagnoses/mitigates, and they continue.

The climax would need to mark a clear break in that pattern, likely through Stansbury setting a meaningful boundary and refusing to resume the old fixer/cleanup relationship.

**Tradeoff:** gives Stansbury a substantial independent arc, but requires additional setup and risks again making many existing scenes serve a thematic mechanism they were not originally written to carry.

### B. Smaller brother-specific turn, not a full flaw-resolution arc

Keep Stansbury as competent, cautious, and already willing to act. His weakness is lighter: he has become resigned to Pathwell and too accustomed to managing around him.

The climax does not have to `cure` a major Stansbury flaw. His movement is relational and specific: the fire produces a consequence that belongs to Stansbury, and with `Let it be` he establishes a boundary against Pathwell's reflex to make that consequence disappear. Pathwell respecting it completes the beat from both sides.

His archive rescue remains his own meaningful choice, but not a test he had to pass to prove he is no longer passive.

**Tradeoff:** less symmetrical than giving every major character a full arc, but much closer to the Stansbury already on the page and requires less retrofitted behavior.

### C. Stansbury is essentially a static foil/supporting character

Do not assign him a meaningful arc. He is consistently the cautious, perceptive brother who sees Pathwell clearly. The archive rescue and scar matter primarily because they affect Pathwell and make the catastrophe concrete.

`Let it be` remains a boundary, but not the culmination of a Stansbury change.

**Tradeoff:** cleanest structurally, but risks leaving Stansbury feeling functional rather than fully inhabited, especially given how much page space and family history he carries.

### D. Restore a full `keeps people small → trust/leadership` arc

Return to the earlier structural model and add the necessary pre-fire controlling behavior so Q116 pays it off.

**Tradeoff:** restores symmetry but reintroduces the exact character behavior the consistency pass found weakly supported and that the user already questioned.

### Audit lean: **B**

B preserves what is already working without forcing Stansbury to demonstrate a broad defect he does not consistently have. He can still change, but the change is **specific to his relationship with Pathwell** rather than a general lesson about courage, responsibility, or leadership.

Most importantly, it lets `Let it be` do the work it already naturally does: Stansbury stops treating Pathwell's fixing impulse as an inevitable condition to manage around and gives him a boundary. Pathwell, for once, leaves it alone.
