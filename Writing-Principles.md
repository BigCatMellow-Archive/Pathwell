# Writing Principles

**Pathwell-specific craft guidance. These are working principles, not universal laws. Use them to diagnose the story; do not force the story to obey a maxim when the actual scene, character, or reader effect points elsewhere.**

---

### 1. Write the Sequel
The sequel assumes you were there. Characters do not explain gravity and they should not explain magic merely for the reader. Let people speak from lived familiarity. Explain only what the current character, relationship, or situation would naturally make visible.

### 2. Therefore, But — Avoid And Then
Scenes should connect through consequence, complication, decision, or relationship pressure.

- **Therefore**: the prior scene causes or makes necessary what follows.
- **But**: the prior scene creates a new obstacle, cost, contradiction, or demand.
- **And then** is a warning sign when nothing in the previous scene makes the next scene happen.

Not every transition must be mechanical plot causality. Character choices, social consequences, knowledge changes, and relationship shifts also count.

### 3. Every Scene Must Change Story State or Make Later Change Matter
A scene does not need a dramatic positive/negative value flip to justify itself.

It does need to do at least one of these:

- cause something;
- change a relationship, knowledge state, practical condition, or expectation;
- make a later choice more believable;
- make a later loss or rupture hurt more;
- create necessary contrast or recovery after a major event.

If the story is materially unchanged when the scene disappears, cut or combine it.

### 4. Show the Iceberg's Tip
Know more than the reader needs.

World depth should come from characters treating their world as lived reality, not from deliberate mystery decoration. A name, scar, procedure, debt, or old place can remain unexplained because no one present needs to explain it.

Do not plant unexplained material merely to prove the world is deep.

### 5. The Body May Know Before the Mind
Embodiment is one useful route into experience, not a compulsory beat.

A body may react before conscious understanding, but people can also:

- suppress;
- mislabel;
- fail to notice;
- understand intellectually before displaying emotion;
- perform an emotion socially;
- react through habit or expertise rather than visceral sensation.

Use the response that fits the person and moment.

### 6. Characters Want Things — Often More Than One
Every important participant in a scene should have some pressure, preference, goal, need, fear, or obligation shaping what they do.

Those wants can:

- conflict with one another;
- remain partly unconscious;
- change during the scene;
- be social rather than practical;
- involve avoiding shame, preserving status, being useful, staying included, or refusing vulnerability.

Do not reduce characters to one declared objective.

### 7. Agency Is a Ladder
Elizabeth's arc is **self-direction**, not magical empowerment.

The climb should remain visible:

> adaptation -> preference -> voluntary continuation -> refusal -> deliberate action -> ownership of consequence -> self-direction

Large choices need smaller prior choices. Agency does not guarantee correct outcomes.

### 8. Pressure the Character's Self-Story
Do not force every major character into one neat "Lie" that the story disproves.

Instead ask:

- What story does this person tell themselves about who they are?
- What behavior keeps that story functioning?
- What real evidence supports it?
- What does the story make increasingly difficult to ignore?

For Pathwell, usefulness is genuinely real. The problem is the pattern by which usefulness becomes self-appointment and self-appointment becomes assumed decision authority.

### 9. Do Not Explain the Metaphor Before Performing It
If image, ritual, action, consequence, or recurrence already carries the meaning, do not pre-translate it for the reader.

Let the reader experience first and infer second.

### 10. Incomplete Answers Need a Human Reason
Partial answers, evasion, contradiction, and silence can build tension and worlds.

They should arise because:

- the character does not know;
- the character refuses;
- the character is protecting status, face, or another person;
- the character misunderstands;
- the character has another goal in the exchange;
- the information should not yet be available from this viewpoint.

Do not make evasiveness the default merely to manufacture mystery.

### 11. Breathe When the Story Needs Possession
Fast scenes often need quieter space afterward, but not by quota.

Breathing room earns its place when it lets the reader:

- absorb consequence;
- live inside a relationship;
- experience ordinary life before loss;
- infer meaning;
- feel contrast;
- enjoy the characters;
- notice a changed state.

A quiet scene that changes nothing and earns nothing still fails.

### 12. Make Place Functionally Specific
A location should matter through what people can do there, notice there, know there, or lose there.

Specificity comes especially from:

- procedure;
- expertise;
- material constraints;
- routine;
- history;
- who belongs;
- who controls access;
- what this place makes possible.

Do not force every room or landscape to behave like a symbolic character.

### 13. Subtext Must Be Recoverable
Subtext is not simply "the sentence the character refuses to say."

Ask:

- what is said;
- what is meant;
- what can reasonably be inferred;
- what social action the line performs;
- what each person knows;
- what each person wants;
- what relationship cost or status pressure is present.

Sometimes direct speech is the strongest move. The Ask works because Elizabeth strips away Pathwell's framing and forces plain language.

### 14. Promise, Progress, Payoff
The opening promises an experience, not merely plot facts.

The middle should keep changing or deepening what the reader is waiting for. The ending should fulfill, transform, or deliberately leave open the important promises.

Track both structural payoff and reader recognition.

### 15. Characters Are More Than Their Structural Function
Do not reduce Shade to villain, mirror, warning, symbol, or Pathwell's hypothetical future.

Do not reduce Elizabeth to agency lesson.

Do not reduce Stansbury to corrective foil.

A character may create thematic resonance, but they must also have motives, habits, relationships, blind spots, and continuity that exceed what the theme needs from them.

For Shade:

> **Origin explains Shade. Origin does not own Shade.**

### 16. Theme Through Action, Not Speeches
Theme emerges from patterns of choice, consequence, relationship, tradeoff, and recurrence.

Author-side questions such as **Whose decision is this?** are planning tools.

They should rarely become dialogue.

### 17. Rules Are Sacred Once Established
A magical resolution must follow from established mechanics.

Do not promote a climax solution because it elegantly matches the theme.

If the mechanism is unknown, leave it unknown until the rules support it.

Current application: Shade survival remains blocked until pruning/cleanup mechanics honestly explain it.

### 18. Let the World Exceed the Scene
The world should feel larger than the current plot because characters possess histories, procedures, relationships, places, and knowledge not all of which need explanation.

There is **no quota** for unexplained references.

One unexplained detail can help. Ten can become noise. Zero can be correct.

### 19. Earn the Big Moments
Major turns should be the payoff of prior lived states.

Ask before any rupture, sacrifice, reveal, reconciliation, or loss:

> What has the reader actually experienced that makes this matter?

If the big moment fails, first inspect the prior state rather than making the big moment louder.

### 20. Read It Aloud
Sound is one diagnostic for Pathwell's prose.

Read for:

- cadence;
- character voice;
- comic timing;
- awkward exposition;
- repeated sentence shape;
- dead rhythm;
- dialogue that no human mouth would naturally carry.

A sentence sounding good is not proof that it belongs. Story, POV, and character still outrank prettiness.

## Universal Chapter Necessity Test

**Required for every chapter and substantial new scene.**

A chapter should not survive merely because it contains good prose, useful lore, a fun set piece, or material we are fond of.

Before drafting, preserving, expanding, or approving a chapter, answer all six questions.

### 1. What later moment is this chapter helping earn?
Name the later choice, sacrifice, revelation, relationship turn, location loss, payoff, or emotional result that would be weaker without it.

### 2. What exactly is missing without it?
Be specific:

- attachment;
- a smaller self-direction rung;
- familiarity before loss;
- relationship time before rupture;
- setup for a rule/object/payoff;
- necessary contrast;
- evidence that makes a later choice believable.

Do not answer only `more development`, `more worldbuilding`, or `the book feels short`.

### 3. Why must this chapter happen here?
What **therefore** or **but** brings us into it?

What changes here that forces, complicates, or makes possible what comes next?

If the connection is only `and then`, test whether the chapter is misplaced, unnecessary, or missing a stronger character pressure.

### 4. Can it perform multiple necessary jobs naturally?
Strong scenes often carry several required functions at once:

- attachment;
- self-direction;
- relationship change;
- setup/payoff;
- world texture;
- object continuity;
- magic calibration;
- humor;
- tonal contrast;
- preparation for loss.

Do not cram unrelated obligations together merely to make a scene efficient.

### 5. Deletion test: is the story genuinely lesser without it?
Complete:

> **If this scene is removed, ______ becomes materially weaker or stops making sense.**

If the blank cannot be filled specifically, cut or redesign the scene.

If two scenes fill the blank with essentially the same answer, combine them.

### 6. Before taking something away, what has the reader been allowed to have?
For every person, place, relationship, object, identity, hope, or possibility that will later be lost, damaged, surrendered, or transformed, identify the reader's **lived memory of it beforehand**.

Do not rely on labels such as `beloved`, `home`, `friend`, `important`, or `irreplaceable`.

A loss should hurt because the reader remembers **having**, not because narration explains absence.

### Governing expansion rule

> **Never add material because the manuscript is short. Add material only because the existing story asks the reader to feel, believe, understand, or mourn something it has not yet earned.**

Before adding a new scene, first ask whether an already-necessary scene can carry the missing function.

### Memory-before-loss rule

> **Before we destroy something important, let it be ordinary for a while.**

## Pathwell-Specific Human and Relationship Diagnostics

### Distinguish kinds of power
Before a major Pathwell intervention, identify:

- expertise;
- information advantage;
- resource control;
- prestige/status;
- dependence;
- delegated authority;
- ability to impose;
- causal responsibility;
- actual decision ownership.

Then ask:

> **Which kind does Pathwell genuinely possess, and which additional kind is he assuming?**

Do not put this vocabulary into manuscript dialogue.

### Build difficult choices from conflicting goods
Late-story alternatives should not reduce to good option versus bad option.

For each serious option ask:

- what legitimate good does it protect?
- who pays?
- what legitimate good does it risk or sacrifice?
- who chooses?
- who did not choose?
- what new consequence does it create?

### Track reader state separately from story truth
For major mystery/reveal beats distinguish:

- what is true;
- what Elizabeth knows;
- what Pathwell knows;
- what Shade knows;
- what the reader currently suspects/believes;
- what question the reader is carrying;
- what later cue changes that model.

### Humor is social action
Humor can establish or change:

- affiliation;
- permission;
- status;
- intimacy;
- avoidance;
- embarrassment;
- rivalry;
- repair.

For Elizabeth and Pathwell, reciprocal play can demonstrate relationship change more efficiently than explicit trust dialogue.

## Additional Learned Principles

- **Scene connections come from character pressure, not plot logistics.**
- **Important objects leave characters' hands through pressure, not convenience.**
- **A self-direction rung must contain a choice, not merely competent reaction.**
- **When in doubt, ask what this costs.**
- **Do not build plot devices to perform a character's emotional work.**
- **Respect what is already on the page; do not undo a real choice merely to reuse an object or beat.**
- **Excitement, curiosity, and comfort are feelings, not decisions.**
- **Comedy needs a real serious side, not a mandatory waiting period.** The situation must be emotionally real enough for the wrong-register response to create the Pathwell gap. Humor and pain may coexist.
- **Do not solve a theme with a rule-breaking mechanism.**
- **Do not add a second proof when a later active scene can simply assume the first proof succeeded.**
