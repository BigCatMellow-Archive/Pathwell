# Writing Principles

**The 20 guiding principles from the Pathwell Story Architecture. Pin this up. Read it before you write. Read it after you write.**

---

### 1. Write the Sequel
The sequel assumes you were there. Characters don't explain gravity and they shouldn't explain magic. The reader catches up by watching, not by being taught. If someone who lives here wouldn't say it, notice it, or explain it — cut it.

### 2. Therefore, But — Never And Then
Every scene must cause the next. "Therefore" means Scene A forces Scene B. "But" means Scene A is complicated by a new problem. If the connection is "and then," one scene is in the wrong place or doesn't belong.

### 3. Every Scene Turns a Value
Something is at stake — safety, trust, freedom, knowledge, love. By the bottom, that value must have changed charge. If the emotional state at the end matches the beginning, nothing happened.

### 4. Show the Iceberg's Tip
Build everything. Show one-eighth. A locked door they never open. A name dropped and never explained. A scar nobody mentions. The world feels deep because you clearly *know* everything and chose to show almost none of it.

### 5. The Body Knows First
Before a character understands something intellectually, their body should react. Goosebumps in the fear section. Warmth in the love section. Sensation before explanation. Always.

### 6. Characters Want Things
Every character in every scene wants something. Even a glass of water. When two characters want conflicting things, you have tension. If a character is just *present* — watching, following, listening — give them a want or get them off the page.

### 7. Agency Is a Ladder
A passive character can become active, but the climb must be visible. Small choice → slightly bigger → consequences → changes the story. No skipping rungs. See [[Agency Ladder]].

### 8. The Lie and the Truth
Every major character believes something false at the start. The story tests that belief. The climax breaks or confirms it. Every scene should pressure someone's Lie.

### 9. Don't Explain the Metaphor Before Performing It
If a ritual is about to show a tree losing its branches, don't have a character explain what branches represent first. The demonstration IS the explanation. Trust the image. Trust the reader.

### 10. Incomplete Answers Build Worlds
When Elizabeth asks a question, the default answer should be partial, evasive, contradictory, or silence. Complete answers close doors. Incomplete answers open them. "You shouldn't have been able to do that" is more compelling than a full explanation.

### 11. Breathe Between Punches
Fast scenes need slow scenes after them. Action needs reflection. The reader needs time to absorb what happened before you hit them again. This is where they fall in love with your characters — in the quiet, not the chaos.

### 12. Locations Are Characters
A place that could be swapped for any other place isn't earning its page space. The magic itself should shape the environment. If a conversation could happen anywhere, the location isn't working. See [[Locations]].

### 13. Subtext Over Text
What characters don't say matters more than what they do. If both speakers say exactly what they mean, the dialogue is flat. Give every character a secret agenda — something they want but won't ask for directly. The gap between said and meant is where tension lives.

### 14. Promise, Progress, Payoff
The opening promises the reader an experience. The middle must feel like satisfying progress toward that promise. The ending pays it off. If the middle sags, the reader has lost track of what was promised. Remind them.

### 15. The Antagonist Is a Mirror
The best villain is a dark reflection. [[Shade]] isn't just a bad guy — he's [[Pathwell (Character)|Pathwell]] if Pathwell doesn't stop. The antagonist should represent the protagonist's worst possible future. When [[Elizabeth]] looks at Shade, she should see what the world does to people who stop choosing.

### 16. Theme Through Action, Not Speeches
If a character says the theme out loud, you've failed. Theme emerges from the pattern of choices and consequences. Let characters disagree through behavior. The reader discovers the theme.

### 17. Rules Are Sacred
Once the [[Magic System]] establishes a rule, never break it. Handwritten only. Pages consumed. Source emotion transfers. [[Pruning]] costs futures. Every violation trains the reader to stop trusting you.

### 18. One Unexplained Thing Per Chapter
Every chapter should contain at least one reference to something beyond the immediate story that never gets explained. A name. A place. A scar. A locked room. These details cost one sentence each and they imply centuries.

### 19. Earn the Big Moments
The car crash works only if Elizabeth has been making smaller choices first. Big moments are the payoff of small investments made chapters ago. If a moment isn't landing, the problem is almost never the moment itself — it's what you failed to set up.

### 20. Read It Aloud
If a sentence sounds wrong in your mouth, it reads wrong on the page. Prose is music. Short sentences punch. Long sentences flow. Monotony kills either way. Your ear knows before your brain does.

## Additional Learned Principles

These emerged through revision work and supplement the 20 above:

- **Scene connections come from character wants, not plot logistics.** When a connection feels weak, don't add mechanics — ask what the character wants.
- **Important objects leave characters' hands through pressure, not logistics.**
- **Every rung must be a choice, not a reaction.** Responding well to pressure is growth, but initiating action is real agency.
- **When in doubt, ask: what does this cost?** If a choice costs nothing, it's not a rung.
- **Don't build plot devices to do the character's emotional work.** No magic portals revealing true desires. No fortune tellers.
- **Respect what's already on the page.** Don't undo a character moment to reuse an object or beat.
- **Excitement, curiosity, and comfort are not choices.** Feelings are fuel, not engines.
- **Comedy requires emotional breathing room.** Trauma must be processed before punchlines can land.
